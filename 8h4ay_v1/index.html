Reporting p values imprecisely (e.g., p < .05) reduces transparency, reproducibility, and re-use (e.g., in p value meta-analyses). Best practice is to report exact p-values with three decimal places (e.g., p = .032) unless p values are smaller than 0.001, in which case you can use p < .001.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (e.g., “decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

P-Value Text

p < .05 Results were plotted at p < .05, and channels surviving false discovery rate (FDR) correction (q < .05) are marked with asterisks in Figures 1 and 2.

The APA manual states: Report exact p values (e.g., p = .031) to two or three decimal places. However, report p values less than .001 as p < .001. However, 2 decimals is too imprecise for many use-cases (e.g., a p value meta-analysis), so report p values with three digits.

To check Non-Significant P Value Check

What we found

We found 1 non-significant p value that should be checked for appropriate interpretation.

Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

Metacheck does not yet analyze automatically whether sentences which include non-significant p-values are correct, but we recommend manually checking the sentences below for possible misinterpreted non-significant p values.

Text Sentence
ps ≥ 0.06 The diagnosed and undiagnosed groups performed similarly on all assessments (ps ≥ 0.06).

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine, Daniel Lakens and Cristian Mesquida

Overall, among all instances flagged as power statements, 90.6% were correct (positive predictive value).

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). Statcheck might still miss one-sided tests that are described in another sentence, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

If you want to extend the package to be able to download files from additional data repositories reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. A TRO uses them for each trov:TransparentResearchPerformance. * run_results$exit_status: the exit status of the process that ran the script (0 = ended normally); NA when the process did not report one (a time limit, or Docker failing to start the container). * artifacts: one row per file, with stage (deposit: as downloaded from the repository, hashed from the downloaded file; before_run: the run folder just before the first script ran, after metacheck’s own changes, such as rewritten paths and unpacked archives; after_run: only the files the scripts made (status new), changed (changed) or removed (removed), as an unchanged file is the one listed before the run), path (relative to the run folder, as valid UTF-8, a byte of another encoding written as <xx>), size (bytes), sha256, mime_type (by extension, NA when unknown), source (the file’s address in the repository, for deposit). The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). Files metacheck itself copied or made between passes (see modifications, output_copied, file_placed) show in after_run too. A TRO uses the rows as trov:ResearchArtifacts and each stage as a trov:ArtifactArrangement. * modifications$sha256_before, modifications$sha256_after: the hash of the changed script as downloaded and as it ran; NA when the script is not at the same path in both (a script made from an R Markdown file). * environment: the system that ran the code (trov:TransparentResearchSystem in a TRO): metacheck_version, started_at, ended_at, host (R version, platform, operating system), sandbox, network_isolated (TRUE only when every Docker run had --network none; the process sandbox does not stop the network), internet, timeout_per_script, total_timeout, python_local (the Python run outside Docker and its version), and docker: runs (for each kind of run, r_run, r_install, python_run, python_install, the image and the docker run arguments, such as --network none, --read-only, --cap-drop ALL, no-new-privileges, the process limit and the memory and processor limits) and images (for each image, its ID and digests from docker image inspect, its creation time and the R and Python versions its environment declares).

run_results$checked says what the run of each script shows about the code, in five groups, so that a script that does not reproduce is kept apart from one the check could not test: ran (it ran to the end); code_error (it did not reproduce: it stopped with an error of the code or the deposit, could not be read, or needs a file the deposit does not hold); dependency (a package or program was not available: a package that could not be installed or was not loaded, a program outside R and Python, a system library, or R crashing in a package’s compiled code); time (it did not finish within the time limit for one script, or was not started because the time for all scripts had run out); and not_checkable (it could not be checked automatically: it needs a person, the internet, a key or another setting of the authors’ computer, one platform such as Google Colab, Windows or OpenSAFELY, more memory than the run allows, a file that was not downloaded, a screen, or a program that runs it, or it is a file of a package of the code that Python runs only as a module of that package). It is NA for files run inside another script, files that are not an analysis, files not read, and a Docker failure. summary_table counts each group (repro_ran_n, repro_code_error_n, repro_dependency_n, repro_time_n, repro_not_checkable_n), and the report’s first sentence gives them. The traffic light reflects the static readiness signals by default; a script in code_error that stopped with an error makes it red, as a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure, and a script in one of the other three groups keeps it from being green. Calls that need a screen, which the run does not have (dev.print(), dev.copy2pdf(), dev.copy2eps(), savePlot(), windows(), win.graph(), x11(), quartz(), View()), are replaced before the run by calls that need none, which modifications records as screen_call_replaced. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

Purpose: Children with dyslexia show persistent phonological difficulties in controlled experiments yet often comprehend everyday spoken language successfully. The present study examined how children with and without dyslexia neurally track phonological, lexical, and syntactic information during naturalistic story listening, and how this tracking relates to reading proficiency.

Study 1 examined neural tracking in 140 typically developing (TD) children. Study 2 compared children with dyslexia with age-and gender-matched TD controls (n = 35 per group). Large language model-derived surprisal estimates indexed tracking at each level at exploratory and multiple comparison corrected thresholds.

Results: In TD children, each level of surprisal tracking showed a distinct cortical profile, and higher reading comprehension was associated with greater phonological and syntactic tracking, but reduced lexical tracking. Children with dyslexia also showed distinct tracking profiles across levels. In this group, however, higher reading proficiency was associated with greater lexical and syntactic tracking and reduced phonological tracking. Group contrasts mirrored these patterns.

Children with and without dyslexia differ in how neural tracking of linguistic information relates to reading. In dyslexia, reduced phonological tracking alongside greater lexical tracking may reflect increased reliance on higher-level linguistic information. Syntactic tracking was positively related to reading proficiency in both groups, consistent with the protracted development of syntax and its contribution to literacy. These findings offer new insights into the real-time neurobiology of naturalistic language comprehension and how the interaction of underlying skills may vary in children with dyslexia.

Introduction

Dyslexia presents a scientific conundrum. Children with dyslexia struggle with reading while appearing similar to their peers in everyday conversation. Both awareness of and early screening for dyslexia have increased over the past decades, yet the disorder often remains difficult to detect until reading demands increase in the early elementary grades (Colenbrander et al., 2018). The International Dyslexia Association (IDA) defines dyslexia as a learning disability marked by difficulty with word reading and spelling, frequently preceded by weaknesses in oral language such as phonological awareness (IDA, 2025). Although phonological differences in dyslexia are now well characterized, especially at the individual-word levels critical to visual word recognition, far less is known about how children with dyslexia process connected speech, where language sound, meaning, and structure accumulate across words (Wolf et al., 2024).

Exploring this level offers an opportunity to uncover potential avenues of strength that could be leveraged in intervention. Our study therefore aims to uncover how children's developing brains track different levels of linguistic information during story listening, and whether children with dyslexia differ from typically developing readers in this tracking.

Reading comprehension, or the ability to derive meaning from written language, builds upon word reading and language comprehension skills (Gough & Tunmer, 1986; Kim, 2020; Tong & Deacon, 2025). Language comprehension is a complex ability that draws on multiple levels of linguistic information, including phonological, syntactic, and semantic information at a foundational level (Kim, 2020; Tong & Deacon, 2025). Characterizing how these different sources of information contribute to comprehension, including naturalistic narrative listening, has long posed both theoretical and methodological challenges. Surprisal Theory (Hale, 2001; Levy, 2008) offers one approach to this challenge by characterizing processing load in terms of the contextual predictability of linguistic input: the surprisal of a linguistic unit reflects how unexpected it is given the preceding context. Combined with neuroimaging, this framework makes it possible to examine how the brain tracks variation in linguistic surprisal during active comprehension (e.g., Brennan & Hale, 2019; Shain et al., 2024; Willems et al., 2015) and, more recently, how individual differences in this neural tracking relate to children's language and reading abilities, including in children with dyslexia (Klimovich-Gray et al., 2023; Salig et al., preprint). Furthermore, advancements in computational language modeling are evolving to offer characterizable predictions at multiple levels of language representation, including phonological, lexical, and syntactic information at the neural level within naturalistic contexts such as story listening tasks (Heilbron et al., 2022). This study builds on theoretical and methodological advancements, using child-friendly fNIRS neuroimaging to examine oral comprehension in real time at multiple levels of language processing in typically developing readers (Study 1) and children with dyslexia (Study 2).

Language development across language domains and learners

Language development emerges through interactions among multiple interacting but partially distinct linguistic systems. Phonological development provides an early foundation for identifying and organizing the sounds of language (Melby-Lervåg et al., 2012). In turn, word learning and gains in lexico-semantic knowledge fine-tune phonological representations, illustrating the reciprocal nature of development across linguistic levels (Werker, 2018). Similar interactions characterize the development of syntax and semantics. Children can use sentence structure to constrain the meanings of unfamiliar words through syntactic bootstrapping (Gleitman, 1990), while familiar words and their meanings can provide a semantic foundation for discovering syntactic regularities (Babineau et al., 2021). These interactions continue across childhood as lexical knowledge expands and children comprehend and produce increasingly complex sentence structures (Owens et al., 2024). Thus, although phonological, semantic, and syntactic processes are distinguishable language sub-processes, their development is deeply interconnected (Kim, 2020). The interconnected nature of language development and processing raises an important question about how potential differences in one language sub-system, such as weaknesses in phonological processes that often characterize dyslexia, might influence the development and functioning of others (Wolf et al., 2024).

Learning to read provides a particularly important context in which to examine these interactions. Phonological processing helps children connect language sounds to orthographic representations, a skill especially critical for learning to read in alphabetic languages. In turn, phonological difficulties typically underlie word-reading difficulties in dyslexia (Wolf et al., 2024). Yet, as reflected in the IDA's recent updates to the definition of dyslexia, researchers are finding that language differences associated with dyslexia may extend beyond phonology, while the nature of these differences remain unresolved (IDA, 2025). For instance, children with dyslexia have shown differences in syntactic processing, yet these differences might be tied to phonological processing rather than reflecting an independent syntactic impairment (Robertson et al., 2024; Rothou & Nisiotis, 2025). Children with dyslexia may also experience difficulty with more abstract derivational morphology while drawing on the semantic transparency of free base morphemes as a source of support during word recognition (Eggleston et al., 2024). These findings suggest that the critical question may not be whether dyslexia is associated with deficits in each linguistic domain independently, but rather how phonological, semantic, and syntactic information are recruited and integrated during comprehension. A critical caveat is that studies often use different tasks to measure different language sub-processes (e.g., phonological awareness vs. sentence judgment tasks). Examining these processes simultaneously during naturalistic spoken-language comprehension may therefore offer a powerful and unique lens for revealing differences that are difficult to capture with standard measures that assess phonological, semantic, and syntactic proficiency separately.

Development of neural systems for linguistic subskills

The neural organization supporting language comprehension undergoes increased finetuning to support advanced language processes required for complex language reading comprehension, from relatively broad, temporal-lobe-centered organization toward increasingly specialized frontotemporal networks (Friederici, 2025). Phonological processing shows relatively early specialization in the left superior temporal gyrus (STG), with increasing involvement of dorsal inferior frontal regions across childhood (Martin et al., 2015; Weiss et al., 2018).

Semantic processing also progresses from early specialization in the left middle temporal gyrus (MTG; Weiss et al., 2018) to involving the ventral aspect of the left inferior frontal gyrus (IFG pars triangularis; Wang et al., 2021). Finally, syntactic specialization appears to follow a relatively more protracted trajectory, showing recruitment of STG regions early on, alongside pathways of semantic processing in the MTG (Wang et al., 2020 (Wang et al., , 2021)), with the dorsal aspect of left IFG (pars opercularis) becoming more selectively engaged later as children near adolescence (Enge et al., 2020; Skeide et al., 2014; Wagley et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2024). Prior literature has also noted the role of the MTG for processing syntactically complex sentences in adulthood (e.g., Friederici, 2012; Matchin & Hickok, 2020) and the parietal lobe for overall linguistic integration (e.g., Zeng et al., 2025), highlighting the intertwined nature of structure and meaning mapping for comprehension. These functional changes occur alongside strengthening anatomical and functional connections between inferior frontal and posterior temporal language regions (Friederici, 2025), resulting in an increasingly specialized but interacting network for phonological, semantic, and syntactic processing.

Behavioral and neurodevelopmental differences for children with dyslexia

Difficulties with language tasks tapping into phonological competences is a core characteristic of dyslexia (Snowling & Melby-Lervåg, 2016). At the neural level, differences have been found between children with dyslexia and typically-developing readers who are either age-matched (and thus show increased reading and phonological awareness performance; e.g., Blau et al., 2010; Cantiani et al., 2013) or proficiency-matched (younger children with comparable reading and phonological task performance; e.g., Eggleston et al., 2024; Hoeft et al., 2007). The latter comparisons suggest differences in the functionality of temporoparietal regions (Hoeft et al., 2007)-differences that likely precede the onset of literacy instruction (Raschle et al., 2012). These differences in phonological processing are thought to influence developmental trajectories for literacy development with dyslexia, and the question is whether and how these differences may relate to other elements of language development and their neural architecture (Hoeft et al., 2007; Im et al., 2015; Raschle et al., 2011).

Our understanding of syntactic processes in dyslexia is sparser and more inconclusive relative to the evidence on phonological processing (Wolf et al., 2024). Studies observing grammatical differences in children at familial risk for dyslexia have shown both significant (Raschle et al., 2012) and nonsignificant (Raschle et al., 2014) differences from their agematched peers. Electrophysiological evidence, however, points to neural differences. For example, subject-verb agreement violations were reported to elicit an expected posterior P600 event-related potential (ERP) response in typically developing children, while children with dyslexia (and without additional language impairment) showed a broadly distributed N400-like response typically associated with meaning-related violations (Cantiani et al., 2013). These children also showed difficulty manipulating nonword morphemes, both pieces of evidence suggesting a potential over-reliance on lexico-semantic information rather than their syntactic representations (Cantiani et al., 2013).

Semantic processing, on the other hand, has been documented as being largely intact in individuals with dyslexia, with differences emerging mainly when meaning is less available or more abstract (Booth et al., 2007; Deacon et al., 2019; Eggleston et al., 2024). For example, children with dyslexia engaged left ventral inferior frontal and middle temporal regions comparably to reading-matched controls for semantically transparent free base morphemes but showed weaker activation for more semantically abstract and analytically-complex derivational affixes (Eggleston et al., 2024). Taken together, these findings suggest differences at one level may depend on the information available at another, raising the question of how phonological, lexical, and syntactic processes interact in real time as children with and without dyslexia comprehend language.

Methodological considerations for capturing naturalistic listening

The elementary-school years are considered a particularly important period for language network specialization, as neural systems for oral language are simultaneously being recruited to support literacy acquisition while adjusting to process increasingly complex written language (Kim, 2020). However, much of what is known about the development of phonological, semantic, and syntactic processing in children comes from experimentally constrained paradigms designed to isolate particular linguistic components, which can sometimes be exceedingly difficult for children with dyslexia or language impairments (e.g. Hoeft et al., 2007; Pothos & Kirk, 2004). These include word-level rhyme or phonological judgments, picture judgments at word or sentence-levels, and examining grammaticality at sentence-levels (see Enge et al., 2020 and Ren et al., 2024 for reviews encompassing different measurement paradigms). As a result, less is known about how the neurobiology of language operates and develops in real time during naturalistic language comprehension across different levels of linguistic processing.

Naturalistic listening paradigms have become increasingly popular in developmental cognitive neuroscience, as they are considered more ecologically valid and less task-specific than paradigms presenting isolated words or sentences (Hamilton & Huth, 2020; Nastase et al., 2020).

Much of this work has focused on functional connectivity, revealing associations with children's language and literacy development, including in children with language and reading difficulties (e.g., Farah & Horowitz-Kraus, 2019; Horowitz-Kraus et al., 2026; Keshavarzi et al., preprint; Twait et al., 2018). However, connectivity approaches are less suited to identifying which aspects of the unfolding linguistic input drive neural responses at a given moment.

Computational models of linguistic predictability provide a means of linking moment-tomoment variation in the speech signal to corresponding variation in neural activity (Brennan & Hale, 2019; Willems et al. 2015). Much of this work has focused on lexical surprisal, or the unexpectedness of a word given its preceding context (Hale, 2001; Levy, 2008), so as to examine how the brain processes language as it unfolds during naturalistic listening in adults (e.g., Brennan & Hale, 2019; Shain et al. 2024) and children (Salig et al., preprint; Yu et al., 2025).

Lexical surprisal has been associated with neural responses implicated in lexico-semantic processing, including the N400 (Michaelov et al., 2024), demonstrating the potential of computational models to characterize specific dimensions of language processing within continuous speech (Wilcox et al., 2020). More recently, this approach has been extended beyond aggregate word-level predictability. Heilbron et al. (2022), for example, decomposed predictions generated from a language model into phonemic, lexical, and syntactic dimensions and identified dissociable neural responses during naturalistic story listening (see also Lopopolo et al. 2017).

This multi-level approach is particularly well suited to the study of developing language systems, whose components become increasingly specialized while continuing to interact (Johnson, 2000; Skeide & Friederici, 2016), as well as to children with dyslexia, whose language processing may involve phonological differences alongside subtler and potentially interacting differences in syntactic and semantic processing (e.g., Cantiani et al., 2013; Eggleston et al., 2024; Robertson et al., 2024). Building on this framework, the present study uses large language model-derived estimates of phonological, lexical, and syntactic surprisal during naturalistic story listening to offer insights into how the developing brain tracks linguistic information and whether neural response at each level varies by children's reading abilities.

Present study

The present study examines how children's brains support language comprehension during naturalistic story listening by characterizing neural sensitivity to phonological, lexical and syntactic predictability. Using functional near-infrared spectroscopy (fNIRS), we modeled hemodynamic responses to large language model-derived estimates of surprisal at these three levels of processing in children ages 7 to 12 (Grades 1 to 5). This approach allows three levels of linguistic information to be examined simultaneously within a single continuous listening task, rather than across separate tasks designed to isolate different levels of language processing. Study 1 examined neurobiology of language comprehension in 140 English-speaking typically developing children with no identified language or reading difficulties. We asked (a) whether and where neural responses track phonological, lexical, and syntactic surprisal within the language network and (b) how sensitivity to these different levels relates to variation in reading proficiency. Given the limited developmental fNIRS work using this approach, all analyses within the present study were considered exploratory. Drawing on prior naturalistic neuroimaging findings with adults (Lopopolo et al., 2017; Heilbron et al., 2022), we expected the three linguistic levels to show partially dissociable patterns of neural tracking. Developmental work further suggested that lexical surprisal tracking may preferentially engage temporal and parietal regions (e.g., Weiss et al., 2018; Zeng et al., 2025), phonological surprisal may track in primarily frontal regions (e.g., Martin et al., 2015; Weiss et al., 2018), and syntactic surprisal in frontotemporal regions (e.g., Friederici, 2012; Matchin & Hickok, 2020; Wang et al., 2024), more broadly for this age group. Notably, these predictions were held cautiously given the substantial methodological differences between traditional experimental paradigms and continuous measures of linguistic predictability.

Study 2 examined neurobiology of language comprehension in children with dyslexia as compared to age-and gender-matched typically developing readers. We asked (a) whether neural sensitivity to phonological, lexical, and syntactic predictability differs between children with and without dyslexia and (b) how these neural responses relate to reading proficiency across children.

Based on prior research with children with dyslexia (including recent works using lexical surprisal: Klimovich-Gray et al., 2023; Salig et al., preprint), we did not anticipate finding group differences at lexico-semantic levels of processing, but expected that children with dyslexia may be less effective at tracking phonological, and possibly syntactic, surprisal at the neural level.

Together, these two studies are aiming to inform both theory and methodological approaches to understanding language development and processing, and how these may vary in relation to reading ability and dyslexia-related variations between learners.

Methods

Participants

Study 1

Study 1 observed data from 140 typically developing English monolingual children ages 7 to 12 years (M = 9.06, SD = 1.21), ranging from Grades 1 to 5. The sample included 56.4% of children who identified as female, 42.9% as male, and one child (.7%) as nonbinary. All participants were from the United States Midwest, with 72.1% being white, 4.3% Asian, 4.3% Black or African American and 19.3% reporting multiple races. Caregiver education level was collected through questionnaires and served as a proxy for socioeconomic status. Mean education level was 6.28 (SD = 0.99; range = 0 to 8), indicating having completed a bachelor's degree. All children attained standard scores of 86 or higher on standardized measures of phonological awareness and word reading, did not have a history of epilepsy, seizures, traumatic brain injury, or other severe neurological conditions, and did not have a formal diagnosis of dyslexia. As the recruitment process was geared toward children with reading difficulties, some parents of children in the Study 1 sample did report language-related concerns, but all included children met the standard score thresholds and were therefore treated as typically developing.

Study 2

To examine potential differences during listening in children with dyslexia, 35 children (31.4% female, 68.6% male) were identified from a larger dataset based on a number of key factors commonly associated with dyslexia. This subset of children had an average age of 9.34 years (SD = 1.15) across Grades 1 through 5. The ethnicity breakdown of the sample is as follows: 74.2% White, 2.9% Latino, 2.9% Black, 2.9% Middle Eastern or North African, and 17.1% reported multiple races. The mean caregiver education level was 6 (SD = 1.07), corresponding to having attained a bachelor's degree.

At the time of data collection, dyslexia screening was not required in public schools in the region of data collection, so many children with reading difficulties had no formal diagnosis.

We therefore included children both with and without a prior diagnosis, and used a stricter cutoff for those without a formal diagnosis. Children with a formal diagnosis were included if they scored at least 1 SD below the normed mean (standard score ≤ 85; M = 100, SD = 15) on at least two of four measures: the Elision subtest of the Comprehensive Test of Phonological Processing (CTOPP-2; Wagner et al., 2013), the Sight Word Efficiency and Phonemic Decoding Efficiency subtests of the Test of Word Reading Efficiency (TOWRE-2; Torgesen et al., 2012), and the Letter-Word Identification subtest of the Woodcock-Johnson IV Tests of Achievement (Schrank et al., 2014). This identified 15 children. Children without a diagnosis had to score at least 1.5 SD below the mean (≤ 78) on at least two of the same measures, which identified 20 more.

Parents of 18 of these 20 children reported concerns of language or reading difficulties but no specific diagnosis. The diagnosed and undiagnosed groups performed similarly on all assessments (ps ≥ 0.06).

Once the dyslexia sample had been identified, 35 children (31.4% female, 68.6% male) from the typically developing sample in Study 1 were age-and gender-matched for group comparison purposes. This sub-group had an average age of 9.30 years (SD = 1.12), also across Grades 1 through 5. The ethnicity make-up of this sample was as follows: 65.7% White, 8.6% Asian, 11.4% Black, and 14.3% reported multiple races. Similar to the other samples in this study, the mean caregiver education level was 6.50 (SD = 0.87).

Procedure

Participants were invited to an in-person laboratory visit that lasted approximately 3 hours. Caregivers and children were first asked for informed consent and assent, followed by caregivers being asked to fill out survey questionnaires about their children's learning experiences, medical history, and demographic information. Children were invited to first complete fNIRS testing, followed by behavioral testing. After testing was completed, participants received monetary compensation. All consent forms, questionnaires, and testing protocols were IRB approved for human subject testing.

All testing was administered by trained research assistants. Research assistants were undergraduate and graduate students studying psychology. All were trained in the administration of all tasks across a series of two to three hour-long sessions, followed by a test-out session to determine whether they were able to accurately follow protocol. Administration of all behavioral assessments followed published guidelines of each respective testing manual.

Behavioral Measures

Language measures administered included oral language, cognitive, and reading assessments. We administered the Test of Nonverbal Intelligence (TONI; Brown et al., 2010) to screen for non-language related disorders. For phonological awareness we administered the Elision subtest of the CTOPP-2 (Wagner et al., 2013). To measure reading ability we administered the Woodcock-Johnson IV Tests of Achievement (Schrank et al., 2014) Passage Comprehension and Letter-Word Identification subtests, as well as the Sight Word Efficiency (real word) and Phonemic Decoding Efficiency (pseudo word) subtests of the TOWRE-2.

fNIRS Task

All participants completed the story-listening task under fNIRS neuroimaging. The task entailed listening to a lab modified, 14-minute-and-54-second version of the Henry Huggins story (Cleary, 2009). The story was about a boy named Henry and his new dog. There were five pauses embedded into the story, at which point children were presented a basic comprehension question with two response options. An example of a question is "What should Henry do to bring the dog onto the bus?" with the options "He should get a box" or "He should get a collar". The child would then have to select their response using a two-button keypad. After the response, prerecorded feedback was provided, and the story resumed. Participants first completed a practice round consisting of the first 56 seconds of the story, followed by the task's first question-and-answer segment. This test portion had 1,956 words and ran 13 minutes and 58 seconds and was used in all analyses for the present study.

Beginning and end times for each word in the presented story were estimated using Praat software (Boersma et al., 2026) and the Montreal Forced Aligner (McAuliffe et al., 2017).

Surprisal values for the next word, the initial phoneme of the next word, and the word's part-ofspeech were calculated for each word of the audio stimulus as described in the next section.

Lexical surprisal reflects the overall contextual predictability of a given word based on preceding discourse context, quantifying how unexpected a specific lexical item is within the ongoing narrative. As previously mentioned, lexical surprisal estimates have been shown to have a reliable relation to N400 responses (Michaelov et al., 2024), electrophysiological reactions which are traditionally thought to capture dissonance in meaning-related processing (Kutas & Federmeier, 2000; Kutas & Hillyard, 1980). Initial phoneme surprisal, from this point on referred to as phonological surprisal, indexes the bottom-up phonological predictability of a word's starting speech sound given the preceding phonetic context, capturing early auditory expectations prior to full lexical access and retrieval. Part-of-speech surprisal, or syntactic surprisal, captures the syntactic expectancy of a word's grammatical category; for instance, whether a noun, verb, or adjective was most expected given the prior context window. This measure was employed to reflect how well each word fits into the expected structural framework of a coherent narrative passage.

Data analysis

Surprisal estimation. Our protocol for the estimation of phonological, lexical, and syntactic surprisal followed the approach of Heilbron et al. (2022), adapted for use with fNIRS data. We used GPT-2 small to estimate surprisal via the Hugging Face Transformers package in Python, the CMU Pronouncing Dictionary (Carnegie Mellon University, 2014) through the Natural Language Toolkit library (Bird et al., 2009) for the initial phoneme estimations, and spaCy (Honnibal et al., 2020) for part-of-speech tagging for the syntactic surprisal values.

Lexical surprisal was calculated as the negative log-probability assigned to each word by GPT-2, given its prior context (Hale, 2001; Levy, 2008). Part-of-speech (e.g., syntactic) surprisal was calculated by, first, sampling k next word tokens given the left-context where k was set to the greater of 50 or the number of tokens whose probability summed to 0.9. Next, we summed the model's predicted probabilities across the next-word candidates that shared the same part-ofspeech tag as the target word. The negative log of that summed probability was used for modeling. Phonological surprisal followed a similar calculation: the initial phoneme of each word was identified, all predicted probabilities of the k next-word candidates sharing that same initial phoneme were summed, and then the negative log of the summed probability was used for modeling. All models also accounted for word rate (a baseline control for the auditory features of each word) and lexical frequency, which was compiled from a database of HAL frequency counts (Balota et al., 2007).

Preprocessing of fNIRS data. Preparation and preprocessing of fNIRS hemodynamic response data was conducted in MATLAB using the AnalyzIR Toolbox for fNIRS (Santosa et al., 2018). Preprocessing steps included optical density conversion, temporal derivative distribution repair (TDDR) motion correction, applying the modified Beer-Lambert law, downsampling the data to 4Hz, and trimming data to remove question-answer sections, thus leaving only story listening portions for analysis.

Subject and group-level modeling. Hemodynamic responses were modeled using a twolevel general linear model (GLM) in the AnalyzIR Toolbox. At the subject level, HbO amplitude in each of 32 channels was modeled as a function of five regressors, each convolved with the canonical hemodynamic response function (peak at 6 seconds): word rate (1 at each word onset, controlling for acoustic onsets), log word frequency, and lexical, phonological, and syntactic surprisal. Short-separation channels were included as nuisance regressors to correct for systemic physiological noise, and estimation used autoregressive iteratively reweighted least squares (AR-IRLS; Barker et al., 2013). At the group level, beta coefficients were modeled with mixed-effects models including condition, age as a covariate, and a random intercept for each subject. Results were plotted at p < .05, and channels surviving false discovery rate (FDR) correction (q < .05) are marked with asterisks in Figures 1 and 2.

For Study One's moderator analysis, the same first-level analysis procedure was used, but the second level included interaction terms created between each condition and WJ-IV Passage Comprehension (PC) raw scores (used as a proxy for reading proficiency). Age was included as a covariate in the reading proficiency model, to examine the effect of reading proficiency above and beyond natural maturation with age, as well as real word reading scores (TOWRE SWE) to account for baseline decoding ability. This approach was also employed to examine the role of reading proficiency within the dyslexia sample using the same model parameters.

For Study Two's group comparison, the second-level model included a categorical group variable, coded as TD (typically developing) or DYS (dyslexia), to examine whether neural tracking of each condition differed by reading-difficulty status. Group category was entered as an interaction term with condition rather than as a main effect, and age was included as a covariate.

In this model, each condition's association with hemodynamic response was estimated separately for the TD and DYS groups. Group differences for each condition were tested using planned contrasts on the group-by-condition interaction terms, comparing the beta coefficient for the dyslexia (DYS) group against the typically developing (TD) group within each surprisal condition separately (e.g., DYS -TD for lexical surprisal).

Results

Baseline rate and frequency characteristics

As mentioned, in order to account for acoustic and lexical influences, all analyses included word rate and log word frequency as control predictors. Rate, which captures acoustic onsets, was positively linked to activation across bilateral superior temporal, temporoparietal, and parietal regions. Only one observation in the right hemisphere parietal lobe did not survive FDR correction. As expected, Log word frequency displayed the opposite pattern, correlating negatively with activation in bilateral posterior temporal and temporoparietal regions, such that higher frequency words were associated with lower activation. All associations survived FDR correction except for two associations, in the left and right MTG. These checks serve to validate the data analysis pipeline.

For this analysis, we observed two participant samples: a sample of 35 children with dyslexia, both with and without formal diagnoses, and a subsample of typically developing sample age-and gender-matched to the identified children with dyslexia. The age and gender matched TD sample (n = 35) showed a pattern broadly similar to that of the full TD sample: negative frontal tracking in a frontal channel for phonological surprisal, positive temporal tracking for lexical surprisal, and substantial right hemisphere tracking of syntactic surprisal.

Discussion

A central question in dyslexia research is how the phonological weaknesses often observed in this population relate to other language systems. Using naturalistic story listening, we asked how neural tracking of phonological, lexical, and syntactic surprisal relates to reading proficiency in children with and without dyslexia. Syntactic surprisal tracking was positively related to reading proficiency in both groups, whereas relations for phonological and lexical tracking appeared to diverge at exploratory thresholds. Among typically developing children, better readers showed stronger phonological tracking. Among children with dyslexia, better readers showed weaker phonological tracking but stronger lexical tracking, a pattern generally mirrored by direct group contrasts. These findings are consistent with accounts in which language subsystems develop interdependently (e.g., Werker, 2018; Wolf et al., 2024). We discuss each linguistic level in turn and then consider the theoretical implications.

Surprisal tracking across levels

The present findings suggest that the developing brain is likely making predictions at multiple levels, as has been documented previously in adults (e.g., Heilbron et al., 2022; Ryskin & Niewland, 2023), but that recruitment may shift with maturity and reading skill. For phonological surprisal, we found unique frontal and anterior temporal activation-a finding substantiated by prior literature using experimental methods to capture phonological processing in the left superior temporal gyrus (Martin et al., 2015) and left dorsal inferior frontal gyrus (Weiss et al., 2018). In contrast, we found more widespread activation for phonological tracking of surprisal in children with dyslexia, likely reflecting a lack of specialization for this group.

When compared to an age-and gender-matched control group, the control group showed more activation in the supramarginal gyrus, a region key for phonological processing and literacy development (e.g., Booth et al., 2004; Sliwinska et al., 2012), over the dyslexia group, at an exploratory threshold. Additionally, while we found an FDR-corrected positive relationship between frontal tracking of phonological surprisal and reading proficiency in our typically developing sample, we found negative correlations in frontal and parietal regions for children with dyslexia at exploratory thresholds, suggesting that as reading proficiency increases, tracking of phonological surprisal may decrease as these children find different strategies and pathways to reading. The trends of these findings fit our hypothesis that children with dyslexia may show reduced tracking of phonological surprisal relative to their TD counterparts. This hypothesis was informed by decades of research (see Snowling & Melby-Lervåg, 2016 for a meta-analysis) showing that phonological difficulties are a core contributor to differences in the linguistic processing of children with dyslexia, and the present study contributes that this may continue to be the case even during a naturalistic listening task not specifically designed to isolate phonological awareness.

Children with and without dyslexia both tracked lexical surprisal in left frontal and temporal regions, consistent with the broader literature implicating these areas in semantic processing (e.g., Wang et al., 2021 Wang et al., , 2024;; Weiss et al., 2018). This suggests that the basic neural patterns for using semantic context to anticipate upcoming words are largely intact in children with dyslexia, in line with prior work reporting little-to-no group differences in lexical surprisal tracking in children (Klimovich-Grey et al., 2023; Salig et al., preprint). However, the relationship between lexical surprisal tracking and reading proficiency showed opposite trends between groups. Among children with dyslexia, stronger tracking in the left superior temporal gyrus (STG) was associated with better reading proficiency, a robust effect that survived FDRcorrection and showed greater tracking than in typically developing children at an exploratory threshold in the group comparison. Typically developing children, by contrast, showed only negative relations to reading comprehension at exploratory thresholds.

One possible interpretation is that the lexical surprisal effects reflect, in part, children's use of higher-level meaning to anticipate upcoming words. What surprisal captures depends on how context is modeled (Hale, 2016). For instance, in grammar-based formulations, a single surprisal value combines syntactic and lexical expectations (Hale, 2001; Levy, 2008). We instead derived surprisal from GPT-2, a large language model trained on extensive text that estimates word probabilities from the full preceding context (e.g., Heilbron et al., 2022; Wilcox et al., 2020). Because syntactic and phonological surprisal were modeled as separate variables, lexical surprisal may more closely reflect semantic and discourse-level expectations, in line with its link to N400 responses (Frank et al., 2015; Michaelov & Bergen, 2020). For children with dyslexia, whose phonological skills are often weaker, these dynamically interacting higher-level skills may lead to comprehension (Wolf et al., 2024). This could help explain why, despite prior work reporting similar spatial profiles across groups (Klimovich-Gray et al., 2023; Salig et al., preprint), correlations with connected reading comprehension uncovered unique variations.

Lastly, contrary to prior work suggesting that syntactic processing is impaired in dyslexia (e.g., Raschle et al., 2012), children with dyslexia in our study showed robust neural tracking of syntactic surprisal during the naturalistic story listening task, which also showed notable relation to their reading proficiency. In key frontal and temporal language regions, the children with dyslexia showed tracking patterns similar to those previously reported in typically developing children (e.g., Friederici, 2025). Most notably, syntactic surprisal tracking in the inferior parietal lobe (IPL), a key region for information integration (Hickok & Poeppel, 2007; Zeng et al., 2025), was positively correlated with reading proficiency at an FDR-corrected threshold. In the larger typically developing sample in Study 1, we observed expected recruitment of the left STG and MTG for syntactic processing, and tracking in these regions was likewise positively correlated with passage comprehension at exploratory thresholds. Taken together, these results suggest that syntactic surprisal tracking, as measured by part-of-speech categorization, in children with dyslexia largely parallels that of typically developing children.

One explanation for this unexpected pattern is that prior work on syntax in dyslexia has largely focused on morphosyntax, such as inflectional morphology and subject-verb agreement (e.g., Rispens & Been, 2004), whose fine-grained features are closely tied to phonological processing (Casani et al., 2022). In one study, group differences in oral syntactic awareness were no longer significant after controlling for verbal working memory and phonological awareness (Robertson et al., 2024). Part-of-speech surprisal, by contrast, captures coarse, word-level category expectations (Heilbron et al., 2022), and similarly large-grained syntactic measures have been shown to be strongly related to reading comprehension (Tong et al., 2025). As children's narratives offer rich predictive structure (Baldassano, 2023), children with dyslexia may draw on expectations about this structure during story listening to support comprehension, despite differences with finer-grained sublinguistic phonological features.

Theoretical connections

Theories of language development and reading emphasize that phonological, lexical, and syntactic knowledge develop interdependently (e.g., Kim, 2020; Metsala & Walley, 1998; Tong & Deacon, 2025; Werker, 2018), yet empirical research, particularly on dyslexia, has traditionally measured these skills separately. By examining neural tracking at all three levels simultaneously, the present study revealed a pattern that isolated measures might miss: weaker phonological tracking in children with dyslexia co-occurred with robust lexical and syntactic tracking, both of which were positively related to passage comprehension. The relation between syntactic tracking and reading proficiency was shared across groups, suggesting that categorylevel syntactic processing may remain largely intact in dyslexia. This is consistent with metaanalytic evidence that structural syntactic skills contribute to reading comprehension from early elementary into secondary school (Tong et al., 2025).

Overall, the findings of the present study support recent calls to understand dyslexia in terms of how language systems interact (IDA, 2025; Wolf et al., 2024). Based on the promising findings of the present study at largely exploratory thresholds, further validating and refining the present study's multi-level surprisal approach may lead to useful implications for clinical and educational practice, such as informing intervention design and assessment features that work to identify the strengths children with dyslexia can leverage for improved reading outcomes.

Limitations and Future Directions

Several limitations in this work come from the novelty of this experimental design. First, phonological surprisal was indexed only at the word-initial phoneme level. This measure was chosen due to its ability to adapt to the slow hemodynamic response measured by fNIRS, as this method of neuroimaging is unlikely to be able to resolve phoneme-by-phoneme timing. Second, all surprisal values were derived from GPT-2, a large language model (LLM) trained predominantly on adult-authored and adult-directed text. Due to these developmental differences, it remains unclear how well these estimates capture what a child listener might find predictable or surprising at each linguistic level compared to adult expectations.

These limitations point to several directions for future work. Methods with finer temporal resolution, such as EEG or MEG, could examine a wider range of LLM-derived measures in children. Additionally, Study 2 intentionally included both children with formal dyslexia diagnoses and children identified as at risk using standardized assessment thresholds, focusing on children actively experiencing reading difficulties. Future work may extend this approach to children with dyslexia who have received substantial remediation and, thus, show higher performance in reading to examine whether patterns of neural tracking shift with intervention.

Conclusion

The two studies presented in the current manuscript demonstrate that linguistic surprisal, estimated using LLMs at the phonological, lexical, and syntactic levels, meaningfully tracks children's hemodynamic responses during a naturalistic listening task. This novel approach offers a viable alternative to explicit, trial-based methodological paradigms that have traditionally been used to study childhood neurolinguistic development. The results of Study 1 show largely converging evidence with prior developmental literature for typically developing children, while Study 2 offered a promising extension of this approach to children at-risk for or diagnosed with dyslexia. The latter examination revealed subtle differences in the balance of tracking across different linguistic levels for the dyslexia group. The present findings extend traditional psycholinguistic and clinical approaches to measuring language processing and highlight potential areas of strength for children with dyslexia, offering potential avenues for future intervention and clinical work aimed at teaching compensatory strategies for improving literacy development.
