How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. A TRO uses them for each trov:TransparentResearchPerformance. * run_results$exit_status: the exit status of the process that ran the script (0 = ended normally); NA when the process did not report one (a time limit, or Docker failing to start the container). * artifacts: one row per file, with stage (deposit: as downloaded from the repository, hashed from the downloaded file; before_run: the run folder just before the first script ran, after metacheck’s own changes, such as rewritten paths and unpacked archives; after_run: only the files the scripts made (status new), changed (changed) or removed (removed), as an unchanged file is the one listed before the run), path (relative to the run folder, as valid UTF-8, a byte of another encoding written as <xx>), size (bytes), sha256, mime_type (by extension, NA when unknown), source (the file’s address in the repository, for deposit). The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). Files metacheck itself copied or made between passes (see modifications, output_copied, file_placed) show in after_run too. A TRO uses the rows as trov:ResearchArtifacts and each stage as a trov:ArtifactArrangement. * modifications$sha256_before, modifications$sha256_after: the hash of the changed script as downloaded and as it ran; NA when the script is not at the same path in both (a script made from an R Markdown file). * environment: the system that ran the code (trov:TransparentResearchSystem in a TRO): metacheck_version, started_at, ended_at, host (R version, platform, operating system), sandbox, network_isolated (TRUE only when every Docker run had --network none; the process sandbox does not stop the network), internet, timeout_per_script, total_timeout, python_local (the Python run outside Docker and its version), and docker: runs (for each kind of run, r_run, r_install, python_run, python_install, the image and the docker run arguments, such as --network none, --read-only, --cap-drop ALL, no-new-privileges, the process limit and the memory and processor limits) and images (for each image, its ID and digests from docker image inspect, its creation time and the R and Python versions its environment declares).

run_results$checked says what the run of each script shows about the code, in five groups, so that a script that does not reproduce is kept apart from one the check could not test: ran (it ran to the end); code_error (it did not reproduce: it stopped with an error of the code or the deposit, could not be read, or needs a file the deposit does not hold); dependency (a package or program was not available: a package that could not be installed or was not loaded, a program outside R and Python, a system library, or R crashing in a package’s compiled code); time (it did not finish within the time limit for one script, or was not started because the time for all scripts had run out); and not_checkable (it could not be checked automatically: it needs a person, the internet, a key or another setting of the authors’ computer, one platform such as Google Colab, Windows or OpenSAFELY, more memory than the run allows, a file that was not downloaded, a screen, or a program that runs it, or it is a file of a package of the code that Python runs only as a module of that package). It is NA for files run inside another script, files that are not an analysis, files not read, and a Docker failure. summary_table counts each group (repro_ran_n, repro_code_error_n, repro_dependency_n, repro_time_n, repro_not_checkable_n), and the report’s first sentence gives them. The traffic light reflects the static readiness signals by default; a script in code_error that stopped with an error makes it red, as a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure, and a script in one of the other three groups keeps it from being green. Calls that need a screen, which the run does not have (dev.print(), dev.copy2pdf(), dev.copy2eps(), savePlot(), windows(), win.graph(), x11(), quartz(), View()), are replaced before the run by calls that need none, which modifications records as screen_call_replaced. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

The Give-a-number (GaN) task is used to determine the symbolic number knowledge of children when they initially acquire the meaning of the number words. Precise measurement of number knowledge is essential because even small measured differences in the development process may lead to fundamental theoretical consequences. The task has several variations, and these versions are usually considered equivalent. However, recent works have raised the issue that different versions may classify children differently, which has been partially confirmed. Here, to extend these initial studies, the role of the various scoring methods of the task, that is, how the number knowledge is calculated given the task responses, was investigated. It was found that the scoring method considerably influences the observed number knowledge, and the effects of the various scoring method components are added up. These results demonstrate that the specific scoring method influences the validity and reliability of the measurement. These differences affect the comparability of studies using different methods of the GaN task, and various versions lead to differing observed patterns that, in turn, may lead to differing theoretical conclusions.

1 Introduction

Mathematical thinking is a fundamental capability in our culture, and understanding how this ability develops is a key challenge in the field of numerical cognition. One fundamental step in development is the initial understanding of the meaning of symbolic numbers, such as number words. In the last decades, a key task to measure whether children understand the meaning of number words is the Givea-Number (GaN) task (Wynn, 1990 (Wynn, , 1992)). In the GaN task, the child is asked to give a specific number of objects. If they succeed, the next larger number is asked, and if they fail, the next smaller number is asked. With this method, the largest reliably known number can be found. Using the GaN task, a series of typical response patterns was described. The youngest children cannot give any sets appropriately, even if they are able to recite the first number words ("one-two-three" etc.) correctly. They are called pre-knowers or non-knowers. Later, they are able to give one object correctly, but not larger numbers (termed one-knowers). In the following months, they learn to give two, then three, and finally four objects consistently (two-knowers, three-knowers, four-knowers). Children who are one-to four-knowers are called subset-knowers because, in the GaN task, they can correctly use only a subset of the numbers that they can appropriately recite. After being four-knowers, a leap was observed by Wynn (1990 Wynn ( , 1992)), and, in the GaN task, children were able to give any number that was in their counting list (i.e., number words that they can recite correctly). These children are called cardinalityprinciple-knowers (CP-knowers) because it is assumed that these children understand the cardinality principle, i.e., that the last number word in the counting process indicates the quantity of the set (Wynn, 1990 (Wynn, , 1992)).

While many details of the development of the initial understanding of symbolic numbers are known, there is an increasing number of works trying to refine this description. For example, it is still debated whether CP-knowers have solid conceptual knowledge about the cardinality principle (Davidson et al., 2012; Le Corre, 2014; Sella et al., 2017; Sella & Lucangeli, 2020). Relatedly, it is still debated what representations support this development (Carey, 2009; Carey & Barner, 2019; Piazza, 2010).

One key reason why the description of the development of the initial understanding of symbolic numbers is debated is that the applied methods may be suboptimal. The GaN task can be unreliable (Marchand et al., 2022) or invalid (e.g., Krajcsi & Reynvoet, 2024), where the measurement can be imprecise or even biased. Moreover, the GaN task is not a single, well-standardized task, but it has many versions (Sella et al., 2021; Wege et al., 2025). Figure 1 presents a series of possible differences between various versions of the GaN task that are used in the literature. In the last few decades, it has been assumed that these versions are equivalent and that the measured ability does not depend on the specific version of the GaN task. However, this is not necessarily true (Wege et al., 2025). For example, PREPRINT 3/21 it has been demonstrated that the follow-up question at the end of a trial in the GaN task (e.g., whether the child is asked to recount the given set or not) significantly modifies the observed number knowledge of the child (Krajcsi, 2021).

If, in contrast with the widely accepted assumption of the literature, various versions of the GaN task are not equivalent, it may have essential theoretical consequences. Many models of subset-and CPknowers depend on relatively small effects of the developmental patterns. For example, an Object Tracking System (OTS) account of subset-knowers (Carey, 2009) relies on the fact that after giving 4 correctly in the GaN task, children become CP-knowers (therefore, number knowledge and OTS show the same limitation of 4), since 5-, 6-, etc.-knowers were not observed in the studies of Wynn (1990 Wynn ( , 1992)). However, those studies included only relatively small samples (smaller than 30 children per study), and members of relatively rare categories are unlikely to be observed. Indeed, in larger samples, 5-, 6-, etc.-knowers can be observed, and these relatively minor differences in the observed patterns may question whether the OTS account is appropriate (see a review in Krajcsi & Fintor, 2023).

Similarly, several studies find that a relatively small proportion of children who are categorized as CPknowers still do not understand basic properties of number handling, raising the question whether they are truly CP-knowers (Davidson et al., 2012; Le Corre, 2014; Sella et al., 2017; Sella & Lucangeli, 2020). Alternatively, it is also possible that these children may be in fact subset-knowers who are incorrectly categorized as CP-knowers (Krajcsi & Reynvoet, 2024). As a final example, it has been demonstrated that in languages where not only singular and plural, but dual morphology is available, children may learn number 2 earlier than children speaking a language without dual morphology (Almoammer et al., 2013). This latter finding can often be observed by different categorization of 10-30% of the children with differing languages. All of these examples show that differing categorization of even a small proportion of children may have substantial theoretical consequences. Therefore, finding the appropriately valid and reliable GaN task version is fundamental to provide appropriate data for the theories of numerical development.

The aim of the present study is to investigate the possible differences between various versions of the GaN task, focusing on the various scoring methods, that is, what calculation method is used to assess children's number knowledge based on their responses in the GaN task.

1.1 Differences between the scoring methods of the GaN task

The GaN task performance can be scored in various ways to assess the child's knowledge of numbers.

In an extensive review of the current practices of the literature, Wege and her colleagues (2025, p. 12) conclude that "studies differ on what counts as evidence that a child knows the meaning of a certain number word". In the present subsection, some characteristic differences of the scoring methods are reviewed (see the three main points in the highlighted area in Figure 1, which are discussed below).

Criteria for correct response. When a trial is scored, in one version of the methods, only the precise responses are considered correct. In another version, a response is considered correct even if the given set is one item larger or smaller compared to the required value when the child is counting the set, e.g., when 5 is asked, responses between 4 and 6 are all considered to be correct (Le Corre et al., 2006; Le Corre & Carey, 2007; Wege et al., 2025). The motivation behind this more liberal latter criterion is not to miscategorize numbers as unknown when the numbers are, in fact, known, but the child makes a performance error. On the other hand, having a more liberal criterion may overestimate the known numbers. Critically, it is unknown how frequent the performance error may be and how heavily the differing scoring methods can modify the observed number knowledge.

A critical component of categorizing a number as known is not only that the number should be given appropriately for a given number word, but that the same number should not be given for other number words (Wynn, 1990). A typical pattern is that the last number that is given correctly is used for larger number words, too. Nonetheless, in the literature, there is no strict rule about how many times a number should be given incorrectly for other number words to be considered unknown, even for the correct number word (Wege et al., 2025). While giving a seemingly known value to another number word may decrease the observed number knowledge, it is unclear how often this may change number knowledge.

Criteria for knowing a number.

A number is usually considered to be known if the number is given correctly two out of three times (Cheung et al., 2016; Le Corre & Carey, 2007; Wege et al., 2025); that is, the mean correct response is larger than or equal to 66%. This is a reasonable threshold for 3 trials per number, but the 66% threshold in itself could be considered to be arbitrary, and it cannot PREPRINT 5/21 necessarily be usable for other trial numbers, for example, when a specific number is asked only once (Piantadosi et al., 2014; Pixner et al., 2018), or twice or four times (Krajcsi & Fintor, 2023; Wege et al., 2025). For example, for four trials, reasonable thresholds could be either two out of four (50%) or three out of four (75%) correct trials. Lower thresholds may lead to a higher observed number knowledge. However, relying on the classic assumption that number knowledge is all-or-nothing (Wynn, 1990 (Wynn, , 1992)), various thresholds would not modify the observed knowledge considerably. Contrarily, if number knowledge is more continuous in the sense that the proportion of correct responses gradually decreases for larger numbers (Krajcsi & Fintor, 2023), changing the threshold may modify the observed number knowledge.

Criteria for number knowledge. In most cases, all requested numbers are individually considered to be known or unknown based on specific criteria (e.g., the criteria discussed above), and number knowledge is defined as the largest number that is known without being a smaller unknown number (Wege et al., 2025). However, an alternative Bayesian approach is also used in the literature, where given responses could be considered in a more gradual way (Negen et al., 2011). According to this latter approach, the prior knowledge (the distribution of children's responses when they do not know what number was requested) is updated by the experimenter's request. If children know the meaning of the requested number, the likelihood of that number will be increased. If the children do not know the meaning of the requested number, the likelihood of their known numbers will be decreased, and the likelihood of their unknown numbers will be increased. Importantly, this simple model can provide response pattern predictions that in turn can be used to identify the number knowledge of a child, given their responses. In this Bayesian approach, the scoring aspects mentioned above (i.e., the criteria for categorizing a number to be known) are not relevant, but the number knowledge is scored in a more gradual and parallel way. Since this Bayesian approach differs radically from other scoring approaches used in the literature, it is not straightforward to predict how the observed number knowledge could be modified.

Find further examples of studies using the various versions of these criteria and related statistics in the excellent, extensive literature review of Wege and her colleagues (2025).

The aim of the present study is to investigate whether the observed number knowledge depends on the various scoring methods or their combinations described above, or, conversely, in line with the assumption of the literature, whether various scoring methods are equivalent. This question is essential because GaN task is scored in various ways (Wege et al., 2025), and potentially even small differences in the scoring outcome may have essential consequences regarding the theoretical conclusions. Additionally, administration and, more generally, the theoretical framework of the GaN task is debated (Krajcsi & Reynvoet, 2024; Marchand et al., 2022; Sella et al., 2021; Wege et al., 2025), and the role of scoring methods is also essential when various administration and related alternative scoring methods are considered. Therefore, the present study investigates whether different scoring methods influence PREPRINT 6/21 the observed number knowledge considerably, which could potentially influence the theoretical conclusions as well.

1.2 Observed number knowledge and study types

The GaN task is used in many studies applying various statistical methods. Probably, the two most frequent statistical approaches are studies that compare the means (e.g., groups are formed based on number knowledge) and correlational studies (i.e., the GaN-based number knowledge is correlated with some other numerical or non-numerical performance). It is important to highlight that these two types of studies are sensitive to different aspects of the observed number knowledge.

In a comparison study, the absolute value is critical: For example, it matters whether a child is categorized as a three-knower or as a four-knower. Additionally, it is even more important if a child is categorized as a subset-knower or as a CP-knower. In other words, in these studies, it is essential that the number knowledge be specified appropriately.

On the other hand, the criteria are less strict in correlational studies. Correlation is not sensitive to the linear transformation of the variables; in other words, if a constant value is added to a variable or the variable is multiplied by a constant value (or both), the correlation remains the same. For the correlational studies using the GaN task, this means that if a measurement method changes the observed number knowledge in a way that the transformation is linear (e.g., all participants are measured one category larger, such as two-knowers are observed as three-knowers, three-knowers are observed as four-knowers, etc.), then this bias will not influence the correlational results.

Therefore, it is important to keep in mind that various aspects of the changes in scoring methods should be investigated, and different study types may depend on different aspects of the potential measurement biases.

2 Methods

A GaN task data set was analyzed with different scoring methods, and the found number knowledge values were contrasted. All the GaN details that are recommended to be reported by Wege et al. (2025) are provided below.

The study was approved by the ethics committee of the Psychology Institute, ELTE Eötvös Loránd University, Hungary.

2.1 Participants

To provide at least 20 children for each number knowledge between non-knowers and five-knowers on average, a minimum sample size of 100 was aimed for. A smaller overall sample size may be suboptimal because children with relatively rare number knowledge categories may be entirely missing (Krajcsi & Fintor, 2023; Rousselle & Vossius, 2021). The study initially included 134 preschool participants from thirteen Hungarian preschools, 76 girls and 58 boys, with a mean age of 4;0 years, SD of 0.4 years, ranging between 3;1 and 5;0 years. Most preschools received children from middle-class families. All participants' parents provided written informed consent, and all participants provided verbal consent to participate in the study.

2.2 Tasks and procedure

Give-a-Number task. The children were presented with a pile of sponge balls of the same color (approximately 30 balls, each with a diameter of approximately 2 cm), and the experimenter asked them to provide a specific number of balls. "In this task, I want to see if you can pick these balls appropriately. Could you give me N balls? Just place them in front of me." After the response, the experimenter asked the child, "Is this N balls?", using the required number in the question. If the child answered no, then the child was asked again to give N balls. The numbers were asked from 1 to 9 in the following pseudo-random order: 1, 3, 2, 6, 4, 5, 7, 9, and 8. This order was used to overcome the predictive nature of an increasing series. The entire series of these values was repeated four times, resulting in 36 trials. The numbers provided by the children were recorded.

Counting list knowledge.

Counting list knowledge was measured to ensure that the number knowledge measured with the GaN task was not constrained by the children's counting list.

In the counting list task, the child was asked to continue verbal counting: "Could you continue this series? I will start it, and you continue! One, two, three..." The task was conducted twice, and the highest numbers in the correctly recited series were recorded.

Counting list knowledge was calculated as the mean of the highest numbers recited correctly from the two counting list repetitions. The mean was considered a compromise between minimum reliable performance (the minimum of the two values, i.e., a number that was reached consistently) and the maximum peak performance (the maximum of the two values, i.e., a number that was not always reached).

There were only two children whose counting list knowledge was not larger than their number knowledge; therefore, their number knowledge may be limited by the counting list knowledge. Because this pattern was rare and the different GaN scoring methods may provide different measured number knowledge, these two children were not excluded from the analysis.

Note that the task instruction included the number words "one, two, three"; consequently, it was not possible to specify if a child could recite the number words up to three or to lower values. Fifteen children fell into this category, and with the exception of a five-knower, all of them were 3-, or lessknowers. Therefore, these children either knew the counting list up to their number knowledge, or their counting list was just below their number knowledge. Importantly, for the large majority of these children, the number knowledge was independent of the scoring method. Consequently, although these PREPRINT 8/21 children's data were not removed from the analyses, their removal would even increase the effect of scoring methods on the observed number knowledge.

Session information.

The session also included a comparison task; however, the results of that task are irrelevant from the viewpoint of the present research question; therefore, those results are not reported here. The session included the tasks in the following order: GaN task, counting list, and comparison. A session lasted approximately 20 minutes. Data were collected by eight experimenters, all of whom were blind to the aim of the study. All tasks were administered in Hungarian.

2.3 Scoring methods

The same data were analyzed using different methods. These scoring versions were chosen because they are relatively frequently used in the literature (Wege et al., 2025) and/or because they can be reasonable alternative versions for future scoring methods and, at the same time, have already been considered in the literature.

1.

Base scoring method. This is the method that the descriptions of other methods of this study are based on, hence the label Base scoring method. Essentially, this label use does not intend to suggest that this could be a common or special method in the literature (Wege et al., 2025). In this scoring method, the average performance (proportion of the correctly given trials) was calculated for each child and each number. For a child, a number was considered known according to two criteria. First, there had to be three or four correct responses out of four trials; that is, 75% or 100% correct responses. (Note that this is equivalent to the 66% threshold of other studies, because 66% of 4 trials is 2.67 trials; therefore, this threshold would require at least 3 correct responses, similar to the present criterion.) Second, a number was considered as known only if the same set size was not given for other number words more than once (e.g., if three was given correctly when asking for three, and three was also given at least twice when asking for four, then three was categorized as unknown).

Number knowledge was specified as the largest known number, after which the first unknown number follows. In other words, number knowledge is the smallest unknown number minus 1. For example, if the smallest unknown number is 4, then the number knowledge of that child is 3. This calculation method mimics the titration method (i.e., where the number of the next trial depends on the success of the actual trial and number knowledge is specified at the value that children repeatedly fail to give correctly) because in the titration method, when an unknown number is reached in the increasing number series, no further numbers are asked, and the previous number is considered as the limit of the child's knowledge. If number knowledge was at least five, then the child was categorized as a CPknower. (Note that it may be questioned whether these children are indeed CP-knowers (Krajcsi & Reynvoet, 2024), but the present scoring method intends to be similar to the most common practice in the literature.)

PREPRINT 9/21 2. ±1 accepted values. In this scoring method, the Base method was applied with the difference that a response could be considered correct even if it deviated by one from the required number (Le Corre & Carey, 2007). For example, if three was asked, two, three, and four responses were all considered correct responses. Note that here any trials are considered correct when the given value is deviated by one from the required numbers, although in the literature, this rule is usually applied to responses with an incorrect counting procedure (Le Corre & Carey, 2007; Wynn, 1990).

3. 50% correct response threshold.

In this scoring method, the Base method was applied, but a number was considered known not only when three or four out of four (75% or 100%) trials were correct, but also when two out of four trials (50%) were correct (Krajcsi & Fintor, 2023; Piantadosi et al., 2014; Pixner et al., 2018).

4. 4 misgiven number.

Here, the Base method was followed, with the exception that the criterion to give a number for the wrong number word was more liberal. In the Base version, a number was categorized as known only if the same number was not given more than once for other number words.

In this scoring method, to categorize a number as unknown, giving the number for a wrong number word was required at least four times instead of twice (Wege et al., 2025).

5. Bayesian scoring method.

The Bayesian scoring method proposed by Negen and his colleagues (Negen et al., 2011) was applied. This method considered the given numbers in a gradual way, providing the number knowledge index based on a model utilizing the assumed priors of the children and the data provided by the experimenter's instruction. Note that this method works entirely differently compared to other methods; therefore, many of the parameters of other scoring methods (such as the number of accepted or misgiven values) are irrelevant here.

6. Combination of scoring parameters.

To investigate a potential accumulation of the scoring parameters, all changes against the Base version (except the Bayesian method, which builds upon a fundamentally different scoring method) were applied at the same time. The combination was compiled so that the potential difference in number knowledge could be maximized. In this combined scoring method, the Base scoring method was followed, but the response was considered correct even if it deviated by one from the requested value (±1 accepted values), the number was considered known even if two out of four trials were correct (50% correct response threshold), and the number was considered correct even if the same value was given for other number words four times (4 times misgiven number). In other words, along with the Base scoring method, the ±1 accepted values, 50% correct response threshold, and the 4 misgiven number methods were applied at the same time.

3.3 Intraclass correlation of the scoring methods

Intraclass correlation (ICC) indices can be used to evaluate the coherence between different raters or methods to describe a to-be-measured attribute. Here, the recommended notation of McGraw and Wong (1996) is used instead of the widely used notation of Shrout and Fleiss (1979). In the current case, it is assumed that, in a study applying the GaN task, only one of the calculated indices is used, and based on the analyses above (section Mean changes across scoring methods), it is assumed that the calculation method has an effect. Based on these considerations, the ICC(A,1) and the ICC(C,1) indices are relevant. Following the recommendation of Liljequist and his colleagues (2019), both of these indices are reported here because they highlight different aspects of the data. The absolute agreement (A) index considers whether different raters or methods provide the same value for the same case; this index is parallel with the analyses where the means of the different calculation method indices were compared. The consistency (C) index is less strict and investigates whether the relative position of the cases is PREPRINT 16/21 substantial. For example, the qualitatively different Bayesian method produces relatively large differences in observed number knowledge compared to other scoring methods. Second, this also means that studies that apply different methods may be comparable only in a limited way. Third, these results are warning signs for future studies applying new scoring methods that the validity and reliability of those methods should be considered because even minor variations in the scoring methods may lead to important changes in the observed number knowledge. Even relatively small proportions of children's different categorization may lead to entirely different theoretical conclusions; therefore, these differences should be considered when appropriate scoring methods are selected.

Note that the present results demonstrate only that scoring method matters when number knowledge is assessed, but they can not specify which scoring version is valid (if any). Future validation studies can explore which scoring methods may describe the development more appropriately.

Note again that the size of the effects of these scoring mechanisms also depends on the analysis a study applies. In correlational studies, the role of the suboptimal reliability and validity is only modest. However, in works comparing the performance of children with different number knowledge, the effect could be more severe since the biases can miscategorize a large part of the sample. Also, even if the influence of the scoring method is limited, it is only a single component that makes the reliability and validity of the results worse, being added to other detrimental influences.

Finally, note that the reported effects are not absolute measurements of the effect of the scoring methods. The observable differences depend on several factors. First, the difference in the observed number knowledge depends not only on the scoring methods but also on the sample. For example, a sample where older CP-knowers are more frequent will reveal smaller changes compared to a sample with fewer CP-knowers because stable CP-knowers are less likely to be categorized differently as a function of the scoring method. Therefore, the statistical analyses reported here are not the property of the scoring methods in themselves, but the combined property of the scoring methods and the sample. Second, the observed differences may depend on other properties of the task or the scoring methods. In other words, the scoring method-dependent change and other properties of the task or scoring may interact. For example, the present analyses assumed that there are no five-knowers, six-knowers, etc., but according to recent findings, this assumption may not be as justified as assumed in the majority of the literature (Gunderson et al., 2015; Krajcsi & Fintor, 2023; Mussolin et al., 2012; Posid & Cordes, 2018; Rousselle & Vossius, 2021; Rovero et al., 2025). If five-, six-, or larger number knowers are considered, then some of the scoring methods discussed here may work differently (Figure 1). Overall, the interaction of various properties may modify the exact effect sizes of the changes caused by different scoring methods. To summarize, while the current results can be considered characteristic examples of how scoring methods of the GaN task influence the observed number knowledge, they cannot be absolute measures of these effects.

5 Conclusions

The GaN task is a gold standard for measuring children's initial symbolic number knowledge (Marchand et al., 2022). Still, the task has several variations, and these variations are not necessarily equivalent (Sella et al., 2021; Wege et al., 2025). The present work is the first empirical study to demonstrate that the scoring method of the task influences the observed number knowledge, and the assumption of the equivalence of the scoring methods cannot be justified. These findings are equally important for evaluating previous studies and for planning future studies to measure children's conceptual development of number understanding and to design an improved version of the GaN task, including designing valid scoring methods.

4 General discussion

The current practice of the literature does not consider the possible effects of different scoring methods (Wege et al., 2025); therefore, it implicitly considers the scoring methods equivalent. The present study tested this assumption and investigated whether different versions of the GaN task's scoring methods influence the observed number knowledge. It was found that the scoring methods influence both the absolute values of the number knowledge and the relative position of the participants within the sample. In addition, the effects of the specific scoring variations can be accumulated. The present work is the first empirical and systematic study to demonstrate the effect of scoring methods on observed number knowledge, and this finding does not justify the implicit assumption of the current practice in the literature that various scoring methods can be considered equivalent.

The differences in the observed number knowledge caused by the scoring methods have important consequences on the methods and related theoretical considerations on how children's number knowledge is measured and described. First, in most cases, various scoring methods cannot be correct at the same time; therefore, most of them must be incorrect. This means that most of the studies that apply suboptimal scoring versions have at least partial reliability and validity issues.
