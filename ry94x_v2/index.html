Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard), also Internal Review Board - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term); not IEC followed by a number, which is a technical standard - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee/Commission, in the singular or plural - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee, Bioethics Committee/Commission, Ethikkommission - Ethical Review Authority/Unit/Office/Agency - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Human Research Protection Office/Program, Office for Human Subjects Protection - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee(s) on/for (Medical and Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt - Ethics review that was not needed: Ethics review was not required, did not require further ethical review, exempt from formal human research ethics review, no new institutional ethics review

A template line the authors have not filled in (“[Add ethics approval … here.]”) is not a statement.

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

When a statement is expected

The module sorts each paper by where its data came from (the internal .data_source(); the signs and how the rule was tested are described in R/data-source.R): - own: the authors collected data from people or animals, for this paper, in an earlier study of their own, or (a protocol) will collect them; - existing_records: records about individual people that the authors obtained for this study, such as hospital records or a cohort; - existing_unclear: existing data about people from a source the module could not identify; - open_resource: an open resource any researcher can use, such as UK Biobank or NHANES; - none: no data about people or animals (a review, simulation, commentary, theory, tutorial, coding of published papers, or figures about groups); - uncertain: the signs were missing or pointed both ways; - no_text: the paper has no text.

A statement is expected for own, existing_records and existing_unclear. A paper without a statement in these classes, or in uncertain, is flagged. The module reads the text, and the results of power, stat_p_exact, stat_check, repo_check, data_check and prereg_check when they ran before it (run it after them); without stat_p_exact, it finds the p-values in the text.

On 199 PsyArXiv and medRxiv preprints labelled after the rule was fixed, the module decided right whether a statement is needed for 86.9% (the earlier live-data search: 83.4%). Its main errors: other people’s studies described in the words of a new study, records and specimens described only as counts, and open resources the paper does not call open.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 685 tests with 34 instances of inconsistent reporting, Statcheck correctly detected 34 of them, and incorrectly identified 26. Therefore 0% of true instances were missed, and 43% of detections were false positives. See https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/ for more details of the validation.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on 226 Psychological Science papers with 5,019 hand-coded p-values, 438 of them imprecise (scienceverse/validation, branch stat_p_exact). Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). Of the misses, 13 are starred table notes (“*p < .05”) that the module leaves out on purpose and 11 are p-values it did not find; of the wrong reports, 20 are p-values the ground truth does not contain (such as “ps < .05”) and 6 use ≤ or ≥. These figures measure agreement with the hand-coding, which has known errors in both directions (scienceverse/validation#48).

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on three hand-coded sets of papers (scienceverse/validation: 249 Psychological Science, 250 Economics and 250 MDPI papers). Of 1,783 non-significant p-values in the ground truth, version 0.4.4.3 found 1,719 (3.6% missed), and 94.8% of its 1,814 detections were in the ground truth. How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. A TRO uses them for each trov:TransparentResearchPerformance. * run_results$exit_status: the exit status of the process that ran the script (0 = ended normally); NA when the process did not report one (a time limit, or Docker failing to start the container). * artifacts: one row per file, with stage (deposit: as downloaded from the repository, hashed from the downloaded file; before_run: the run folder just before the first script ran, after metacheck’s own changes, such as rewritten paths and unpacked archives; after_run: only the files the scripts made (status new), changed (changed) or removed (removed), as an unchanged file is the one listed before the run), path (relative to the run folder, as valid UTF-8, a byte of another encoding written as <xx>), size (bytes), sha256, mime_type (by extension, NA when unknown), source (the file’s address in the repository, for deposit). The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). Files metacheck itself copied or made between passes (see modifications, output_copied, file_placed) show in after_run too. A TRO uses the rows as trov:ResearchArtifacts and each stage as a trov:ArtifactArrangement. * modifications$sha256_before, modifications$sha256_after: the hash of the changed script as downloaded and as it ran; NA when the script is not at the same path in both (a script made from an R Markdown file). * environment: the system that ran the code (trov:TransparentResearchSystem in a TRO): metacheck_version, started_at, ended_at, host (R version, platform, operating system), sandbox, network_isolated (TRUE only when every Docker run had --network none; the process sandbox does not stop the network), internet, timeout_per_script, total_timeout, python_local (the Python run outside Docker and its version), and docker: runs (for each kind of run, r_run, r_install, python_run, python_install, the image and the docker run arguments, such as --network none, --read-only, --cap-drop ALL, no-new-privileges, the process limit and the memory and processor limits) and images (for each image, its ID and digests from docker image inspect, its creation time and the R and Python versions its environment declares).

It is NA for files run inside another script, files that are not an analysis, files not read, and a Docker failure. summary_table counts each group (repro_ran_n, repro_code_error_n, repro_dependency_n, repro_time_n, repro_not_checkable_n), and the report’s first sentence gives them. The traffic light reflects the static readiness signals by default; a script in code_error that stopped with an error makes it red, as a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure, and a script in one of the other three groups keeps it from being green. Calls that need a screen, which the run does not have (dev.print(), dev.copy2pdf(), dev.copy2eps(), savePlot(), windows(), win.graph(), x11(), quartz(), View()), are replaced before the run by calls that need none, which modifications records as screen_call_replaced. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Abstract

Attention can be selectively directed to both external sensory inputs and internally generated representations. The relationship between these external and internal attentional states is not fixed but varies between competition, concurrence, and cooperation. However, the conditions that allow for each of these relationships to occur remain poorly understood. We distinguish between the level of brain states and behavior and synthesize evidence for each of these relationships at both levels. Our review shows that shared, capacity-limited mechanisms and opposing computational modes (e.g., encoding vs. retrieval) often produce competitive dynamics, particularly in the presence of simultaneous and incompatible demands. At the same time, several studies have revealed partially independent substrates that permit concurrent selection of internal and external information, especially when controlled, intentional processing is not required. More recently, researchers have started exploring conditions under which external and internal attention can support or facilitate each other, enabling cooperative integration in the service of a single goal. We argue that task demands, degree of control, representational overlap, and the considered timescale jointly determine whether external and internal attention compete, co-occur, or cooperate, and we outline key directions for future work aimed at a more nuanced understanding of how these states jointly shape cognition.

Introduction

Adaptive cognition relies on both external and internal sources of information, stemming from one's immediate sensory environment and from one's memories and self-generated thoughts, respectively (Chun et al., 2011). Attentional selection is the process through which we prioritize information among the abundant flow from these sources, filtering the relevant from the irrelevant to varying degrees of success (Desimone & Duncan, 1995; Nobre & Kastner, 2014). This process has long been studied primarily in the context of external attention, but researchers in the past decades have come to realize the importance of internal attention as well (van Ede & Nobre, 2021; Verschooren & Egner, 2023). In recent years, this distinction between external and internal attention has become a key driver of research on attention (e.g., Chun et al., 2011; Fiebelkorn & Kastner, 2019; Narhi-Martinez et al., 2023; Nobre & Gresch, 2025; Servais et al., 2023; Tarder-Stoll et al., 2020; van Ede et al., 2026; Verschooren et al., 2019; Verschooren & Egner, 2023).

Several research lines highlight the tension that arises due to the need to prioritize either incoming sensory signals or internally generated information, which places external and internal attentional states partly in opposition (Narhi-Martinez et al., 2023) given their use of overlapping resources. We define a cognitive resource as a limited supply of mental or neural capacity (dedicated to computations and/or representations) that is used by many different tasks (see Logan, 2026; Musslick & Cohen, 2021; Navon & Gopher, 1979 for discussions). Because externally and internally oriented states inherently depend on different sources of information but draw partly from the same resources, they have been considered to be mutually opposed: cognitive and neural operations primarily prioritize one state or the other at a given moment in time (Honey et al., 2017). However, several recent findings challenge the purely antagonistic nature of this relationship and suggest that these different attentional states can co-occur independently and even cooperate (Bornstein et al., 2023; Huang et al., 2025; Liu & van Ede, 2025; Poskanzer, Tarder-Stoll et al., 2025). In this chapter, we address what we view as a critical area within this field that deserves a more prominent place on the research agenda and would benefit from a better understanding: the conditions under which external and internal attention operate in a competitive, concurrent, or cooperative manner. To address this question, we first give a broad overview of various conceptual frameworks and empirical findings regarding external and internal attention. We then discuss the different ways that internal and external states may interact at the level of the brain and in behavior.

The attentional state space

To provide a grasp on the burgeoning research on external and internal attention, we build on a recent state space for attention (Verschooren et al., 2026), which captures the different states of attention along two axes: direction (external vs. internal) and goal-Relevance (on-task vs. off-task). The direction axis is our primary focus, as it captures the distinction between external and internal states that is the topic of this chapter. We will, however, also refer to the goal-relevance axis to increase precision in characterizing the competitive, concurrent, and cooperative relationships between external and internal attention, which are in part dependent on this second axis. This axis delineates on-and off-task attention in reference to a proximate goal, i.e., the goal that has to be completed next. If attention is directed to information that is aligned with a proximate goal, we define it as on-task attention. Otherwise, we define it as off-task attention.

When combined orthogonally, the direction and goal-relevance axes yield four distinct attentional states: on-task external, on-task internal, off-task external, and off-task internal (Fig 1). For example, when grocery shopping, we might first retrieve the next item on our memorized list (on-task internal), after which we scan the aisle for the product in question (on-task external). While doing so, however, we might become distracted by a shiny display promoting a new product (off-task external) or by the memory involving one of our dinner guests (off-task internal). We use these four states to develop a nuanced overview of the relationship between external and internal attention.

Our state space echoes frameworks proposed within the mind-wandering literature, which cross dimensions of task-relatedness with stimulus-dependent and stimulus-independent thought (e.g., Stawarczyk et al., 2011). However, these frameworks do not address on-task internal states; instead, task-relatedness in the internal domain is considered only with reference to task-related interference rather than an internally oriented task. Our framework generalizes beyond the classification of spontaneous thought content and incorporates recent work on transitions between on-task external and on-task internal states (Chun et al., 2011; Fiebelkorn & Kastner, 2019; Narhi-Martinez et al., 2023; Nobre & Gresch, 2025; Servais et al., 2023; Tarder-Stoll et al., 2020; van Ede et al., 2026; Verschooren et al., 2019; Verschooren & Egner, 2023). The on-task external state is arguably the state people bring to mind most intuitively when thinking of attention. This attentional state, capturing the selective prioritization of information from the environment that is aligned with the proximate goal, has been at the center of attention research since its early days and has received ample empirical interrogation (Driver, 2001; Nobre & Kastner, 2014).

Classical paradigms investigating on-task external attention make use of cueing to activate and direct an external focus of attention. One paradigmatic example is the Posner cueing task, in which endogenous or exogenous cues are used to direct attention to a spatial location prior to the appearance of a target (Posner, 1980). What is robustly observed in such tasks is that participants respond significantly faster and are less error-prone when a cue is valid (i.e., directs attention to the location where the target will appear) compared to when the cue is neutral (uninformative) or invalid (directs attention to a different location; Nobre & Kastner, 2014).

External attention can also be directed to task-irrelevant information, i.e., in the off-task external state.

There exists a large and diverse field of research focused on external distraction (Liesefeld et al., 2024), using paradigms such as the flanker task (Eriksen & Eriksen, 1974) or additional singleton task (Theeuwes, 1991 (Theeuwes, , 1992)). Attentional capture for both on-and off-task information can occur through different processes, such as bottom-up (e.g., saliency), top-down (e.g., goal-relevance), and selection history (Awh et al., 2012). More recently, researchers have become interested in situations in which the proximate goal is temporally released to fully engage in off-task external attention in a more self-guided and spontaneous manner (Christian et al., 2024).

Even though attention to external information has been historically central to the research field, since the early 2000s it has become increasingly clear that attentional selection also applies to the prioritization of internal representations (Cowan, 1999; Griffin & Nobre, 2003; Landman et al., 2003; Oberauer, 2002). Internal attentional selection is central to both working memory and long-term memory, which are the systems responsible for making information available that is no longer present in the environment (Cowan, 2017; Tulving, 2002). Long-term memory allows us to encode, store, integrate, and retrieve information across larger timespans (e.g., episodic long-term memory harbours the events you experienced throughout your life; Tulving, 1983). Working memory is more fragile and of shorter duration, requiring continued attention for maintenance, but simultaneously allowing for online manipulation of stored information (e.g., during arithmetics; Cowan, 2017). Embedded models of working memory integrate both systems and place a central role for internal attention in retrieving and maintaining information from and between both systems (Cowan, 1999; Oberauer, 2002). Working memory serves as an interface between externally available and internally generated information (Chun et al., 2011) and can be updated based on perceptual or long-term memory information (Verschooren, Kessler, & Egner, 2021). That said, information maintained in working memory is necessarily internal, as it does not rely on current sensory input. In this regard, it is important to distinguish the stages of working memory: encoding perceptual information into working memory requires external attention, but maintenance and retrieval (once the information is no longer present) requires internal attention.

Using both long-term and working memory, internal attention can be leveraged for many internallydirected cognitive functions, such as simulation of future events, mental imagery, and dreaming (Dixon et al., 2014).

As for external attention, a similar division between on-and off-task states applies to internal attention.

On-task internal attention captures the selective prioritization of information that is goal-aligned.

Empirically, on-task internal attention is often investigated with tasks that require individuals to retrieve information from memory. This can be assessed with memory tests that ask individuals to voluntarily recall previously studied information or judge whether presented items were studied before (Ciaramelli, Grady, et al., 2008; Hong, Moore, et al., 2023; Long, 2023; Servais, Hurter, et al., 2023; Wagner, Shannon, et al., 2005). On-task internal attention is also commonly investigated with the retro-cuing task (Griffin & Nobre, 2003; Landman, et al., 2003). The retro-cuing task follows a similar logic as the Posner cueing task, but flips the order of stimulus presentation and cueing: after the stimuli have been presented and removed, and while the stimuli are actively maintained in working memory, a cue appears that highlights a subset of the actively maintained stimulus set. Performance benefits for valid cues compared to invalid or neutral cues are consistently observed, further evidencing internal attention's functional role of selective prioritization ((Myers et al., 2017; Souza & Oberauer, 2016). Off-task internal attention is often investigated in research on mind wandering and related internal cognitive states that are not aligned with the proximate goal (Christoff et al., 2016; Dixon et al., 2014; Mittner et al., 2016; Smallwood & Schooler, 2006 , 2014). We use a content-based definition of off-task internal attention, defined as mental content that is misaligned with the proximate goal. We have argued elsewhere (Verschooren et al., 2016) that this operationalization can capture both associative, freely moving forms (e.g., jumping from one thought to the next without elaboration or structure; Christoff et al., 2016) and more directed forms (e.g., elaborating on a single task-irrelevant thought; Mittner et al., 2016) of off-task internal attention (Verschooren et al., 2026).

One popular approach to measure off-task internal attention is through the use of thought probes, which interrogate participants at random intervals about the quality and focus of their attention, often during dull tasks that require limited attentional engagement (Smallwood & Schooler, 2014). A robust finding in this literature is that participants report more off-task attention the longer they perform a task (Esterman et al., 2013; Esterman & Rothlein, 2019). The distinction between on-task internal and offtask internal attention is relevant in real-world applications: Casner and Schooler (2014) reported that airline pilots spend much of their in-flight cognition retrieving task-relevant information such as the flight plan (i.e., on-task internal), but that actual mind wandering (i.e., off-task internal) was associated with performance costs. Both on-task internal and off-task internal states are associated with perceptual decoupling, characterized by a blunted response in sensory cortex, to allow for optimal engagement with internally-generated information (Kam & Handy, 2013; Kam et al., 2022; Verschooren et al., 2025).

Interplay Between Internal and External Attention

There are different ways in which these external and internal attentional states can relate: they can operate competitively, concurrently, or even cooperatively. Adding complexity to these types of interactions, competition at one level (e.g., brain states) can give rise to the appearance of cooperation at another level (e.g., behavior). For example, recent work by Poskanzer, Tarder-Stoll, and colleagues (2025) investigated the relationship between (externally oriented) memory encoding and (internally oriented) memory retrieval on a trial-by-trial basis. Prior research using electrophysiological studies in both humans and non-human animals has highlighted that encoding and retrieval rely on distinct, computationally competitive brain states (Hasselmo, 2005; Kerrén et al., 2018; Tarder-Stoll et al., 2020). Yet Poskanzer, Tarder-Stoll et al. observed that behaviorally, when participants successfully retrieved memories to predict upcoming scenes, they were also more likely to encode a novel image that was presented on that trial. This result (reviewed in more detail below) indicated that these neurally competitive processes can operate in a way that gives the appearance of cooperation at the level of overt behavior, because the success of the retrieval state increased the likelihood of success for the encoding state. Competition at the neural level could lead to apparent cooperation at the level of behavior because the relevant timescales of operation differ: rapid, efficient switching between competitive neural states can allow both states to succeed at their goal at the slower timescale of behavior. This speculative proposal is based on recent results (such as Poskanzer, Tarder-Stoll, et al), and additional research is required to establish and characterize how competition at the neural level can lead to cooperation at the behavioral level. To start addressing this seeming paradox, we distinguish between the level of brain states and the level of cognition and overt behavior.

Moreover, we acknowledge that few cases are perfect examples of competition, concurrence, or cooperation. For example, although resource sharing and overlap in selection mechanisms are sometimes used to define an interaction as competitive (Narhi-Martinez et al., 2023; Verschooren, Schindler, et al., 2019; Verschooren & Egner, 2023), resource sharing can also lead to cooperative interactions (e.g., Dijkstra et al., 2025). As such, while resource sharing might set the stage for competition and provide a necessary condition, it is not a sufficient condition. Specifically, resource sharing will lead to competition when there are demands that are simultaneous and opposing, e.g., when requiring high levels of control or intentionality (Dixon et al., 2014) or for brain areas that have fundamentally opposing computations (Hasselmo, 2025), or direct attention in opposing ways (Verschooren, Schindler, et al., 2019 , 2021). Whether competition, concurrence, or cooperation is observed may also depend on whether the internal and external states cross task relevance (one off-task, one on-task) or are aligned on task relevance (e.g., both on-task). Their respective positions on the goalrelevance axis may determine the level of control required for the two states, whether they overlap in their representations, and the timescale at which the states unfold. For example, high levels of control and representational overlap may be more likely when both states are on-task. We discuss this below as we consider how goal relevance may affect resource demands, degree of control, and representational overlap to shape the amount of cooperation or competition. In general, more empirical research and conceptual clarity into these different possible relationships is required, for which this chapter provides a first step. We will first focus on brain states and further below, we will discuss the interplay between external and internal attention at the level of behavior.

Brain States for Internal and External Attention

In the brain, external and internal attention are best characterized as sometimes competitive and sometimes independent, depending on task demands and the level of description. At a coarse-grained, resource-based level, they often behave competitively, but at a mechanistic level, there is evidence for partially independent components specialized for internal versus external operations. Moreover, while there is more limited evidence available, some studies show support for cooperative interactions between external and internal brain states as well. This question has been gaining traction in the literature; for example a recent proposal addresses this contradiction with a dual-process Attentional Switching Framework (Kam Spreng, et al., 2026). This framework posits a domain-general switching mechanism that is a shared resource between external and internal states, and domain-specific, resourceindependent mechanisms maintaining the content within these states. Here, we review the existing evidence for each of the three possible relationships between these external and internal states (competition, concurrence, and cooperation).

Competition

One of the main questions that came to the forefront early on in research on on-task external and ontask internal attention is whether they rely on overlapping neural mechanisms (Gazzaley & Nobre, 2012; Kiyonaga & Egner, 2013; Verschooren, Schindler, et al., 2019). Researchers tackled this question by comparing neural signatures of attention for (external) pre-cueing vs. (internal) retro-cuing paradigms. These studies found that on-task external and on-task internal attention were associated with overlapping neural activity (Gazzaley & Nobre, 2012). More recent work using decoding in human participants (Zhou et al., 2022) and non-human primates (Panichello & Buschman, 2021) further confirmed this overlap in neural mechanisms. Zhou and colleagues (2022) showed that multivariate patterns in occipital, parietal, and to a lesser extent frontal cortex encoded the selected item both when attention was shifted in external perception and in internal working memory, suggesting an overlapping mechanism that leads to competition when opposing demands are encountered. Panichello and Buschman (2021) showed that populations in visual, parietal, and prefrontal cortex implement selection within working memory using the same kind of priority coding that supports classic spatial attention to external stimuli, similarly implying a limited, shared resource between them. More indirectly, researchers have shown that decoding accuracy of an item held in working memory is impaired by the presentation of an irrelevant stimulus (Bettencourt & Xu, 2016; van Moorselaar et al., 2018), concurrent demands on external attention (Kiyonaga et al., 2017), or when one or the other is prioritized (Rademaker & Serences, 2024), which is also suggestive of overlap -and potential resource competition -between the neural operations used to process internally maintained and externally presented information.

From a different perspective, memory researchers have arrived at similar conclusions and have argued for a competitive relationship between encoding and retrieval modes (Hasselmo, 2025; Hasselmo, Schnell, and Barkai 1995; Hasselmo, Bodelón, and Wyble 2002; Tarder-Stoll et al., 2020). These encoding and retrieval modes can be viewed as instantiations of on-task external and on-task internal attention, respectively (or off-task when misaligned with the proximate goal). The encoding mode is optimized for processing stimuli as novel information that should be encoded into a new memory trace, whereas the retrieval mode is optimised for processing stimuli as retrieval cues that can be used to bring to mind already stored memories (Herron & Evans, 2018; Servais et al., 2023; Tarder-Stoll et al., 2020; Tulving, 1983). An influential framework in this literature is the Separate Phases of Encoding and Retrieval (SPEAR) model (Hasselmo, et al., 1995; Hasselmo, et al., 2002; Hasselmo, 2025). The main tenet of this model is that the encoding and retrieval modes need to operate separately to allow effective encoding of novel information without interference with pre-existing associative memories. According to this model, there are two main mechanisms through which this separation is ensured. First, encoding and retrieval occur at different phases (i.e., peak vs. trough) of the theta rhythm in the hippocampus (Hasselmo et al., 2002). Work in humans has shown that decoding performance is higher for encoded vs. retrieved stimuli at opposite phases of the theta rhythm (Kerrén et al., 2018 (Kerrén et al., , 2022)). Second, at a slower timescale of seconds, the dominance of the encoding vs. retrieval mode is modulated by acetylcholine (Hasselmo et al., 1992 (Hasselmo et al., , 1995;; Hasselmo & Schnell, 1994). High levels of acetylcholine promote the encoding state at the expense of the retrieval state, whereas low levels of acetylcholine do the reverse. The competition and prominence of the encoding vs. retrieval mode is moreover dependent on the environment, with a retrieval mode being promoted in a familiar environment and an encoding mode in a novel environment, which is mediated by cholinergic activity (Douchamps et al., 2013; Lever et al., 2010). Zhao and colleagues (2026) recently reported evidence for the role of acetylcholine in spontaneous internal-to-external transitions using an fMRI reanalysis of naturalistic think-aloud data.

They found that these transitions were preceded by increased activity that was largest in brain regions with higher cholinergic receptor density (α4β2 nicotinic, M1 muscarinic), based on associations with independent PET atlases. This association was weaker for internal-to-internal transitions and absent when considering brain regions' NMDA and GABAA receptor densities -supporting a preferential role for acetylcholine in mediating switches between internal and external states. As a caveat, this study did not examine switches from external to internal states, which would be predicted to be associated with lower activity of the cholinergic system (Hasselmo et al., 1995).

There is also evidence for negative relationships (anti-correlation) in the BOLD signal for networks engaged in on-task external and off-task internal attention (Dixon et al., 2014; Fox et al., 2005). Mind wandering, a prominent form of off-task internal attention, reflects internally oriented, stimulusindependent thought that draws resources away from online perceptual processing (Smallwood et al., 2011). Work on the internally-oriented default mode network and externally oriented attention systems (e.g., dorsal frontoparietal networks) shows frequent anticorrelations between them, both at rest and during externally demanding tasks, suggesting that activity in the default mode network often rises as externally oriented systems subside and vice versa (Fox et al., 2005). Smallwood and Schooler (2015) review evidence that stimulus-independent thought is accompanied by increased default mode network activity coupled with reduced processing in sensory cortex, consistent with a trade-off between internal and external processing (Kam & Handy, 2013; Kam et al., 2022). As a caveat, these results rest on a comparison of on-task external and off-task internal attention, a comparison that confounds distinctions along the direction and the goal-relevance axes. This confound needs to be further teased apart in future empirical work, for example by also comparing transitions between internal on-task states and internal off-task states (i.e., a transition along the goal-relevance axis within internal states) or off-task external and off-task internal states (i.e., a transition along the direction axis within off-task states; Verschooren et al., 2026).

A final complexity in comparing on-task external and off-task internal states arises because on-task states are often operationalized as goal-directed behaviors, whereas off-task states are often considered to be spontaneous or involuntary. It is therefore important to conduct work that matches on-and offtask states in terms of their intentionality (goal-directed vs. spontaneous; see Dixon et al., 2014). This could be accomplished by comparing on-task internal attention to directed mind wandering, a type of off-task internal attention in which attention is narrowly focused on a single train of thought (Verschooren et al., 2026).

Concurrence

Although much evidence points to competitive neural states for internal and external attention, there are also several findings that indicate a more independent relationship. For example, single-cell recordings in monkey lateral prefrontal cortex show partially independent activity patterns for external and internal attention (Mendoza-Halliday & Martinez-Trujillo, 2017). In humans, Benedek and colleagues (2016) showed that performing internally-oriented tasks involves reduced activation and coupling in dorsal attention and visual areas, along with increased posterior alpha and functional coupling indicating top-down suppression of visual input. They argued that internal attention involves a parietal "core" region of the frontoparietal control network devoted to maintaining internal focus, distinct from both dorsal attention and classic default mode nodes. Rademaker and colleagues (Rademaker et al., 2019) provided further nuance by showing that early visual cortex supports coexisting sensory (external) and mnemonic (internal) representations via distinct neural codes and layers, enabling simultaneous decoding but not true independence, as distractor salience disrupts mnemonic fidelity and behavior. More broadly, indirect evidence highlights situations in which brain states associated with internally and externally oriented states may unfold concurrently (Dixon et al., 2014). Dixon and colleagues (2014) argued that concurrence is more likely when internally and externally directed tasks depend on different brain regions or when one or both tasks can be done relatively automatically when intentionality is low. For example, models of attention have proposed distinct neural systems involved in spontaneous vs. controlled switches of attention (Corbetta et al., 2002; Petersen & Posner, 2012).

However, none of these studies have directly shown that external and internal modes can operate concurrently. The most direct evidence to date in this regard comes from a recent paper by Liu and van Ede (2025), who used a task in which participants had to concurrently select internal representations in visual working memory and external sensory inputs. Specifically, participants were instructed to memorize two colored objects presented either left and right or above and below fixation, then wait for a color change of the central fixation cross that cued which one of these objects would become the current search goal. This search cue (i.e., the color change of the fixation cross) was presented simultaneously with a visual search display containing both remembered objects plus two fillers, so that internal attentional selection (in memory) and external attentional selection (in the visual display) could both begin at the same time. Cooperation

Finally, one can also ask how external and internal brain states might cooperate or facilitate each other in the service of a single goal. This cooperation is more difficult to capture and dependent on the level at which the goal is defined -for high-level goals like survival, all brain states can be seen as cooperative. Despite this challenge, some neural findings can arguably be interpreted as indicating cooperation within the goals of a specific task. One way in which cooperation or facilitation can occur is when two compatible but weak inputs together are summed up in the same brain area to provide a more stable representation. For example, overlap in the visual, parietal, and frontal cortex for external and internal attentional states has been found when comparing external visual perception and internal visual imagery, with differences mainly in the strength, granularity, and temporal dynamics of activation (Dijkstra et al., 2019). Dijkstra and colleagues ( 2025) recently showed that these systems can facilitate each other when judgments of the "reality" of a percept are underpinned by the combined strength of sensory activity generated by either imagery or perception in the fusiform gyrus. Moreover, the degree to which imagery reuses perceptual codes in visual cortex predicts vividness: trials and individuals with more vivid imagery show stronger pattern overlap with perception, especially in early visual areas. Other researchers have used resting-state network connectivity measures to argue that the frontoparietal control network serves as a flexible integrator for external and internal attention (Spreng et al., 2010 (Spreng et al., , 2013)). This network shows strong positive connectivity to both the (internal) default mode network and (external) dorsal attention network, enabling balanced integration via regions like the middle frontal gyrus. Such architecture in principle provides an avenue for cooperative interaction when a single goal is driving both internal and external states. It should be noted, however, that in this restingstate finding, participants did not engage in tasks that simultaneously required an external and internal focus, making their interpretation as cooperative brain states more limited. A related finding in a study that used a task-based design, however, lends support to this potential way that internal and external states can cooperate in the brain (Kragel et al., 2017). Kragel and colleagues (2017) used intracranial recordings in human epilepsy patients to show that a common brain state predicts both encoding success and retrieval success. Although not directly showing cooperation, it nevertheless highlights that the presence of a particular neural state can facilitate internally and externally oriented functions despite their opposing computational demands. Together, this work points to the potential for cooperation, but overall, there is less available research on cooperative (vs. concurrent or competitive) neural relationships. Future empirical and conceptual work in this domain is necessary.

Internal and External Attention in Behavior

We now turn to the relationship between external and internal cognition, as measured through behavior.

Here again, there is evidence for competition, concurrence, and cooperation. As noted above, it is important to keep in mind that the way internal and external states interact at the level of behavior need not imply the same mode at the level of the brain: for example, competition at the neural level can give rise to the appearance of cooperation at the behavioral level (e.g., Poskanzer, Tarder-Stoll, et al., 2025).

Competition

Several strands of research have suggested a competitive relationship between on-task external and ontask internal attention. From a conceptual perspective, such competition is supported by models that argue for an intimate link between (internal) working memory and (external) perceptual attention (Awh et al., 1998; Awh & Jonides, 2001; Cowan, 1995; Gazzaley & Nobre, 2012; Kane et al., 2001; Kiyonaga & Egner, 2013; Oberauer, 2009; Verschooren, Schindler, et al., 2019). Empirically, attention researchers have used interference tasks in which both external and internal attention are taxed simultaneously to determine whether they compete. For example, search templates in working memory automatically guide perceptual attention to matching stimuli, which can lead to costs if the search template matches task-irrelevant information (Olivers et al., 2011; Soto et al., 2006 Soto et al., , 2008)). More recently, it was found that task-related but irrelevant external stimuli that happen to match workingmemory content involuntarily activate the working-memory item as well, with costs for non-matching targets (van Harmelen et al., 2026). It should be noted that both of these research lines also observed benefits when the working memory and perceptual items matched in the task-relevant feature, which speaks to cooperation.

From a different perspective, researchers have become increasingly interested in transitions between on-task external and internal attention (Calzolari et al., 2022; Gresch, Boettcher, Gohil, et al., 2024; Gresch, Boettcher, van Ede, et al., 2024; Poskanzer & Aly, 2023; Verschooren, Liefooghe, et al., 2019).

For example, Verschooren, Liefooghe and colleagues (2019) developed a task in which participants perform a probe-to-target matching task in which they switch on a trial-by-trial basis between external targets (presented on the screen) and internal targets (retrieved from memory). This design creates four conditions of interest: repeat-external, switch-to-external, repeat-internal, and switch-to-internal.

Participants show a robust performance cost (e.g., increased reaction times and error rates) on switch trials compared to repetition trials (Hautekiet et al., 2023; Verschooren et al., 2020 Verschooren et al., , 2025;; Verschooren, Kessler, et al., 2021; Wang et al., 2024 Wang et al., , 2025)), confirming a bottleneck caused by opposing, competitive demands on a shared resource (Kam, Spreng, et al., 2026; Verschooren et al., 2019). Recently, Gresch et al. (2026) directly tested simultaneous competition between on-task external and on-task internal attention. They found that dividing attention between one perceptual and one working-memory item produced an asymmetric trade-off relative to dividing attention within a domain: external report improved while internal report worsened, indicating that external items are boosted while internal items suffer during external/internal competition. Whether external or internal states prevail during competition is an important outstanding research question in this field (Christian et al., 2024; Verschooren et al., 2020 Verschooren et al., , 2023)).

Memory researchers have similarly reported behavioral evidence that suggests a competitive relationship between encoding and retrieval modes. Much of this evidence comes from animal models but an increasing amount comes from studies in humans, some of which have used pharmacological manipulations to assess the effects of cholinergic modulation on encoding and retrieval in behavior (e.g., Atri et al., 2004; Gedankien et al., 2025; Ruiz et al., 2021). Because the acetylcholine neurotransmitter system modulates the strength of encoding vs. retrieval states on the order of seconds, its effects can yield lingering biases that can be detected in behavior (Decker & Duncan, 2020; Duncan et al., 2012; Patil & Duncan, 2018; Tarder-Stoll et al., 2020). Across a series of behavioral experiments, Duncan and colleagues (Duncan et al., 2012; Patil & Duncan, 2018) capitalized on novelty and familiarity as a way to indirectly modulate the cholinergic system and measure effects on behavior, as novelty should increase cholinergic modulation of the hippocampus (Meeter et al., 2004). They showed that the recent presentation of a novel image promotes an encoding state that helps differentiate similar items, whereas the recent presentation of a familiar image promotes a retrieval state that facilitates associative memory. These effects decayed on the order of seconds, consistent with the timescale of cholinergic action. Although these studies did not directly show competition between encoding and retrieval states, they demonstrated that opposing contextual factors (novelty vs. familiarity) differentially promote the success of these states.

Finally, for on-task external and off-task internal states, the mind-wandering literature has reported evidence for a competitive relationship as well. Task performance is typically poorer when attention drifts away from the here-and-now and toward task-unrelated thoughts. For example, mind wandering is reliably associated with slower and more variable responses, more errors, and reduced accuracy in sustained attention tasks such as the Sustained Attention to Response Task (Robertson et al., 1997). In general, higher rates of probe-caught mind wandering predict greater vigilance decrements over time (Esterman & Rothlein, 2019; Fortenbaugh et al., 2017; Smallwood & Schooler, 2015). Here again, the caveat should be made that competition in this context is confounded across both the direction and goalrelevance axis, so future research should focus on teasing these contributions apart (Verschooren et al., 2026). For example, it is an open question whether competition, concurrence, and cooperation are more or less prevalent between on-task external and off-task internal states, compared to when both external and internal attention are on-task.

Concurrence

While ample research has now provided evidence that external and internal attention often compete, other research lines suggest that they can co-occur under certain circumstances. Some of this work focuses on the relationship between perceptual attention and working memory, instances of externally and internally oriented states, respectively. For example, Oberauer (2019) argued that the extreme position, claiming that working memory and perceptual attention are one and the same is almost certainly wrong, as it would imply that external attention always determines the content of working memory and vice versa. Not only can this position be rejected on logical grounds, but empirical findings also show that independence between both systems is possible. For example, several studies have found that externally attending to stimuli can occur without leaving traces in working memory (Chen et al., 2016; Chen & Wyble, 2015a , 2015b; Oberauer, 2018). Furthermore, research with the retro-cuing task introduced above shows that attending to an external stimulus during the retention interval does not strongly affect the retro-cue benefit (Hollingworth & Maxcey-Richard, 2013; Myers et al., 2017; Rerko et al., 2014). In addition, some studies show that even when decoding accuracy for working memory maintenance after the presentation of an external stimulus is reduced, this is not necessarily reflected in behavioral performance (Bettencourt & Xu, 2016; van Moorselaar et al., 2018), again suggesting partial independence. Other studies furthermore suggest independent organizing principles for external and internal attention (Arora et al., 2025), which allows for some independence as well. Finally, while ontask external and off-task internal states often interfere, they can sometimes occur concurrently without interference (Dixon et al., 2014). For example, spontaneous or reflexive external attention (Corbetta et al., 2002) can occur in parallel with spontaneous internal attention, such as mind wandering in the absence of awareness (Bengtsson et al., 2011; Christoff et al., 2009). Similar to the neural findings, the most direct evidence for concurrence comes from a recent study by Liu and van Ede (2025). As introduced above, participants needed to rely on both external and internal selection to complete a visual search task. Their results showed that internal and external attention can develop concurrently and could be used effectively even when the task demands were opposing. That is, performance remained highly effective, with no detectable costs even when internal (working memory goal) and external (search target) selections targeted opposing spatial locations, showing a relatively strong degree of independent processing in both modes. As discussed in the neural section, Wei, Gresch, and Nobre (2026) extended this logic to search and found that search speed was statistically equivalent for targets held in working memory versus targets presented perceptually, replicated across four experiments with different response demands and delays. This work provides further behavioral evidence for parallel search across the two domains.

Cooperation

At the behavioral level, there are different ways in which cooperation between internal and external states can express itself. For example, cooperation is required when switching between our internal model and external feedback during learning (Honey et al., 2017), but it is also evident that our more complex mental states, including creativity, mentalizing, memory-guided attention, and meta-cognition, rely in some way on an effective integration between the external and internal world (Dixon et al., 2014). We see two ways in which such cooperation can be instantiated: two independent states can cooperate by occurring simultaneously, with the operations of one benefiting the other (Ishai & Sagi, 1997) or two competitive processes can cooperate by switching quickly (e.g., Poskanzer, Tarder-Stoll et al., 2025) or sampling from both external and internal sources (Bornstein et al., 2023). The latter case reinforces the benefit of our approach to distinguishing between the neural mechanistic level, where fast, frequent switches can be interpreted as competitive, and the behavioural level, at which the states can appear cooperative when responses to the ongoing tasks are measured at a single, relatively slow timescale.

Research on source confusion for (external) perception and (internal) imagination provides an example of how overlapping external and internal states can support each other (Farah, 1985; Ishai & Sagi, 1997; Neisser, 1976; Perky, 1910). Overlap between imagination and perception poses both potential challenges and potential benefits: the reuse of representations might lead to source confusion, i.e., a misclassification between perception and imagination, but the upside is that simultaneous activation can lead to a stronger representation that can facilitate behavior (Dijkstra et al., 2019). In line with this notion, work using a psychophysical approach showed a facilitator effect of imagination on perception (e.g., Ishai & Sagi, 1997). That is, when participants saw a Gabor target flanked by Gabor masks, the mere act of imagining congruent flankers reduced contrast-detection thresholds for the target, i.e., improved sensitivity relative to a no-imagery baseline. Another example of such cooperation comes from studies investigating how internal and external selection processes can be deployed jointly, which leads to efficient search behavior. During memory-guided search, episodic memory can support visual search by directing external attention to locations that have a learned relevance (Chun & Jiang, 1998; Chun & Turk-Browne, 2007; Favila & Aly, 2026; Summerfield et al., 2006). This is also the case for working memory search-templates, which can facilitate the identification of a target stimulus if the target matches features in memory (Olivers et al., 2011; Soto et al., 2008).

Several recent studies more explicitly looked into the cooperation of external and internal attention (Bornstein, Aly et al., 2023; Huang, et al., 2025; Poskanzer, Tarder-Stoll, et al., 2025). Poskanzer, Tarder-Stoll, and colleagues (2025) investigated the behavioral relationship between predicting and encoding, which can be mapped onto internal retrieval and external encoding modes, respectively.

These modes are viewed as competitive processes at the neural level (Duncan et al., 2012; Long & Kuhl, 2021; Patil & Duncan, 2018; Poskanzer & Aly, 2023; Richter et al., 2016), but the authors provided arguments that competitive neural states can nevertheless be compatible with competitive, independent, or cooperative relationships at the behavioral level. They sought to test which of these three types of relationships between encoding and prediction was present, when both were measured on a trial-by-trial basis behaviorally. To do so, they exposed participants to a predictable sequence of scene categories during the initial phase of the experiment (e.g., beaches followed by forests). In a second phase, they were shown novel category exemplars (e.g., a new beach) and instructed to predict the upcoming image category. Finally, participants were unexpectedly tested on their memory for the novel exemplars that served as the prediction cues in the second phase; this memory test provided a measure of whether encoding was successful or not. The authors found that successful prediction increased the likelihood of successful encoding on a trial-by-trial basis, providing another strand of evidence for the cooperative relationship between external and internal attention in behavior. They argued that this cooperative relationship can arise if successful memory retrieval triggers a switch to an encoding mode. This switch to an encoding mode is adaptive because it both prevents further retrieval that can cause interference, and promotes the encoding of novel information. In this way, mutually opposed neural mechanisms can give rise to coupled success of these mechanisms in behavior. Interestingly, these findings are ostensibly at odds with others reporting that predictive (vs. non-predictive) stimuli are associated with worse encoding, and that evidence of prediction in the brain is associated with worse encoding in behavior (Sherman et al., 2022; Sherman & Turk-Browne, 2020). There are several differences in the design of these studies that might explain these apparently disparate results (see Poskanzer, Tarder-Stoll, et al., 2025 and Sherman, 2026 for discussion), such as whether the higherorder sequence learning was part of a separate experimental phase or not, time provided for encoding/prediction, and whether sequence learning was explicit or implicit. Further empirical interrogation will be necessary to tease apart these different factors, but together these studies highlight that the behavioral relationship between encoding and prediction can be more nuanced than originally thought. These externally vs. internally oriented states may sometimes compete but at other times work cooperatively.

A hint into how prediction and encoding might mutually support each other comes from another recent study (Huang et al., 2025), which tried to resolve an inconsistency on the role of prediction errors in encoding. Some studies report that prediction errors lead to better episodic memory (Bein et al., 2021; Jang et al., 2019; Quent et al., 2022). Other studies, however, show that prediction accuracy instead is associated with better memory performance (Huang et al., 2023), as learning new information is easier when it is consistent with prior knowledge (Bein et al., 2015; Brod & Shing, 2019). Using an innovative design, Huang and colleagues (2025) deconfounded the effect of prediction accuracy from those related to stimulus probability. They found that when participants use schematic knowledge about temporal structure to correctly predict upcoming board-game moves, their later memory for those moves is substantially better. As such, internal structure can act as a scaffold that facilitates encoding, proving another example of cooperation and a potential mechanism through which it operates. Relatedly, in a relatively direct test of cooperation, Bornstein and colleagues (2023) investigated whether participants dynamically sample both memory and sensory information to inform perceptual decision-making when sensory input is noisy. Using drift diffusion modeling, they found that expectations are set by dynamic inference from memory, which starts before the onset of the stimulus and aids in perceptual decisionmaking. As a unifying explanation for their own and related findings, the authors suggest that the brain attempts to reduce uncertainty about its current state by drawing on memories of similar past situations.

As such, this constitutes a strong example of cooperation between external and internal states.

The preceding studies primarily showed evidence for cooperation between on-task external and on-task internal states. A recent case for a cooperative relationship between on-task external and off-task internal attention has also been proposed (Simor, Likar, et al., 2026). Simor and colleagues (2026) propose that one function of mind-wandering may be as a "gatekeeper" between goal-directed, modelbased control and automatic, model-free probabilistic learning. From a neurocompetitive framework, they propose that executive control (frontoparietal/dorsal-attention networks) and implicit extraction of statistical regularities (basal ganglia circuitry) compete for shared resources, such that reduced topdown engagement during mind-wandering releases probabilistic learning from top-down constraints. This idea is supported by observations made by Vékony, Farkas and colleagues (2025), who show that within the same task blocks, self-reported mind-wandering was associated with impaired general visuomotor accuracy but increased sensitivity to implicit probabilities in the task structure (i.e., faster, more accurate responses to high-versus low-probability sequence elements). They further propose that mind wandering promotes transient, local offline-like states (slow waves and sharp-wave ripples occurring during wakefulness) that support rapid consolidation of this newly extracted statistical information.

Discussion, Future Directions, and Conclusion

In the previous sections, we reviewed disparate strands of evidence that show how the relationship between external and internal attention can be either competitive, concurrent, or cooperative. If external and internal attention can operate in these different ways, the question naturally arises under which conditions which relationship prevails. We propose that a clear understanding of these different relationships requires considering the direction axis (i.e., external vs. internal) in coordination with the goal-relevance axis (on-task vs. off-task). A recurring limitation of the reviewed off-task literature is that many contrasts vary both axes of the state space simultaneously. Comparisons of on-task external attention against mind wandering, for instance, cross both the direction axis (external to internal) and the goal-relevance axis (on-task to off-task) at once, making it impossible to determine whether the resulting competition (or lack thereof) reflects the change in direction, the change in goal-relevance, or their interaction. An important future direction is to develop tasks that hold one axis fixed while manipulating the other, e.g., contrasting on-task external with off-task external to isolate a pure goalrelevance effect, or on-task-external with on-task internal to isolate a pure direction effect (e.g., Gresch et al., 2026). This will allow future work to determine which dimension of the state space, or their interaction, is the actual driver of competitive, concurrent, or cooperative relationships between external and internal attention.

We further proposed that researchers must clearly distinguish between the level of the brain and the level of behavior when considering potential relationships between external and internal attention. This theoretical distinction has value because recent work has raised the possibility that competitive interactions at the neural level may lead to the appearance of cooperative interactions at the behavioral level (Poskanzer et al., 2025). This is a novel direction in research on external and internal attention and more empirical work is required to understand how and when neural and behavioral levels can lead to different types of relationships between external and internal states. Relatedly, future work should take into account subjective experiences, which might further dissociate from the behavioral (e.g., response time differences) and neural (e.g., competitive activation; see also Schwartz & Clearly, 2025) levels.

For example, it may seem difficult to balance external attention to the outside world and internal attention to our thoughts, but this tension in our subjective experience need not map on to competition between the underlying neural processes that support external and internal attention.

With these tools for conceptual clarification in mind, we can summarize the main drivers of the different types of relationships observed here and sketch additional important future directions. First, interference -or lack thereof -between external and internal attention seems to be largely task-dependent (Finke, 1986). Tas and colleagues (2016) argued that to understand the nature of interference, the many varieties of attention need to be taken into account, such as feature-vs. object-based attention (Bichot et al., 2005; Tresch et al., 1993; Zhou & Desimone, 2011). Within these different domains, the nature and extent of shared representational codes determines whether external and internal attention operate competitively or can co-occur (Woodman et al., 2001; Woodman & Luck, 2004). Moreover, while resource sharing can be an important factor driving competition, it constitutes a necessary but not sufficient condition. Specifically, when a single goal is present, resource sharing can lead to cooperative interactions as well. Disentangling the role of the features and overlap of the external and internal task presents a first obvious target for future research aiming to clarify the relationship between external and internal attention.

Another important dimension is the degree of control or intentionality required in either the external or internal focus. Oberauer (2019) emphasized that the distinction between controlled and automatic attention is important in this light. The author defines controlled attention as attention that is aligned with our current goal, while automatic attention is directed by forces independent of our current goals.

In this framework, controlled attention is akin to "top-down" influences, while automatic attention is more akin to "bottom-up" influences and those of our learning history (Awh et al., 2012). When an attentional process is controlled (e.g., controlled external attention), it consumes resources that cannot be invested in other processes simultaneously (e.g., controlled internal attention), and thus competition prevails. This also aligns with the intuitive notion that highly-practiced (or automatized) but not novel tasks allow for processing resources to be freed up and used elsewhere -an experienced driver can easily listen to the radio while driving whereas a novice driver cannot (Schneider & Shiffrin, 1977). Dixon and colleagues (2014), when discussing whether external and internal foci operate competitively or co-occur, have similarly highlighted the distinction between controlled and automatic attention, which they framed as the level of intentionality of the attentional process. These authors proposed that controlled external and internal processing streams converge on 'higher-order' brain regions, such as the lateral prefrontal cortex, which represents a diverse array of currently relevant information (Duncan, 2001; Miller et al., 2018; Miller & Cohen, 2001). Most frameworks converge on this argument that intentional and controlled processing are capacity limited (Desimone & Duncan, 1995; Schneider & Shiffrin, 1977; Tamber-Rosenau & Marois, 2016), which leads to a competitive relationship between internal and external states when they both call on this capacity-limited resource (Chun et al., 2011; Dixon et al., 2014; Narhi-Martinez et al., 2023; Oberauer, 2019; Verschooren, Schindler, et al., 2019).

Overall, there seems to be a consensus that whether or not external and internal attention can co-occur depends on the degree of the controlled nature of attention. Crucially, however, intentionality should not be conflated with the goal-relevance axis: both on-task and off-task states can be high or low in intentionality (and hence potential for competition), so these factors should be manipulated independently in future studies.

Finally, different timescales are important to capture the full relationship between external and internal states (Honey et al., 2017). It is important for future studies to consider such differences in timescales when interpreting the relationship between external and internal states, as competition at a fast timescale (e.g., theta oscillations) can be associated with cooperation at a slower timescale (e.g., visual search).

For example, we can productively distinguish between faster brain states and slower behavioral states when interpreting cooperation vs. competition (e.g., Poskanzer, Trader-Stoll, et al., 2025). In general, at the shortest timescale (i.e., simultaneous activation) competition often arises due to resource sharing, while at very long timescales external and internal attention often cooperate (e.g., in the context of highlevel goals related to a specific task or to needs of the organism). To allow for a more fine-grained interpretation of the role of timescales in between these coarse extremes, future research can more prominently and directly consider timing in both neural and behavioral studies by varying the length of a trial or experiment, and mapping how competition vs. cooperation between states varies as a function of the amount of time available to complete a cognitive task.

In sum, we have provided a selective review of research speaking to the different relationships between external and internal attention, organized along the direction and goal-relevance axes introduced at the outset and separated at the level of brain and behavior. Historically, the field has emphasized competitive interactions -between on-task states that share representational codes and across on-task and off-task states -but a growing body of research highlights that concurrence and cooperation can be observed in certain circumstances. Future studies that vary task demands, required levels of control, and timescales of processing will be important for disentangling the factors that determine the nature of the relationship between external and internal attention. These studies will set the stage for a more nuanced understanding of the mind and brain, and the myriad ways that our external and internal worlds interact.
