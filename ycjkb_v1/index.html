Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). Statcheck might still miss one-sided tests that are described in another sentence, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. A TRO uses them for each trov:TransparentResearchPerformance. * run_results$exit_status: the exit status of the process that ran the script (0 = ended normally); NA when the process did not report one (a time limit, or Docker failing to start the container). * artifacts: one row per file, with stage (deposit: as downloaded from the repository, hashed from the downloaded file; before_run: the run folder just before the first script ran, after metacheck’s own changes, such as rewritten paths and unpacked archives; after_run: only the files the scripts made (status new), changed (changed) or removed (removed), as an unchanged file is the one listed before the run), path (relative to the run folder, as valid UTF-8, a byte of another encoding written as <xx>), size (bytes), sha256, mime_type (by extension, NA when unknown), source (the file’s address in the repository, for deposit). The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). Files metacheck itself copied or made between passes (see modifications, output_copied, file_placed) show in after_run too. A TRO uses the rows as trov:ResearchArtifacts and each stage as a trov:ArtifactArrangement. * modifications$sha256_before, modifications$sha256_after: the hash of the changed script as downloaded and as it ran; NA when the script is not at the same path in both (a script made from an R Markdown file). * environment: the system that ran the code (trov:TransparentResearchSystem in a TRO): metacheck_version, started_at, ended_at, host (R version, platform, operating system), sandbox, network_isolated (TRUE only when every Docker run had --network none; the process sandbox does not stop the network), internet, timeout_per_script, total_timeout, python_local (the Python run outside Docker and its version), and docker: runs (for each kind of run, r_run, r_install, python_run, python_install, the image and the docker run arguments, such as --network none, --read-only, --cap-drop ALL, no-new-privileges, the process limit and the memory and processor limits) and images (for each image, its ID and digests from docker image inspect, its creation time and the R and Python versions its environment declares).

run_results$checked says what the run of each script shows about the code, in five groups, so that a script that does not reproduce is kept apart from one the check could not test: ran (it ran to the end); code_error (it did not reproduce: it stopped with an error of the code or the deposit, could not be read, or needs a file the deposit does not hold); dependency (a package or program was not available: a package that could not be installed or was not loaded, a program outside R and Python, a system library, or R crashing in a package’s compiled code); time (it did not finish within the time limit for one script, or was not started because the time for all scripts had run out); and not_checkable (it could not be checked automatically: it needs a person, the internet, a key or another setting of the authors’ computer, one platform such as Google Colab, Windows or OpenSAFELY, more memory than the run allows, a file that was not downloaded, a screen, or a program that runs it, or it is a file of a package of the code that Python runs only as a module of that package). It is NA for files run inside another script, files that are not an analysis, files not read, and a Docker failure. summary_table counts each group (repro_ran_n, repro_code_error_n, repro_dependency_n, repro_time_n, repro_not_checkable_n), and the report’s first sentence gives them. The traffic light reflects the static readiness signals by default; a script in code_error that stopped with an error makes it red, as a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure, and a script in one of the other three groups keeps it from being green. Calls that need a screen, which the run does not have (dev.print(), dev.copy2pdf(), dev.copy2eps(), savePlot(), windows(), win.graph(), x11(), quartz(), View()), are replaced before the run by calls that need none, which modifications records as screen_call_replaced. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Full text

Abstract

Psychological stress is closely associated with mental and physical health, yet existing measures may not fully capture its nature and sources. Guided by the Cognitive Integration Model, we developed the Multidimensional Integrated Stress Scale (MISS), which conceptualizes stress as a perceived imbalance between potential stressors and available resources. The MISS comprises two dimensions, internal needs and external demands, with 22 subdimensions and 135 items. We administered it to 608 Chinese adults (492 community and 116 hospital participants; mean age = 25.62 years). Separate formative models at the dimension and subdimension levels were anchored by global ratings of perceived stress and impact. At the dimension level, internal needs showed a strong conditional association with the latent construct, whereas external demands did not. At the subdimension level, four internal needs and three external demands subdimensions showed significant conditional associations. MISS scores were higher among hospital than community participants, particularly for internal needs (Cohen's d = .58, p < .001). In validation subsamples (n = 66-71), MISS scores correlated moderately with perceived stress, anxiety, and depressive symptoms, but weakly with stressful life events. Test-retest reliability (n = 31) was high for the composite and dimension scores (ICCs = .917-.949), but varied across subdimensions (ICCs = .446-.885). Seven experts generally rated content favorably, although item clarity and relevance warranted improvement. These findings provide preliminary support for the reliability and validity of the MISS. The MISS captures both overall stress and specific sources, helping characterize stress profiles across individuals and populations.

Impact statement

The Multidimensional Integrated Stress Scale (MISS) provides a way to assess psychological stress based on how people perceive unmet internal needs and external demands. In 608 Chinese adults, the scale showed preliminary evidence of reliability and validity. It captures not only overall stress but also specific sources of stress. This approach may help researchers and clinicians better characterize differences in how stress is experienced across individuals and populations.

Psychological stress is a universal human experience with important implications for health and functioning. It has been linked to adverse mental and physical health outcomes, including anxiety, depression, cardiovascular disease, and cancer, as well as reduced productivity (Chrousos, 2009; Sari & Dudija, 2024). Stress also carries substantial societal costs; for example, stress-related costs in the United States have been estimated at $300 billion annually (American Psychological Association & APA Working Group on Stress and Health Disparities, 2017).

Conceptualizations of psychological stress have varied across theoretical traditions.

Within cognitive and appraisal-based formulations, stress is commonly conceptualized as a perceived imbalance between external demands and an individual's coping resources (Lazarus & Folkman, 1984). Notably, appraisal is central because different individuals may experience the same circumstances as more or less stressful. More recently, Gu et al. (2026) extended this view into the Cognitive Integration Model (CIM) by proposing that psychological stress may also arise from unmet internal needs (e.g., physiological needs, safety needs, love and belonging needs, control needs, and certainty needs). Evidence from the broader psychological-needs literature indicates that satisfaction of basic psychological needs is associated with well-being, health, and functioning (Ng et al., 2012; Van den Broeck et al., 2016; Vansteenkiste et al., 2020). Such need-related stress may persist even when overt external demands are limited. In addition to cognitive appraisal, the stress literature has emphasized other aspects of the stress process, including exposure to stressors and stress responses (Epel et al., 2018; Gu et al., 2026). These differences in theoretical emphasis determined the choices for measurement.

Questionnaires for assessing psychological stress vary substantially in their measurement targets. Stressor-exposure measures, such as the List of Threatening Experiences (LTE; Brugha & Cragg, 1990) and the Social Readjustment Rating Scale (SRRS; Holmes & Rahe, 1967), primarily record the occurrence of stressful events and therefore do not directly quantify the psychological stress associated with those experiences.

Appraisal-based measures address this limitation by assessing subjective experience, but they often provide limited information about the specific sources of stress. For example, the Perceived Stress Scale (PSS; Cohen et al., 1983) captures general perceptions of unpredictability, uncontrollability, and overload without identifying the particular needs, demands, or life domains from which these experiences arise. Other instruments offer greater contextual specificity but are typically limited to predefined difficulties or particular settings rather than offering a comprehensive, theoretically organized representation of stress sources.

Internal psychological needs have been especially underrepresented: Gu et al. (2026) found that only 3 of 67 definitions of psychological stress explicitly referred to internal needs and only 7 of 73 stress questionnaires included related content.

Existing instruments also provide limited characterization of the temporal context and pattern of stress experience. Many focus on relatively recent periods, such as the past week, month, or year, whereas stress may vary across time as stressors and available resources change. The meaning of experiences changes across life stages and role transitions (Elder Jr, 1998; Pearlin & Skaff, 1996). Individual stress experiences may also differ not only in intensity but in frequency and duration (Slavich, 2019). Recurrent or prolonged experiences may therefore represent a different stress burden from brief or isolated experiences of similar intensity (Guidi et al., 2021).

To address these gaps, we developed the Multidimensional Integrated Stress Scale (MISS), which assesses psychological stress as a subjectively perceived imbalance between potential stressors and available resources. Instead of measuring stressors and resources separately, the MISS operationalizes this imbalance through individuals' appraisal of specific need-and demand-related experiences. Building on our CIM framework (Gu et al., 2026), these sources are organized into two complementary dimensions, internal needs and external demands, each further divided into subdimensions. The MISS also assesses stress across major life stages and captures the intensity, frequency, and duration of individual stress experiences. By integrating these features, the MISS enables a more fine-grained characterization of psychological stress by capturing source-specific heterogeneity that global stress scores may obscure. We further performed a preliminary evaluation of its reliability and validity in Chinese adults. Because the MISS dimensions and subdimensions are conceptualized as distinct sources that jointly contribute to psychological stress, their structural relations were evaluated using a formative measurement framework (Bollen & Lennox, 1991; Diamantopoulos & Winklhofer, 2001; Jarvis et al., 2003).

Methods

Scale development

Conceptual framework

The MISS was developed within the CIM (Gu et al., 2026), which conceptualizes psychological stress as a perceived imbalance between stressors and available resources (see Figure 1, Panel a). Guided by this framework, the MISS assesses source-specific perceived stress associated with two broad domains: internal needs and external demands. The MISS was specified formatively because its dimensions and subdimensions represent distinct, noninterchangeable sources that may contribute differently to overall psychological stress (Bollen & Lennox, 1991; Diamantopoulos & Winklhofer, 2001; Jarvis et al., 2003).

Accordingly, their covariance was not assumed to arise from a single underlying latent cause.

Domain and Life-Course Structure

Specifically, the MISS domain structure was developed through a comprehensive review and integration of existing theories and measures of psychological stress and was prespecified as comprising two overarching dimensions: internal needs and external demands (see Figure 1, Panel b). The internal needs dimension also drew on Maslow's hierarchy of needs while extending beyond it to incorporate additional needs considered relevant to psychological stress. It comprised 15 subdimensions: physiological needs, safety needs, health needs, love and belonging needs, self-esteem and respect needs, cognitive needs, selfactualization needs, aesthetic needs, transcendence needs, control needs, stability needs, certainty needs, personal and psychological space needs, work-related needs, and learningrelated needs. The external demands dimension comprised seven subdimensions: workrelated demands, learning-related demands, demands from children, demands from parents, demands from a partner, demands from other people, and societal demands. Descriptions of the dimensions and subdimensions are provided in Supplementary Table S1.

To assess stress across the life course, the questionnaire distinguished three life stages using age boundaries that are socially salient in the Chinese context and broadly correspond to major role transitions, including entry into adulthood and the conventional transition from working life to retirement. Separate reports were obtained for Stage 1 (ages 0-18 years), Stage 2 (ages 19-54 years for females and 19-59 years for males), and Stage 3 (≥55 years for females and ≥60 years for males).

Item development and refinement

Item development drew on two sources: (1) a targeted review of commonly used stress measures to identify candidate content and item formulations for the prespecified domains, and (2) in-depth interviews with hospital-and community-based adults across a broad age range to elicit culturally salient stress experiences. Information from both sources was synthesized into candidate items, redundant content was removed, and each item was mapped a priori to its corresponding dimension and subdimension. The item pool was then refined through pilot administration and preliminary expert consultation to improve wording, reduce redundancy, and clarify item-domain correspondence. The resulting 135-item study version was used in all subsequent evaluation and validation procedures (Supplementary Table S2).

Item format and response structure

Each item was designed to capture stress as a subjective experience. Respondents first rated the intensity of each experience using an 11-point bipolar Likert-type scale ranging from -5 (very distressing) to +5 (very satisfying), with 0 indicating neither distressing nor satisfying. Frequency and duration were treated as temporal characteristics of an experience already appraised as distressing rather than as independent indicators of stress; an experience was not considered stressful solely because it occurred frequently or persisted for a long time.

When the intensity rating indicated distress, respondents additionally rated frequency and duration using six-point Likert-type scales scored from 0 to 5 to characterize recurrence and persistence, respectively.

Study design and participants

Recruitment and study procedures

This study combined an online community sample with an onsite hospital-based sample recruited between 2025 and 2026. Participants were classified by recruitment sources.

Hospital participants were recruited from psychiatric departments of the Second People's Hospital of Hunan Province and Xiangya Hospital of Central South University; all had a documented psychiatric diagnosis. Community participants were recruited online and were not treated as a psychiatrically healthy control group because some of the participants reported histories of mental health conditions. Self-reported disease histories were not used to define group membership because they were potentially incomplete and were not independently verified.

Community participants completed the survey on mobile devices, whereas hospital participants completed the assessment onsite with standardized instructions from trained study staff. Participation was voluntary, modest monetary compensation was provided, and all measures were administered in Chinese. The study was approved by the ethics committees of the School of Life Sciences and Xiangya Hospital, Central South University (Approval Nos. 2023-1-27 and 2025091567, respectively), and Hunan Provincial Second People's Hospital (Approval No. 2024K017). All participants provided informed consent before participation.

Eligibility and screening

Participants were eligible if they were aged 18 years or older, had sufficient cognitive capacity to understand and complete the questionnaire, could complete the assessment independently, and provided informed consent. Additional exclusion criteria for the hospital sample were severe cognitive impairment, acute psychiatric instability (e.g., acute mania or active psychotic symptoms), or severe physical illness or an acute medical condition that could interfere with participation. Screening was conducted by trained staff using onsite observation and brief clinical information when available.

Sample size and power considerations

The formative framework was evaluated at two levels, with 2 dimension-level predictors and 22 subdimension-level predictors. Sample size planning used a multipleregression approximation based on the 22-predictor model, which yielded the larger required sample size. Assuming α = .05, 80% power, and an effect size of 𝑓2 = .1, the minimum required sample size was approximately N = 235; we therefore targeted at least N = 300 to accommodate incomplete data and planned sensitivity analyses. For known-groups validity analysis comparing hospital and community participants, at least 64 participants per group were targeted to provide approximately 80% power to detect a medium standardized mean difference (d = .50) using a two-sided independent-samples t test at α = .05. For external validity analyses, approximately 85 participants were estimated to provide 80% power to detect a medium correlation (r = .3) using a two-sided test at α = .05. For the retest analysis, we targeted approximately 30-35 participants to provide an initial assessment of test-retest reliability.

Data collection and measures

MISS administration

The MISS battery consisted of (1) demographic items, (2) the 135 core MISS stress items, (3) two global single-item anchors assessing overall lifetime stress and its impact, and (4) embedded quality-check items for data-quality screening (Supplementary Table S3).

Demographic and role-status information (sex, age, marital status, parental status, education, income, and occupation) was collected for sample characterization, with selected variables also used to determine the applicability of relevant MISS sections and reduce respondent burden. Participants received standardized instructions before completing the MISS.

Completion took approximately 20 minutes.

Validation measures

Additional measures were administered to evaluate the performance of MISS using existing instruments that measure perceived stress, stressor exposure, and stress-related psychological outcomes. These measures were completed by 73 participants (52 community and 21 hospital participants). Because some questionnaires were incomplete, analytic sample sizes ranged from 66 to 71 across correlation analyses.

The assessment included the following measures: 1) Convergent validity was assessed using the 10-item Perceived Stress Scale (PSS-10; Cohen, 1988), which measures perceived stress over the past month. Positive associations with MISS scores were expected. 2) Associations with stressor exposure were examined using a brief checklist of six major stressful life events (SLE) experienced during the past two years, adapted from the UK Biobank assessment protocol (Data-Field 6145). Because external demands constitute one dimension of the MISS, higher MISS scores were expected to be associated with greater recent exposure to major stressful life events. The events included serious illness, injury, or assault to oneself; serious illness, injury, or assault to a close relative; death of a spouse or partner; death of a close relative; marital separation or divorce; and major financial difficulties. 3) Associations with anxiety and depressive symptoms were examined using the Self-Rating Anxiety Scale (SAS; Zung, 1971) and Self-Rating Depression Scale (SDS; Zung, 1965), respectively. Both scales assess symptom severity over the past week, and positive associations with MISS scores were expected.

Separately, two global single-item measures were administered immediately after the MISS to provide criterion anchors for subsequent validation analyses. (1) Lifetime perceived stress was rated on an 11-point scale from 0 ("no stress") to 10 ("extreme stress"), referring to the participant's overall stress from birth to the present. (2) Overall perceived impact of stress experiences was rated on an 11-point bipolar scale from -5 ("completely negative impact") to +5 ("completely positive impact"), covering emotional state, daily life, and physical health.

Expert content validity assessment

Seven experts were recruited through advertisements based on their professional or research backgrounds in mental health and psychology, representing the fields of psychiatry, psychological counseling, and psychological research. The 135-item study version was evaluated using two structured rating forms to assess content validity (DeVellis, 2017; Lawshe, 1975) and identify areas for further refinement. At the item level, experts rated clarity (1-5), relevance to the intended dimension or subdimension (1-5), necessity (1 = not necessary,2 = useful but not necessary, and3 = essential), and the accuracy of the prespecified item-to-subdimension assignment (correct, better fits another dimension or subdimension, or uncertain). Experts could also provide suggestions regarding item wording and classification. At the domain level, experts evaluated each of the 22 subdimensions for potentially missing content, redundancy, and ambiguous boundaries with other subdimensions. They could additionally identify missing content, redundant items, and recommended reassignments. The two rating forms are provided in Supplementary Tables S4 and S5.

Statistical Analyses

Because no participants in the final sample had reached Stage 3 (≥55 years for females and ≥60 years for males), all primary analyses used lifetime MISS scores derived from Stage 1 (ages 0-18 years) and Stage 2 (ages 19-54 years for females and 19-59 years for males). To characterize life-stage-specific variation underlying these aggregated scores, we additionally conducted supportive analyses examining shared and stage-specific patterns across Stages 1 and 2, as described below.

We specified the MISS as a formative measurement system in which distinct stress sources and domains jointly define the construct rather than function as interchangeable manifestations of a common latent factor (Bollen & Lennox, 1991; Diamantopoulos & Winklhofer, 2001; Jarvis et al., 2003). High intercorrelations among formative indicators are not required, because each represents a distinct component of the construct and omitting a relevant domain would alter its content. Observed MISS scores were calculated using the prespecified scoring algorithm described below; the formative SEM was used to evaluate how the prespecified dimension-and subdimension-level scores were jointly related to the criterion-anchored latent MISS construct.

Overall model fit was evaluated using the robust χ² test and four approximate fit indices (Hu & Bentler, 1998; Kline, 2016). The χ² test assessed exact model fit. The Comparative Fit Index (CFI) and Tucker-Lewis Index (TLI) assessed fit relative to an independence model. To evaluate the incremental contribution of each dimension beyond the other, the full model was compared with reduced single-dimension models using changes in R² and robust χ² difference tests.

The primary formative models were estimated using robust maximum likelihood (MLR) estimation. To assess sampling uncertainty, models were fitted separately using maximum likelihood (ML) estimation with 2,000 nonparametric bootstrap resamples. Biascorrected 95% confidence intervals were calculated for the unstandardized formative path coefficients linking dimension-and subdimension-level scores to the latent MISS construct and for the paths linking this construct to the two global criterion anchors.

Because multicollinearity among formative indicators can increase uncertainty in estimated unique contributions, variance inflation factors (VIFs) were examined for the 22 subdimension scores. VIF values greater than 5 were treated as indicating potentially problematic collinearity (Hair et al., 2021).

Known-groups validity

Known-groups validity is a form of construct validity that examines whether a measure can distinguish between groups expected to differ on the construct being measured.

We assessed known-groups validity by comparing MISS scores between participants recruited from psychiatric hospital settings and those recruited from the community, expecting higher MISS scores in the hospital group. Welch's t tests compared the MISS composite, dimension, and subdimension scores, with effect sizes summarized using Cohen's d. Wilcoxon rank-sum tests were additionally performed as a sensitivity analysis. For the 22 subdimension comparisons, p values were adjusted using the Benjamini-Hochberg procedure to control the false discovery rate (FDR). Additional linear regression models adjusted for sex and age to assess whether the observed group differences were sensitive to these demographic characteristics.

Test-retest reliability

To assess temporal stability, participants in the retest subsample completed the MISS again after approximately 2-3 weeks. Test-retest reliability was evaluated using two-way absolute-agreement, single-measure intraclass correlation coefficients (ICCs) with 95% confidence intervals for the composite score, the two dimension scores, and the 22 subdimension scores. Paired-samples t tests examined mean differences between administrations. For the 22 subdimension comparisons, p values were adjusted using the Benjamini-Hochberg procedure to control the FDR.

Life-stage analyses

Life-stage analyses examine whether reported stress levels differ across developmental stages. To examine shared and stage-specific patterns, scores from the two life stages represented in the sample were compared using paired Wilcoxon signed-rank tests and Spearman correlations. Known-groups and external-criterion analyses were repeated separately for each life stage using the procedures described above, and differences between corresponding stage-specific criterion correlations were evaluated by bootstrap resampling.

False discovery rate correction was applied where appropriate.

Data quality control and record screening

To improve the quality of self-reported data, we applied a predefined quality-control (QC) procedure based on response accuracy on five QC items and completion time. Records with incorrect responses to at least three of the five QC items (error rate ≥ 3/5) were flagged as low quality. In the community sample, records with completion times at or below the 5th percentile of the community sample were additionally flagged as potentially indicating insufficient engagement. Records meeting either applicable criterion were excluded.

After QC-based exclusions, duplicate records in the community sample were removed in two sequential steps: duplicate participant identifiers were addressed first, followed by duplicate IP addresses among the remaining records. Within each set of duplicates, one record was retained according to a prespecified priority rule: fewer QC errors, longer completion time, and earlier submission time, in that order.

Rule-based checks were applied to identify implausible or internally inconsistent demographic reports. Records were flagged for review if they met any of the following criteria: (1) education level was inconsistent with age (e.g., reporting a university degree before age 15); (2) marriage/partnership or parenthood was reported before age 18; or (3) monthly income of ≥5,000 CNY was reported by participants younger than 18 years or by students. Separately, all participants younger than 18 years were considered ineligible and automatically excluded. Other flagged records were retained after further review and verification.

External-demand subdimensions provide incremental model information

At the subdimension level, the full formative SEM including all 22 subdimensions showed good overall fit, χ²(21) = 26.64, p = .183, robust CFI = .988, robust TLI = .975, robust RMSEA = .018, and SRMR = .012. The anchor loadings were λ = .429 for single-item stress and λ = .639 for single-item impact, corresponding to R² values of .184 and .408, respectively. The full model explained 88.9% of the variance in latent MISS. The internal needs-only model retained most of this explanatory power (R² = .853), whereas the external demands-only model showed substantially lower explanatory power (R² = .567) and poor fit (Table 2). Consistent with this asymmetry, adding the internal needs subdimensions to the external demands-only model markedly improved fit, Δχ²(15) = 143.48, p < .001 (Table 2).

Importantly, however, adding the external demands subdimensions to the internal needs-only model also improved fit, Δχ²(7) = 16.48, p = .021 (Table 2). The improvement was clearly much greater when adding internal needs than when introducing external demands.

Specifically, in the full model, two external-demand subdimensions showed significant positive conditional weights with latent MISS: demands from parents (β = .168, p = .011) and societal demands (β = .184, p = .012), see Supplementary Table S9. Among the internal needs subdimensions, significant positive weights were observed for cognitive needs (β = .223, p = .007) and health needs (β = .354, p < .001). Several subdimensions also showed significant negative conditional weights, including aesthetic needs (β = -.212, p = .003), demands from other people (β = -.203, p = .005), and transcendence needs (β = -.245, p < .001), whereas the remaining subdimensions showed no significant conditional weights with latent MISS (Supplementary Table S9 and Figure S3). Because the predictors overlap substantially, these coefficients represent model-specific conditional associations and should not be interpreted as total associations or indicators of the substantive importance of individual subdimensions. Negative conditional weights may reflect suppression or collinearity within the multivariable formative model rather than inverse substantive relations.

Collinearity diagnostics indicated generally limited multicollinearity among the 22 subdimensions in the full sample, with only control needs exceeding the prespecified threshold of 5 (VIF = 6.01; Supplementary Table S10), warranting cautious interpretation of its unique contribution. Pairwise squared correlations among the subdimensions are presented in Supplementary Figure S2.

Hospital participants show higher MISS scores, particularly for internal needs

Consistent with the prespecified group-comparison hypothesis, hospital participants had higher MISS composite scores (M = .093, SD = .098) than community participants (M = .061, SD = .068; p < .001, d = .44). The difference was larger for the internal needs score (hospital: M = .119, SD = .117; community: M = .069, SD = .078; p < .001, d = .58), whereas the external demands score showed a smaller, nonsignificant difference (hospital: M = .068, SD = .087; community: M = .052, SD = .065; p = .073, d = .22). Adjustment for sex and age did not materially alter the pattern of between-group differences.

At the subdimension level, 12 of 22 comparisons remained significant after FDR correction. Eleven were internal needs subdimensions, indicating that the higher hospital scores extended across multiple aspects of internal needs rather than a single subdimension.

The largest differences were observed for cognitive needs (d = .69), stability needs (d = .68), health needs (d = .66), control needs (d = .62), and love and belonging needs (d = .57).

Among the seven external demands subdimensions, only demands from other people remained significant after FDR correction (d = .29; Supplementary Table S11).

External associations are consistent with convergent and criterion-related validity

MISS scores showed the expected pattern of associations with external validation measures (Figure 3; Supplementary Table S12). Perceived stress (PSS-10) was moderately to strongly associated with the MISS composite score and both dimension scores (n = 67;

Spearman's ρ = .50-.57, all FDR-adjusted ps < .001), supporting convergent validity.

Associations with SLE were weaker (n = 66; ρ = .21-.28); those with the composite and internal needs scores remained significant after FDR correction, whereas the association with external demands did not. Anxiety symptoms (SAS) (n = 67; ρ = .38-.47, all FDR-adjusted ps ≤ .002) and depressive symptoms (SDS) (n = 71; ρ = .46-.54, all FDR-adjusted ps < .001) were moderately associated with all three MISS scores.

Supplementary hierarchical regression analyses showed that the MISS composite score explained significant additional variance in anxiety and depressive symptoms beyond SLE, whereas its incremental contribution beyond PSS-10 was not statistically significant (Supplementary Table S13).

The MISS composite and dimension scores show high test-retest reliability

In the retest subsample (n = 31; mean interval = 16.9 days, SD = 3.3), test-retest reliability was high for the MISS composite score and both dimension scores. ICCs were .949 (95% CI [.898, .975]) for the composite score, .917 [.835, .959] for the internal needs score, and .927 [.854, .964] for the external demands score. No significant mean differences were observed between administrations for any of these three scores (ps ≥ .168). Among subdimension scores, estimable ICCs ranged from .446 to .885, and no mean difference remained significant after FDR correction (Supplementary Table S14). The ICC for demands from children could not be estimated because the subdimension score showed no variability across administrations.

Expert review provides generally favorable content-validity evidence while identifying areas for refinement

All seven experts completed the evaluations for all 135 candidate items. Overall, expert ratings indicated generally favorable item-level clarity and relevance, although the scale-level content validity indices fell below the prespecified benchmark of .90 (S-CVI/Ave = .862 for clarity and .881 for relevance). At the item level, most items met the prespecified I-CVI criterion (≥ .78) for clarity (102/135) and relevance (109/135), while 33 and 26 items, respectively, fell below the criterion and were identified for further review and potential refinement. For necessity, 75% of judgments classified the items as essential, the mean CVR was .501, and 121 items (89.6%) had positive CVRs. Agreement with the prespecified itemto-subdimension assignments was also high: 89.2% of judgments supported the original classification, and 114 of 135 items met the assignment-accuracy criterion of ≥ .78. Detailed item-level results are provided in Supplementary Table S15.

At the domain level, majority agreement regarding potential problems was limited to missing content in the health-needs subdimension and redundancy in the physiological-needs and love-and-belonging-needs subdimensions. No boundary-ambiguity concern received majority endorsement. Additional concerns raised by individual experts were retained as priorities for future refinement, with detailed quantitative results and qualitative comments provided in Supplementary Table S16.

Life-stage scores show shared and stage-specific patterns

The two life stages represented in the sample were Stage 1 (ages 0-18 years) and Stage 2 (ages 19-54 years for females and 19-59 years for males). The composite score was higher in Stage 2 (M = .084, SD = .096) than in Stage 1 (M = .05, SD = .073; FDR-adjusted p < .001), and the two stage-specific scores were strongly correlated (ρ = .73). Similar patterns were observed for internal needs (Stage 1: M = .058, SD = .086; Stage 2: M = .099, SD = .113) and external demands (Stage 1: M = .041, SD = .067; Stage 2: M = .069, SD = .087; both FDR-adjusted ps < .001), with cross-stage correlations of ρ = .72 and .68, respectively.

At the subdimension level, cross-stage associations were more variable (ρs = .15-.66), with stronger associations for several internal-need domains and weaker associations for roledependent domains such as work-related demands, demands from a partner, and demands from children. Stage differences were observed across 21 of the 22 subdimensions, including higher work-related needs and work-related demands scores in Stage 2 and higher learningrelated demands scores in Stage 1 (Supplementary Table S17).

The external validity pattern observed for the lifetime MISS score was also evident within each life stage. Both Stage 1 and Stage 2 composite scores were associated with perceived stress, stressful life events, anxiety symptoms, and depressive symptoms (Stage 1: ρs = .28-.44; Stage 2: ρs = .27-.57; all FDR-adjusted ps < .05). The magnitudes of these associations did not differ significantly between the two life stages. The pattern of knowngroups differences was also similar across stages, with higher scores among hospital participants most evident for internal needs. Overall, these findings indicate substantial crossstage continuity alongside stage-specific variation, supporting the use of the lifetime MISS score as an overall summary across the two represented life stages.

Sensitivity analyses

Sensitivity analyses using the alternative subdimension-weighted aggregation approach yielded results highly consistent with those from the primary item-weighted approach, with very high rank-order correspondence across scores (Spearman's ρ = .996-.999), differences in external-validity correlations ranging from Δρ = -.012 to .005 (all bootstrap 95% CIs included zero), and differences in known-groups effect sizes ranging from Δd = -.005 to .009 (Supplementary Table S18).

Similarly, the intensity-only scoring approach (M2) produced results highly consistent with M1, with very high rank-order correspondence (Spearman's ρ = .997), external-validity correlation differences of -.02 to .01 (all bootstrap 95% CIs for Δρ included zero), and known-groups effect-size differences of Δd = .008-.013 (Supplementary Table S19).

Discussion

The present study provides preliminary support for the MISS as an appraisal-based, multidimensional framework for characterizing psychological stress, with initial evidence of its reliability and validity. First, the two broad dimensions, internal needs and external demands, showed substantial overlap but were not interchangeable, as their constituent subdimensions retained complementary information, supporting a more fine-grained characterization of specific stress sources. Second, internal needs emerged as a particularly important dimension, showing a significantly stronger conditional association with the criterion-anchored latent MISS construct than external demands. Third, psychological stress showed both continuity across life stages and variation in specific stress sources, highlighting the value of preserving stage-specific information alongside an overall lifetime summary.

Finally, the findings also provide preliminary support for the reliability and validity of MISS scores across test-retest, known-groups, and external validation analyses.

The formative findings support the conceptual distinction between internal needs and external demands as two complementary sources of psychological stress. Although strongly correlated at the dimension level, their constituent subdimensions provided complementary information, indicating that these sources are related but non-interchangeable. These findings highlight the importance of assessing not only the overall level of psychological stress but also the specific sources contributing to it. Whereas global perceived-stress measures such as the PSS-10 primarily summarize overall subjective stress, the MISS provides source-specific profiles that characterize stress heterogeneity across individuals and populations (see Figure 1, Panel c).

Several factors may explain the substantial overlap between internal needs and external demands. From a theoretical perspective, external demands may acquire stressful significance partly through their perceived threats to valued needs, such as security, belonging, or controllability. This interpretation is consistent with transactional theories, which emphasize the role of appraisal in determining the stressful significance of environmental demands (Lazarus & Folkman, 1984). Methodologically, the global anchors may have aligned more closely with the appraisal-related content of internal needs, as they focused on subjective stress and its negative impact. Shared self-report methodology may also have contributed to the observed associations (Podsakoff et al., 2003). These explanations remain provisional, as the present study did not establish the causal or temporal relationships between the two domains. Moreover, formative coefficients should be interpreted cautiously because they are sensitive to shared variance, model specification, and criterion selection (Bollen & Lennox, 1991; Diamantopoulos & Winklhofer, 2001; Jarvis et al., 2003).

The findings also support explicitly incorporating unmet internal needs into the MISS assessment of psychological stress. Internal needs showed a substantial contribution in the formative models, and the known-groups analysis provided complementary support, with the largest hospital-community difference observed for internal needs and extending across several need-related subdimensions. These findings are consistent with the CIM proposition that unmet internal needs constitute an important source of psychological stress (Gu et al., 2026) and support their systematic inclusion in stress assessment.

The life-stage findings highlight the importance of preserving temporal context in the assessment of psychological stress. Although broader stress patterns showed substantial continuity across the two represented stages, the configuration of specific stress sources was more heterogeneous. By retaining stage-specific information, the MISS can distinguish continuity in overall stress burden from shifts in the particular sources contributing to that burden, distinctions that may be obscured when experiences are collapsed into a single lifetime score. This pattern is consistent with life-course perspectives emphasizing that stressors, resources, and social roles change across stages of life (Crosswell & Lockwood, 2020).

The pattern of external associations provided preliminary support for the validity of the MISS. MISS scores were moderately associated with perceived stress, anxiety, and depressive symptoms, indicating expected associations with conceptually and clinically relevant constructs. Associations with stressful life events were weaker, consistent with the distinction between subjective stress and exposure to stressful events (Brugha & Cragg, 1990; Crosswell & Lockwood, 2020; Epel et al., 2018). A similar pattern of stronger associations with affective symptoms than with stressful life-event exposure was also reported for the PSS (Cohen et al., 1983).

Several limitations define the scope of these conclusions. First, all MISS scores were based on retrospective self-report. This is particularly relevant to life-stage comparisons because recall and reinterpretation of earlier experiences may vary with age and current state (Hardt & Rutter, 2004). The present findings therefore characterize retrospectively reported rather than prospectively assessed stress experiences. Second, the MISS operationalizes the CIM through subjective appraisal of needs and demands rather than measuring stressors and resources as separate variables, so the study does not test the causal process by which their imbalance is proposed to give rise to stress. Third, the formative SEM relied on two global single-item criteria that shared appraisal-related content and measurement method with the MISS and should not be regarded as independent external standards. Formative coefficients should therefore be interpreted as criterion-specific conditional parameters, with the elevated VIF for control needs warranting caution in interpreting that coefficient. Finally, generalizability was constrained by the relatively young Chinese sample, limited representation of the oldest life stage, mixed recruitment and administration procedures, and modest validation subsamples. The comprehensiveness of the 135-item MISS also entails substantial respondent burden. Future studies should therefore evaluate the scale in larger and more diverse populations, further refine its items and subdimension boundaries, and determine whether shorter forms can reduce burden while retaining adequate content coverage and validity.

Conclusions

This study provides preliminary support for the reliability and validity of the MISS as an appraisal-based, multidimensional measure of psychological stress. By capturing specific stress sources while retaining life-stage information, the MISS may enable a more finegrained characterization of psychological stress across individuals and populations. The findings also highlight internal needs as an important source of stress, supporting their inclusion in stress assessment. Further validation in larger, more diverse, and longitudinal samples is needed to establish its robustness and generalizability.
