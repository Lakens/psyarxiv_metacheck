How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard), also Internal Review Board - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term); not IEC followed by a number, which is a technical standard - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee/Commission, in the singular or plural - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee, Bioethics Committee/Commission, Ethikkommission - Ethical Review Authority/Unit/Office/Agency - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Human Research Protection Office/Program, Office for Human Subjects Protection - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee(s) on/for (Medical and Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt - Ethics review that was not needed: Ethics review was not required, did not require further ethical review, exempt from formal human research ethics review, no new institutional ethics review

A template line the authors have not filled in (“[Add ethics approval … here.]”) is not a statement.

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. A TRO uses them for each trov:TransparentResearchPerformance. * run_results$exit_status: the exit status of the process that ran the script (0 = ended normally); NA when the process did not report one (a time limit, or Docker failing to start the container). * artifacts: one row per file, with stage (deposit: as downloaded from the repository, hashed from the downloaded file; before_run: the run folder just before the first script ran, after metacheck’s own changes, such as rewritten paths and unpacked archives; after_run: only the files the scripts made (status new), changed (changed) or removed (removed), as an unchanged file is the one listed before the run), path (relative to the run folder, as valid UTF-8, a byte of another encoding written as <xx>), size (bytes), sha256, mime_type (by extension, NA when unknown), source (the file’s address in the repository, for deposit). The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). Files metacheck itself copied or made between passes (see modifications, output_copied, file_placed) show in after_run too. A TRO uses the rows as trov:ResearchArtifacts and each stage as a trov:ArtifactArrangement. * modifications$sha256_before, modifications$sha256_after: the hash of the changed script as downloaded and as it ran; NA when the script is not at the same path in both (a script made from an R Markdown file). * environment: the system that ran the code (trov:TransparentResearchSystem in a TRO): metacheck_version, started_at, ended_at, host (R version, platform, operating system), sandbox, network_isolated (TRUE only when every Docker run had --network none; the process sandbox does not stop the network), internet, timeout_per_script, total_timeout, python_local (the Python run outside Docker and its version), and docker: runs (for each kind of run, r_run, r_install, python_run, python_install, the image and the docker run arguments, such as --network none, --read-only, --cap-drop ALL, no-new-privileges, the process limit and the memory and processor limits) and images (for each image, its ID and digests from docker image inspect, its creation time and the R and Python versions its environment declares).

run_results$checked says what the run of each script shows about the code, in five groups, so that a script that does not reproduce is kept apart from one the check could not test: ran (it ran to the end); code_error (it did not reproduce: it stopped with an error of the code or the deposit, could not be read, or needs a file the deposit does not hold); dependency (a package or program was not available: a package that could not be installed or was not loaded, a program outside R and Python, a system library, or R crashing in a package’s compiled code); time (it did not finish within the time limit for one script, or was not started because the time for all scripts had run out); and not_checkable (it could not be checked automatically: it needs a person, the internet, a key or another setting of the authors’ computer, one platform such as Google Colab, Windows or OpenSAFELY, more memory than the run allows, a file that was not downloaded, a screen, or a program that runs it, or it is a file of a package of the code that Python runs only as a module of that package). It is NA for files run inside another script, files that are not an analysis, files not read, and a Docker failure. summary_table counts each group (repro_ran_n, repro_code_error_n, repro_dependency_n, repro_time_n, repro_not_checkable_n), and the report’s first sentence gives them. The traffic light reflects the static readiness signals by default; a script in code_error that stopped with an error makes it red, as a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure, and a script in one of the other three groups keeps it from being green. Calls that need a screen, which the run does not have (dev.print(), dev.copy2pdf(), dev.copy2eps(), savePlot(), windows(), win.graph(), x11(), quartz(), View()), are replaced before the run by calls that need none, which modifications records as screen_call_replaced. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

The Core Psychological Needs (CPN) framework is a biopsychosocial model integrating Spitz, Goldfarb and Bowlby's observations of institutionalized children with evolutionary and neurobiological perspectives. The model proposes that these needs emerge from biological regulatory systems and co-regulate physiological functioning.

Cross Level Construct Decomposition was used to operationalize the theoretical model. Data was collected from 213 participants using chain-referral sampling and ten pre-validated measures prepared using back-translation. Item reduction was conducted using exploratory factor analysis (EFA), followed by structural equation modelling (SEM).

The model showed good fit (CFI = .93, TLI = .91, RMSEA = .053). Safety explained 72.4% of the variance (R² = .724) and showed positive associations with Affiliation (β = .47), Predictability (β = .40), and Control (β = .34), while Social Information showed a significant negative association with Safety (β = -.47). No multicollinearity was identified (VIF < 5).

These findings provide preliminary evidence for the proposed five-factor structure and the central role of Safety. Interpretation within the BOTSA neurobiological framework suggests potential regulatory and compensatory relationships among dimensions; however, these mechanisms remain theoretical given the present empirical evidence.

Introduction

1.1. The evolutionary problem of emotional deprivation

The observational studies by Goldfarb (1943), Spitz (1945) and Bowlby (1952) have shaken the world. They documented several cases where institutionalized children were severely underdeveloped both physically and mentally compared to those in foster homes. However, these youths had the sufficient resources that their biological systems needed for healthy development.

Therefore, the question is raised: What other needs are required for normative development? The concept of Core Psychological Needs might provide an explanation for the question.

While several studies have been conducted on the topic, the explanations are still fragmented. For instance, Gunnar & Bowen (2021) summarized the current evidence on institutionalized children, and according to their findings, the main reason for the underdevelopment and deaths among children is the dysregulation of the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis. The stress response is a nonspecific reaction to any outside stimulus, which requires the organism to re-allocate extra energy in order to react to the outside stressor (Schulkin et al., 2004). However, when the outside stressor is constant, the cortisol level is elevated, which can lead to the collapse of the stress system due to the limited energy that an organism can obtain (Schulkin et al., 2004). When this happens, various changes appear in the body, such as neurodegeneration, stomach ulcers and even excessive apoptosis (Gunnar & Bowen 2021).

Therefore, the proximate explanations of emotional deprivation have been established through neuroscience. Nevertheless, an integrated ultimate explanation of why it has occurred remains unclear. Indeed, Spitz (1945) stated, that even though hygiene conditions were impeccable, onefourth of the children died from infections. It is currently known that elevated cortisol levels can lead to energy reallocation from the immune system, but it does not explain the reason for stress production (Nelson et al., 2014). The greatest problem with emotional deprivation is its breadth.

There is no explanation for why the nervous system starts producing stress response when there are no visible, concrete threats. Emotional deprivation does not provide an answer to this phenomenon.

Subsequently, it may lead to two possible explanations: When emotional deprivation happens an essential constituent is hurt in the organism which is only implicitly represented in the body, and there might be another system besides the biological that co-regulates the physiological reactions.

Subsequently, these constructs are abstract and hard to measure directly. For instance, the need for safety is a core element for every organism, but it cannot be quantified because there is no universal form of this concept because of its breadth. However, if the same phenomenon emerges across species and developmental stages, consistently invoking similar biological processes, it becomes identifiable; if those particulars happen to converge on their broader factor, then the abstract concept becomes quantifiable. For my theory development, I conducted a method which could be used for latent construct identifications. I named it cross level construct decomposition.

1.2 Cross Level Construct Decomposition

In this study, I connected evidence at three levels, but this is not necessarily a rule of thumb. The first step of Cross Level Construct Decomposition (CLCD) is to identify the group where the phenomenon appears. For instance, the examined group in this study was children with emotional deprivation.

Afterwards, it is crucial to establish three major factors: What are the environmental effects, and what behavioral and biological outcomes did they cause? In this study, the environmental factor was the institution with several behavioral outcomes emerging, like unresponsiveness to sympathy (Goldfarb, 1943). Finally, the biological response was cortisol production. After identifying these conditions, the proposed sub-dimension's manifestations become observable across different species as well.

Additionally, the comparison group shall be chosen for evolutionary reasons. In this case, I studied social mammals because social connections are important for survival in these species. Therefore, evolution may have caused similar mechanisms to be evoked across organisms. Rodent and primate studies have been used extensively in this study to observe the previously mentioned conditions. If the criteria were met, a hypothesis should be made for a sub-dimension based on evolutionary principles. For instance, malevolence may be adaptive in stressful situations because the nervous system is already loaded cognitively. Therefore, there are no free resources to differentiate between prosocial or harmful intentions. Ultimately, the adaptive action to take, to maintain fitness is to anticipate every stimulus as negative (Bartal et al., 2016; Goldfarb, 1943). Once the candidate subdimension has been inferred from the comparative evidence, corresponding evidence must then be sought in an adult human sample.

For the final step, the previously gathered data shall be integrated. Indeed, if similar environmental, behavioral, and biological mechanisms emerge in a human study, this could lead to a potential sub dimension. Continuing the previous example: Tang et al. (2024) found in their study that when cortisol levels become elevated in an organism, the connection between the salience network and the sensorimotor network degrades; reducing prosocial behavior, like in rodents. In this case the environmental effect was induced experimentally using the ScanSTRESS paradigm (Streit et al., 2014 , as cited in Tang et al., 2024), but the example is still relevant because now there are three important yet distinguishable pieces of evidence. First, institutionalization causes stress responses, then behavioral changes occur. Secondly, rodent examples provide additional information that this behavioral phenomenon could occur across species. Ultimately, the human study provides the underlying behavioral mechanism for the observed variable. After this procedure, a sub-dimension has been identified: Interpersonal trust. This can be established based on several pieces of evidence; when this latent construct is not satisfied, biological and behavioral changes might appear. Therefore, it is a candidate to be a component of a higher factor.

In latent variable construction, one of the most challenging parts is identifying the specific items that could converge on the latent factor. Without knowing the specific compartment of that construct, we cannot establish that those items measure that factor (Zickar, 2020). CLCD addresses this problem and attempts to provide a solution. After the examination has been completed, item construction can happen because we have a defined variable. For instance, Interpersonal trust can be defined through the warmth and closeness towards others. Therefore, items should be constructed along this continuum. For example: "I feel like people want to hurt me." This already contains the dubiousness and alertness towards people (Zickar, 2020). After item creation, Exploratory Factor Analysis (EFA) is the next step. It will distinguish the items into factors, and if our items align with each other, the subfactor shall be established (It is advisable to run the EFA in a different sample as well for crossvalidation) (Zickar, 2020). The new variables have to undergo reliability testing before model construction. In this case, I specifically used Structural Equation Modelling (SEM) because I wanted to examine the relationships between factors. Nevertheless, if the chosen sub-variables load onto the same factor, the abstract latent factor becomes measurable. Ultimately, the exact structure of that factor can be established and identified because we know the compartments behind it.

1.3 Core Psychological Needs

I identified Core Psychological Needs (CPN) through the states of satisfaction-dissatisfaction. When a CPN gets damaged or the nervous system anticipates its neglect, extra resources will be allocated in order to satisfy the need or to compensate for it, so it can regulate the stress system.

The proposal of this system originated due to the lack of explanation on psychological regulation.

Several studies examined the relationship between stress regulation and psychological processes, but there is still some missing evidence. For instance, what does the nervous system regulate precisely when a psychological mechanism disrupts the organism? When the internal stability is disrupted, a physiological response will happen. It can be sweating, if the internal body temperature reaches an exact level. If the physiological thermoregulation is not enough, a behavioral response will follow, like going somewhere colder (Schulkin et al., 2004). This is an exact example for homeostatic regulation, but what happens when the internal stability gets disrupted by a psychological phenomenon? For example, when the organism happens to be in a dangerous situation (like facing a hostile animal), amygdala activation will lead to fight or flight mode. It will activate the firmer stress system, the sympathoadrenal system, preparing the organism for the behavioral outcome (Schulkin et al., 2004).

The main idea behind CPN is co-regulation. What if the behavioral and physiological outcome is not as separable as it appears? Social mammals required a strong group cohesion in order to survive, but evolution did not make a concrete structure dedicated to group environments. That is why psychological needs (such as Affiliation) might be embedded in the biological one. Continuing the previous argument: What if thermoregulation is not the first line of defense against heat but only a preventive stage in the regulatory process? Sweating might prevent the organism from overheating before it can emerge into a cooler environment. I do not believe psychological and biological regulations are separated but rather form one system which co-regulates the body. In the following sections I shall describe each psychological need thoroughly and present why it could provide an answer to the mentioned hypotheses (Schulkin et al., 2004).

1.3.1 Safety

It is universal for every organism to seek stability and safety. Indeed, from an evolutionary perspective, a living creature's primal function is to seek biological survival. Ultimately, this mechanism activates when safety gets disrupted or if the organism perceives a threat. However, the evaluation of hostile stimuli depends on organism type. Universality cannot be identified through threat detection because several environmental factors and personal experiences form the association with the perceived event (LeDoux, 2022). Following LeDoux's (2022) work, and the evolutionary principles, it is reasonable to conclude that Safety is the most fundamental, core psychological need.

Conversely, we cannot reduce it to a dimension. Indeed, every psychological need is layered, which means that latent constructs have several components with a specific, detailed definition. Following the CLCD framework, I identified three sub-dimensions for Safety.

Environmental security: It represents the living space of the organism as well as its quality, providing protection and functioning as a safe base. An environment which lacks hygiene, protection from intruders, and sufficient temperature cannot fulfill the role of a secure environment (Chen & Baram, 2016; Bowlby, 1952). Bowlby (1952) reports that the observed institution only provided restricted living spaces due to hygiene concerns degrading the psychological well-being of the children.

Social security:

This dimension represents the social area with which the organism frequently interacts. It provides the knowledge that the members of that social circle are not hostile and do not threaten the physical and mental wellbeing of the individual (Gunnar & Bowen, 2021 , Spitz, 1945). Spitz (1945) states that nurses often changed, therefore destabilizing the social area. The kids did not have the chance to evaluate the state of that group, which might cause a stress response.

Material security:

It not only represents money, but all the available resources that are necessary for our wellbeing and survival. For humans, money is a representative means, which shows how much assets we can acquire. Indeed, it contains all the necessities, clothing and hygiene tools (Chen & Baram, 2016; Bowlby, 1952). Spitz (1945) observed that none of the youth had toys or an object that they owned. That is an indicative sign of material security deprivation, and the kids easily could have perceived this as poverty.

If these needs are not met, a cortisol response may happen. Indeed, after distress, behavioral changes might take place, like resource gathering, hypervigilant observation or simply fight or flight (Gunnar & Bowen, 2021). After examining the selected group, I connected the evidence with rodent studies.

The cognitive degradation that Goldfarb (1943 ) observed, Chen & Baram (2016) also found similar outcomes in different rodents. Therefore, evidence points to the fact that resolving the distress, which threatens the survival of the organism, has a higher regulatory priority than processes such as growth and immune functioning. Indeed, not only is development postponed, but in adults, chronic cortisol levels decrease immune functioning (Gunnar & Bowen, 2021; Chen & Baram, 2016).

1.3.2 Predictability

Anticipation was crucial in order to survive; therefore, the organism could foresee the danger and avoid it. Predictions are made throughout each segment of our lives, to increase our security. Despite the fact that Predictability buffers Safety, those are two separate constructs. Indeed, abusive relationships illustrate this disparity the best. In these types of relationships, Safety is degraded, although Predictability is secured. The victim knows the amount of pain that comes and knows that it is not lethal. Therefore, staying in the relationship may be more "adaptive" in the abused person's mind, than facing the unknown, a possible greater risk (Friston & Kiebel, 2009). This example corresponds with the human study by De Berker et al. (2016). They have found that an unpredictable electric shock caused a greater increase in cortisol than an almost certain chance of electric shock.

Nevertheless, the amplitude of the shock was the same every time. I divided this dimension into two segments.

Interpersonal trust: It represents the general continuum from suspicion to goodwill towards others.

It should not be confused with social security, where the core thought is that of safety. Indeed, interpersonal trust embodies warmth toward others. The environment of the institution may have caused the children to be unreactive to sympathy (Goldfarb, 1945). Bartal et al. (2016) found similar behavior when corticosterone was injected into rodents; they observed that prosocial helping behavior disappeared. This may be a negative bias toward stimuli when the nervous system is loaded cognitively. This hypothesis aligns with the findings of Tang et al. (2024). When they induced distress in their human participants, the connection between the salience network and the sensorimotor network was degraded. Indeed, communication between these networks contributes to prosocial behavior, and when the connection weakens, so does empathy for pain. Ultimately, this evidence shows that interpersonal trust is a candidate sub-dimension.

Institutional trust:

We are all living in our own institutions. We all have pictures in our minds about how the world works. The hospitalized children had explicitly experienced this, and they thought that someone would adopt them someday, but this was seldom the case (Bowlby, 1952). This is the essence of institutional trust. It represents the belief about how a system works; the confidence that if somebody is calling the ambulance, it will come. If this belief shatters, the feeling of helplessness could be evoked (Maier & Seligman, 2016). Herzog et al. (2009) found that social instability caused anhedonia in rats, which is similar to what happens when a human feels helplessness. Indeed, Maier & Seligman (2016) found a similar mechanism in humans. When the social environment is unpredictable and unsafe, an evolutionary response (freezing) is evoked, which bears a striking similarity to depression. Therefore, institutional trust is a potential sub-dimension.

The word "trust" could be misleading in the predictability sub-dimensions. However, from my perspective, trust is the construct to attain a predictive view. Trust may be a cognitive representation of other people and systems that a person forms based on previous experiences. Ultimately, trust can be a representative "tool" to seek prediction even when uncertainty arises (Friston & Kiebel, 2009).

1.3.3 Affiliation

Through the work of Bowlby (1969) and Ainsworth et al. (1978), the importance of attachment has been established. Indeed, even poor parental behavior affects the child; therefore, severe neglect could cause even greater damage in the offspring's development (Ainsworth et al., 1978). Certainly, the observations of Goldfarb (1943) confirm these effects. Passivity and constant attention-seeking were typical among the deprived children. The most shocking statement was made by Bowlby (1969) when he wrote that neglect seldom ends in despair. Children stop seeking attention and simply accept the fact that nobody is coming to comfort them. I named this dimension Affiliation instead of Attachment because I aim to find evidence for the evolutionary universality of CPNs. Affiliation can be established across species, but Attachment is a more complex form of bonding, which is difficult to identify in non-human organisms. Additionally, the examined group has its own limitations because of the narrow developmental stages. Therefore, in this sample, romantic relationships cannot be identified. Nevertheless, in later developmental stages, having loving affection for someone is crucial not only for mental health but also because it has a stress regulating role (Sbarra & Hazan, 2008). Due to this limitation, I identified a family subdimension, even though it is made up of multiple parts as well, such as parental, sibling, and later romantic bonds. It encompasses these constructs, although the limitations remain present.

Family bond: I believe the family bond and its members constitute the first environmental regulatory aspect of an organism. Indeed, oxytocin regulates cortisol levels in humans (Page et al., 1990).

Chronic stress could induce a reduction in oxytocin; therefore, it could disturb normal biological regulation (Jurek et al., 2015). However, in the case of social buffering, oxytocin may start to rise again (Muroy et al., 2016). Due to this, the conclusion that the institutionalized children lacked this kind of protective factor, which contributed to their severe emotional deprivation, has already been established (Spitz, 1945). Nevertheless, this phenomenon does not appear only in humans. Walker et al. (2017) applied the limited bedding/nesting (LBN) paradigm to female rodents and examined the stress it generated. Subsequently, the quality of the female rat's parenting deteriorated, and her pups' corticosterone levels increased as a result. Therefore, not only absent parenting but also poor parenting could cause a stress response. These pieces of evidence support the idea that Family bond can be a potential sub-dimension of Affiliation.

Friendship bond: This sub-dimension represents strong bonds that are not based on blood relationships within the social group. Isolation from the tribe is fatal to humans, as it is to non-human social mammals. Consequently, HPA-axis dysregulation can occur when a socially dependent organism experiences prolonged solitude (Hawkley et al., 2012). However, strong connections that are not based on blood relationships might have a protective effect against social exclusion. Indeed, Peters et al. (2011) found that schoolchildren who had strong friendships showed a healthier cortisol pattern than children who were also excluded by their classmates but did not have friendships of similar quality. Children in the institution had difficulty forming friendships, and exclusion among them was not uncommon (Goldfarb, 1943). Therefore, this could have worsened their HPA responses and elevated their stress levels (Hawkley et al., 2012). Ultimately, Friendship bond could be a potent sub-dimension of Affiliation.

Intrapersonal bond: This construct represents our relationship with ourselves and our beliefs about

what kind of person we are. According to Bowlby (1969), attachment is a dyadic relationship between two people. However, poor internal representations could also lead to HPA-axis dysregulation, as can difficulties in interpersonal relationships (Nealis et al., 2020). Therefore, I believe it is worth investigating Intrapersonal bond as a candidate sub-dimension. Indeed, similar mechanisms may occur across species. For instance, Sapolsky (1982) observed the hierarchy of olive baboons. He found that lower status was associated with elevated cortisol levels compared with those of higherranking individuals. The similarity lies in interpersonal relationships. Depending on their surroundings, organisms can gather information from their position in the group. If an organism observes that all the other group members rank higher in the hierarchy, this might lead to an increase in cortisol because its position in the group is threatened, potentially leading to stress (Sapolsky, 1982). Nealis et al.'s (2020) findings support this. Students who exhibited extensive self-criticism had elevated cortisol levels. If one's worth is questionable, so is one's position in the group. Indeed, Goldfarb (1943) documented that emotionally deprived children had a confused grasp of themselves in relation to others. In the absence of a constant caregiver, children may be unable to identify their own worth, so the Intrapersonal bond may not remain intact.

1.3.4 Social Information

In the previous section, the importance of information has been established. Nevertheless, to my knowledge, Social Information has not yet been considered a need, although feedback is crucial in order to position ourselves in the social group and the world. Consequently, Social Information might reinforce the organism's behavior, allowing it to adjust to environmental needs. The concept of social information emerged from the behavior of neglected children. Goldfarb (1943) observed that the youths in the institute sought attention from their surroundings in a very uncomfortable way. They did not consider the other people behind it, but only the feedback itself. It can be explained with an important distinction: close bonds and group involvement are two different necessities for survival that are connected but separate in their regulatory functions.

Achievements: Contains all the external and internal stimuli, including those found not only within the environment but also in oneself. The internal stimulus is the expectation of that specific reward, and the external stimulus is the actual one. I believe the expectation is, in this sense, the need itself, but it should not be confused with the Predictability dimension. Indeed, while achievement is needed for the organism to attain orientation and identify its worth in the group, Predictability is needed to foresee danger and then avoid it. More simply: Social Information gives feedback about one's worth, while Predictability provides feedback on the world. Indeed, this distinction can be seen in the findings of Frimanson et al. (2021). They changed a company's employee evaluation schedule from every six weeks to every twelve weeks and found that cortisol levels were elevated in the twelveweek group. However, the six-week group did not show any change in cortisol secretion. This shows, in a human sample, that reward expectations can change HPA responses. Subsequently, similar neural mechanisms can be found in rodents as well. Pecoraro et al. (2009) implemented the successive negative contrast paradigm in rodents and found that corticosterone levels increased in rats that expected more sucrose but received less. This is the common mechanism: the reward. Indeed, while the two mechanisms are similar rather than analogous, the reward expectation is the same. Therefore, when humans expect more achievement and more social reward but ultimately attain less, cortisol levels may rise in advance (Frimanson et al., 2021). Goldfarb's (1943) observations on the institutionalized children might show this phenomenon in the aspiration-frustration experiment.

While all the foster-home children wanted to succeed, a couple of institutionalized children did not.

After an error, they were not able to reallocate extra energy because their systems were already overloaded by chronic stress (Schulkin et al., 2004).

Praise: This dimension contains both material and verbal applause. This could be a simple compliment or an award for the work one did. Through the concept of praise, the essence of Social Information becomes easily understandable. Praise provides information about our position in the group. If the organism receives reinforcement that its contribution to the group is valuable, that behavior may appear in the future. This mechanism may have evolved in this way to achieve better group cohesion. Nevertheless, too much negative feedback (critique) may cause an elevated cortisol level (Dickerson et al., 2008). Indeed, possible negative feedback could cause a stress response. Even without being present in a group situation, critique could evoke the danger of exclusion by itself (Dickerson et al., 2008). The reason behind emotionally deprived children's clinging behavior maybe the need to secure feedback in order to be able to identify themselves (Goldfarb, 1943). I believe Pecoraro et al.'s (2009) experiment is also appropriate here indicating that the difference between expectations and the actual reward could cause a stress response.

1.3.5 Control

The lack of Control among the children was visible in Goldfarb's (1943) observations. The California Behavior Inventory indicated that 73% of the neglected children were unresponsive in problemsolving. The essence of Control lies in the ability to change the organism's own behavior and have influence on the environment (Rotter, 1966). This is where it becomes distinctive from Predictability; during anticipation the organism "trusts" in the outcome, but in the dimension of Control the organism creates the outcome for itself.

Agency: Contains the belief that the occurrence in the environment is dependent on the organism's behavior. Therefore, the loss of Agency may lead to cortisol production. Indeed, Agrigoroaei et al. (2013) in their study found that participants who experienced hardships with driving in a simulation produced more cortisol. Additionally, Agency could regulate the stress system. Kearton et al. (2020) found that sheep who could avoid electric shock generated significantly less cortisol than the ones who only got voice signaling before the shocks. Ultimately, the loss of agency may have been evoked in Goldfarb (1943) observations.

He stated that deprived children were less capable of sustained effort, less thoughtful in problem solving, and more likely to abandon difficult tasks. This can be seen as a damage in the Agency sub dimension.

Autonomy: Represents the choice of freedom, that the organism's behavior aligns with its desire. Goldfarb (1943) stated that independence is a maturing sign in normative environmental conditions, while he observed that the children in the institution were confused, hazy and had a hard time independently experiencing reality. This lack of independence might be a sign of insufficient autonomy. Svensson et al. (2016) found that when rodents were forced to run using electric shock, it not only evoked cortisol production, but it counteracted the positive effects that movement could cause. Indeed, the freedom of choice might downregulate cortisol while forced options might have the opposite effect on humans as well (Steel et al., 2021).

1.4 The neurobiological regulations of Core Psychological Needs

The concept of allostasis provides a better understanding of how an organism reacts and adapts to the environment's challenges. In case of increased physical activity, muscles must elevate blood flow from 1.2 liter/min to 22 liter/min. The heart cannot provide this alone; therefore, muscles have to attain blood from its surroundings. The liver, intestines and kidneys all "lend" blood to the muscles until the organism manages to accommodate the increased physical activity (Schulkin et al., 2004, Chapter 2, p. 65). However, in case of psychological challenges we cannot pinpoint this energy reallocation because identifying which psychological construct "needs more blood" is impossible through physiological changes alone. Nevertheless, behavioral changes may provide information on the psychological challenge, so connecting together the environmental, behavioral and biological vagaries postulate which psychological construct has elevated activity. Ultimately, I believe the concept of CPN and its integration to neurobiological structure is important, to have a better understanding of psychological stress responses.

Safety: The stimuli's valence and quality are defined by the interaction between the Anterior Cingular Cortex (ACC) and Insula (IN) depending on previous experience, with the following signals being forwarded to the Basolateral Amygdala (BLA) (Monosov, 2017; Pessoa, 2008). The BLA sends the signal to the Central Amygdala (CeA), which shall start the physiological responses depending on the stimulus (Janak & Tye, 2015). The most important thing for an organism is survival; therefore, securing safety is always a top priority (LeDoux, 2022). It seems appropriate to connect Safety to the Amygdala not only because of the wide range of affective and cognitive responses that it has, but because of its wide range of connectivity (Pessoa, 2008). Indeed, if Safety is the most important need, attaching to a network hub which regulates not only the physiological, but affective and cognitive responses, appears adequate (Pessoa, 2008).

Predictability: Crockett et al. (2008) used placebo-controlled acute tryptophan depletion (ATD) procedure to investigate the relationship between sudden social unfairness in the ultimatum game (UG). They found that the ATD group was more sensitive to social injustice. Therefore, serotonin has a modulatory role that helps in modulating the behavior in unexpected group occurrences. Indeed, the Dorsal Raphe Nuclei (DRN) has collaterals with the BLA which, in case of serotonin elevation, modulates the CeA (Bocchio et al., 2016). Thus, the DRN-Amygdoid pathway has a role in prediction-making and serotonin might modulate the BLA's learning (Bocchio et al., 2016).

Ultimately, making the DRN as a hub for predictability seems appropriate.

Affiliation: Oxytocin (OT) is traditionally linked with bond forming. Indeed, OT can modulate how an individual moves towards affectively to its mate (Machin & Dunbar, 2011). However, OT has a more prominent role in stress-regulation. In the paraventricular hypothalamic nuclei (PVN) the expression of CRH and OT is shared. It would be logical to conclude that CRH production stops OT secretion, but it is the opposite. OT regulates the CRH secretion when the stress response happens; therefore, it has a neuroprotective, regulatory role (Jurek et al., 2015). However, in case of chronic stress the OT storages get depleted, but social buffering may restart the production (Muroy et al., 2016). Considering this, the connection between PVN and Affiliations is promising.

Social Information:

The concept behind this need, that positive social feedback provides a rewarding feeling which reinforms the behavior and adjusts it to increase group cohesion, hence survival. However, it seems contradictory at first, because the traditional dopamine (DA)-Nucleus Accumbens (NAc) literature stated DA as the "reward" neurotransmitter. Nevertheless, this concept proved to be reductive and did not explain how hedonistic feelings calm down the nervous system after reward pursuit (Berridge & Kringelbach 2015). It is more adequate to treat DA as the incentive salience neurotransmitter because the actual hedonistic feeling happens in the NAc's opioid receptors. Thus, endogenous opioids cause the hedonistic feeling and the calming effect on the Amygdala (Correia et al., 2016; Fassett-Carmanet et al., 2023). However, this "calming" feeling might be achievable without rewards as well. For instance, Periaqueductal Gray (PAG) coordinates analgesic effects through the body. Indeed, mu and kappa opioid activation leads to analgesia (Loyd & Murphy, 2009).

Therefore, I propose that activation of PAG through pain has a similar stress-regulatory effect as reward does neurobiologically (Bresin & Gordon, 2013). Additionally, PAG is activated through negative social information, thus it may have a stress regulatory effect as well (Hsu et al., 2013).

Control: There is a well-established literature on how the sense of Control can regulate the organism (Maier & Seligman, 2016) However, recent neuroscience studies provided an explanation to the precise biological mechanism. Indeed, Pizzi et al. (2017) found that the medial Prefrontal Cortex (mPFC) innervates the BLA's inhibiting neurons, thus when the mPFC excites these nerve cells, the activation of BLA degrades. Nevertheless, the amygdala has inhibiting neurons in this area as well; therefore, if needed, the amygdala can block the mPFC's inhibition. Ultimately, the mPFC can be a great potential pair for the Control need, because securing it leads to stress-regulatory effects both psychologically and biologically.

The proposed neurobiological integrations should not be interpreted as one-to-one mappings between psychological needs and discrete brain structures. Rather, I would propose that the five CPNs emerge from a shared underlying regulatory architecture, while their expression may involve different configurations of interacting neural systems. Genetic predispositions, environmental conditions, and previous experiences may modulate how information is evaluated and how the organism adjusts its behavior, producing different patterns of brain activity and physiological responses. Within this framework, the neurobiological associations proposed above represent candidate nodal points for their corresponding psychological needs, rather than exclusive anatomical localizations or mere analogies. For instance, for Social Information, I hypothesize that the interaction between the basolateral amygdala and nucleus accumbens (NAc) constitutes a recurrent mechanism through which socially evaluative information acquires motivational significance and influences subsequent behavior. This proposal is consistent with evidence that basolateral amygdala inputs facilitate NAc responses to reward-predictive cues and that NAc activity is associated with both the pursuit of social approval and the avoidance of social disapproval (Ambroggi et al., 2008). The framework thus predicts that these candidate nodes will show measurable associations with their corresponding psychological functions, while their wider patterns of neural connectivity and physiological expression will vary with the information encountered and the individual's previous experiences.

These proposed associations provide testable hypotheses for evaluating the CPN framework as an underlying stress-regulatory system.

2.2 Statistical Analysis and Structural Equation model conducting

The statistical analysis was made in the Jamovi software. Exploratory factor analysis (EFA) was used to see if the items are separated in their anticipated sub-dimensions. Based on the theoretical framework, EFA was run depending on the proposed need's structure simultaneously. Promax rotation and minimal residual extraction were used during this stage. In view of the sample size, we implemented item reduction. Of each factor, three items were selected based on how strongly it was loaded to its construct. Subsequently, using an average calculation we made new variables from the items, thirteen total. The new variables were analog in essence with the theoretical ones. Reliability analysis was used to test the items' homogeneity. All variables had at least a 0.7 McDonald omega (For further details see Table 3). The last stage was the analyses completed in Jamovi's R module.; lavaan, semPlot, dplyr, psych packages were used. Three base models were made to avoid ambiguity in model conduction: one hierarchical with Safety on the top, one where Safety is the endogenous construct while the others were the predictors, and one dynamical where Safety is in the central position. The dynamic model provided the most stable admissible solution, so we continued the subsequent model development with it. The latent factors contained all the thirteen variables we made. Additional pathways were added based on the theoretical neurobiological integrations.

Therefore, bidirectional pathways were added between Safety and the other needs. Moreover, a reciprocal pathway was added between Affiliation and Social Information to evaluate the relationship between close connections and broader group feedback. Ultimately, two more pathways were added, one from Affiliation to Control and another from Predictability to Control based on brain connections (Jang et al. 2022; Maier & Seligman, 2016).

The model creation happened by structural equation modelling (SEM) with a robust maximum-likelihood estimator. The structural model was developed to represent the CPN framework as an interconnected regulatory system rather than a hierarchy of psychological needs. Although Safety was assigned a central position in the proposed framework, 516 this did not imply that it was considered theoretically superior to the other needs. Equality constraints were imposed on selected reciprocal regression paths to represent the hypothesized symmetry of these relationships and to reduce the number of freely estimated parameters. The resulting model was evaluated against the observed data to examine the adequacy of these structural assumptions. 3.3 Explained Variance and Structural Relationships

The proportion of variance explained differed across the five latent constructs. The model explained 72.4% of the variance in Safety (R² = .724), 64.9% in Control (R² = .649), 45.6% in Affiliation (R² = .456), 34.7% in Predictability (R² = .347), and 9.1% in Social Information (R² = .091). Safety showed positive standardized regression paths to Predictability (β = .402, p < .001), Affiliation (β = .469, p < .001), and Control (β = .338, p = .002). The corresponding paths toward Safety were also positive for Predictability (β = .170, p < .001), Affiliation (β = .238, p < .001), and Control (β = .266, p = .014).

Negative standardized paths were observed in both directions between Safety and Social Information: the path from Safety to Social Information was β = -.467 (p < .001), whereas the path from Social Information to Safety was β = -.142 (p < .001).

The paths between Affiliation and Social Information differed in direction. Affiliation positively predicted Social Information (β = .432, p = .001), whereas the path from Social Information to Affiliation was negative (β = -.199, p < .001). Control also showed positive standardized paths from Affiliation (β = .268, p = .001) and Predictability (β = .192, p = .001). Variance inflation factor (VIF) values calculated from the latent-variable correlation matrix ranged from 1.245 to 4.845, remaining below the prespecified threshold of 5. Values represent standardized regression coefficients (p < .05, *p < .01, **p < .001, ***p.) When the organism has secured its bond, the need for affiliation decreases (Bowlby, 1969).

Initially, "Self-bond" belonged under Affiliation. However, during the model conduction it converged more under Safety. Therefore, Self-Bond was renamed to Self-security. It could be interpreted as that self-compassion may plays an essential role in fostering a general sense of safety and security. It leads to some intriguing hypotheses, like how Social Information could modulate Self-security. Therefore, Self-security seems to be an important subdimension which requires further experiments in the future.

4.2 The broader theoretical explanation of the data

The statistical results and the neuroscience literature showed several similarities. The theoretical framework is based on neuroscience, so it appears adequate to interpret the results theoretically as well. Indeed, considering the fact that the psychological explanation is not sufficient to explain the whole model we may find. For instance, if Social Information is elevated because of lower Safety, there is a possibility it would cause the decrease in the Affiliation factor as well. However, if we are considering the framework as a biologically embedded system, which regulates the organism jointly with the psychological and physiological system, we could have a better explanation and similarities with other already established mechanisms. Indeed, incorporating Machin and Dunbar's (2011) Brain-Opioid Theory of Social Attachment (BOTSA) provides a more elegant explanation.

Unlike solitary animals, social mammals require much closer and more enduring relationships to survive. Consequently, a biological system must have evolved that provides the hedonic pleasure associated with social bonding (Panksepp, 1997 , as cited in Machin & Dunbar, 2011). According to the BOTSA framework, this hedonic experience emerges through the interaction between oxytocin and β-endorphin. This hypothesis was tested through the administration of naloxone, an opioid antagonist. In rhesus monkeys, it was found that vocalizations associated with separation anxiety occurred even when their mothers were present. In a human study, participants reported that they did not experience an intimate connection with their partners following the administration of an opioid antagonist (Machin & Dunbar, 2011). The results of the CPN model become more comprehensible in light of these findings, as they can be interpreted by analogy with the observations of Machin and Dunbar (2011). Affiliation might excite oxytocin production in the PVN, which subsequently could activate the reward center and the release of endogenous opioids within it. Through negative feedback, this process inhibits the pursuit of Social Information because the need for Affiliation has been satisfied through the interaction between oxytocin and β-endorphin (Berridge & Kringelbach, 2015; Machin & Dunbar, 2011).

However, another pathway with a negative sign can be observed between Safety and Social Information, without a feedback loop. This relationship can be most effectively explained through the mechanism of allostasis. When an organism must exert greater effort in response to an external stimulus, beyond what its general state of internal equilibrium can accommodate, a new, temporary mode of functioning becomes active: allostasis (Schulkin et al., 2004). On this basis, it seems unlikely that this mechanism would not also apply to the central nervous system, which is sensitive even to the smallest changes in pH. This compensatory mechanism may be mapped onto the pathway between Safety and Social Information in the SEM model. When an organism does not feel secure within its social environment, it may seek Social Information to establish whether its position within the hierarchy and the community is secure.

A doubly negative pathway between the two needs is also visible in Figure 1, and this relationship can be most effectively understood in terms of biological causation. The amygdala signals the nucleus accumbens (NAc) to initiate active reward seeking by the organism. Once the reward has been obtained, amygdala activity decreases through feedback (Berridge & Kringelbach, 2015). However, if the external stressor persists, signaling from the amygdala may continue. Increasingly greater external stimulation will be required after each feedback loop to elicit the same hedonic response, because chronic stimulation leads to the downregulation and desensitization of opioid receptors. This is where the negative pathway between the NAc and the amygdala becomes relevant. When the organism can no longer obtain the level of reward necessary to modulate amygdala activity, the stress response is triggered, and cortisol production increases to stimulate greater activity. However, the organism will eventually be unable to sustain this demand. This mechanism constitutes allostatic overload (Schulkin et al., 2004).

Nevertheless, the model also shows that Social Information reduces the need for Affiliation. This relationship can be interpreted in a manner consistent with the BOTSA framework. The large number of endogenous opioids responsible for hedonic pleasure may substitute for the pleasure elicited by Affiliation. Numerous clinical observations support this interpretation, suggesting that individuals with substance-use problems may devalue and damage their interpersonal relationships in pursuit of the pleasure provided by the substance. Interpreted in this way, the model suggests that increased Social Information may reduce the organism's need for Affiliation, indicating a potentially pathological pathway (Machin & Dunbar, 2011).

The explained variance in Social Information was the lowest among all five needs, at only 9%.

However, it would be premature to conclude that Social Information is a different or lower order need than the others on this basis alone. The low value may instead indicate that its importance is context dependent. This possibility warrants consideration because Affiliation has a positive association with Social Information, whereas Safety has a negative association of a similar magnitude. These opposing effects could cancel each other out in a cross-sectional sample, whereas in a longitudinal sample, they might be expected to alternate over time. This remains a theoretical possibility, as the current data does not provide sufficient evidence to support this interpretation.

4.3 Limitations

This study has several limitations. The CLCD method was not followed completely because, instead of creating new items, we used established questionnaires. Consequently, both the usefulness of the methodology and the construct validity of the proposed constructs remain uncertain. Therefore, the next step in testing the framework should begin with independent item development.

Furthermore, the EFA and SEM were conducted using the same sample, creating a risk of overfitting, which may have inflated the model fit indices. Gender distribution also presents a limitation. The framework proposes that these needs are universal across species; however, the unequal gender distribution and the lack of testing in other social mammals limit the evidence supporting this hypothesis. Additionally, the effects of culture and age on CPNs remain unclear.

The most significant limitation lies in the lack of physiological data. Consequently, it remains uncertain whether these needs have a stress-regulatory function. Moreover, longitudinal studies are needed to investigate the temporal relationships among the needs and explore potential compensatory mechanisms.
