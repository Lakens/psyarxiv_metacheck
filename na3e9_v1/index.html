Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on 226 Psychological Science papers with 5,019 hand-coded p-values, 438 of them imprecise (scienceverse/validation, branch stat_p_exact). Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). Of the misses, 13 are starred table notes (“*p < .05”) that the module leaves out on purpose and 11 are p-values it did not find; of the wrong reports, 20 are p-values the ground truth does not contain (such as “ps < .05”) and 6 use ≤ or ≥. These figures measure agreement with the hand-coding, which has known errors in both directions (scienceverse/validation#48).

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

Abstract

Background: Selecting an appropriate questionnaire to measure autistic traits in children for research or clinical practice requires considering several factors. These include the validity of the questionnaire's structure, the questionnaire's sensitivity and specificity, the extent to which autistic traits are represented (e.g., whether all diagnostic sub-criteria are adequately represented), the clarity and respectfulness of item wording, and inclusive research practices used during questionnaire development. To provide clinicians and researchers information on the most sensitive, specific, and contemporary measures for identifying and assessing autistic traits, we conducted a review of existing caregiver-report questionnaires for measuring autistic traits in children aged 4-12 years.

Method: Ten questionnaires were identified for inclusion. For each included questionnaire, targeted searches were conducted in the form of forward citation searches to identify peer-reviewed studies reporting on the instrument's development or subsequent psychometric evaluation (factor or component analysis, sensitivity, specificity).

Results: Overall, there was marked variability in the extent to which questionnaires assess autistic traits across diagnostic sub-criteria, as well as in their sensitivity and specificity for differentiating autism from various other groups. Several questionnaires lacked psychometric support for their proposed structure. Respectfulness and clarity of item wording could be improved in most questionnaires. Autism community input during questionnaire development was typically limited or absent. Of note, the recently developed Autism Symptom Dimensions Questionnaire stood out as a promising freely available measure.

Discussion: Developers of new questionnaires should use inclusive research practices, write items using clear and respectful language, ensure adequate psychometric properties, and aim to include items from all diagnostic sub-criteria.

The efficiency and accessibility of questionnaires makes them a valuable tool in timeand resource-limited environments (Trevethan, 2017). Accordingly, autism questionnaires are often used in research and clinical practice (Lyall, 2023; Sobieski et al., 2022). However, the implications of questionnaire selection, whether it be for either of these purposes, should not be understated. In research contexts, these tools are commonly used to operationalise and quantify autistic traits, supporting investigations into the distribution of autistic traits across the general population and exploration of how certain behaviours change across development (e.g., Pender et al., 2023). Such studies have played a pivotal role in shaping our current understanding of autism. Notably, studies examining the relationships between autistic traits (e.g., Huerta et al., 2012) contributed to the development of the current diagnostic criteria for autism outlined in the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorder, Fifth Edition, Text Revision (DSM-5-TR; American Psychiatric Association, 2022). However, rigorous, high-quality research depends on the validity and appropriateness of the measures used, thus the selection of psychometrically sound questionnaires grounded in current theoretical frameworks is essential in continuing to advance our understanding of autism.

Concurrently, autism questionnaires are widely used in clinical practice as screening tools to indicate whether a formal diagnostic assessment may be warranted (Thabtah & Peebles, 2019). Because autism can be diagnosed through a variety of trait combinations, a questionnaire used for screening which focuses on only a subset of diagnostic sub-criteria may fail to identify individuals who present differently. Put differently, if certain sub-criteria are not represented in the items of a questionnaire, some individuals showing traits or behaviour consistent with those sub-criteria may not obtain a score considered high enough to prompt referral for a full diagnostic evaluation. Therefore, improper selection of a questionnaire could be a contributing factor in the high estimates of missed autism diagnoses reported in the literature (e.g., Cooper et al., 2017; Wiggins et al., 2020). These considerations are particularly relevant during the primary school years (approximately ages 4 to 12), which is an especially important period for identifying and measuring autistic traits. Autism research is most frequently conducted on child populations (Mason et al., 2022). This developmental stage also aligns with the most common age of autism diagnosis identified in a recent meta-analysis of international data (Van 'T Hof et al., 2021). Indeed, the prevalence of autism is highest among Australian children aged 5 to 12 years (Australian Bureau of Statistics, 2024), while a large-scale cross-sectional study in the United Stated reported the highest rate of autism diagnoses among 5-to 8-year-olds (Grosvenor et al., 2024). Similarly, Scottish census data indicate the prevalence of autism increases with age until around 10-11 years, reflecting the typical timing of diagnosis (Rydzewska et al., 2019).

During this developmental period, children often lack the abilities required to reliably report on their own experiences. As a result, caregivers are typically the primary informants in both clinical and research settings. Caregiver-report questionnaires therefore represent a particularly appropriate and commonly used method for measuring autistic traits in this age group.

We suggest that researchers or clinicians selecting a caregiver-report questionnaire for identifying and measuring autistic traits in primary school aged children should consider the five characteristics summarised below, weighing these in accordance with the aims of the questionnaire's use.

1. Psychometric adequacy of questionnaire

When selecting a questionnaire, its psychometric adequacy must be considered.

Psychometric adequacy refers to statistical properties which determine if a questionnaire fits its intended purpose, be it for research or clinical use. Two such properties are (a) psychometric support for scale structure and (b) the sensitivity and specificity of the questionnaire (i.e., the capacity to correctly identify who is autistic and who is non-autistic, respectively).

1a) Psychometric support for scale structure

Questionnaires that demonstrate sound scale structure have typically been subjected to factor analysis to determine the number and nature of dimensions (factors) within the construct being measured (Boateng et al., 2018). Factor analysis refers primarily to Exploratory Factor Analysis (EFA) and Confirmatory Factor Analysis (CFA; Yong & Pearce, 2013). Sometimes Exploratory Structural Equation Modelling (ESEM; Asparouhov & Muthén, 2009) is used instead of either EFA or CFA. ESEM freely estimates cross-loadings, as with EFA, but can evaluate model fit, as with CFA.

Alternatively, Principal Components Analysis (PCA) is sometimes used instead of EFA. PCA functions differently in that it does not separate specific variance and error variance, instead seeking to explain total variance. This can limit the generalisability of the derived component structure (Tavakol & Wetzel, 2020). Given this, the use of PCA instead of EFA is controversial (Loewen & Gonulal, 2015). However, this review will include PCA where it has been used in the analysis of scale structure.

Regardless of the statistical approach applied, typically, an exploratory technique should initially be used to derive a factor structure, which then forms the a priori model for further evaluation through a confirmatory technique (Morgado et al., 2017). If a wellestablished model already exists, using a confirmatory technique directly is appropriate. The absence of psychometric support for scale structure calls into question the validity with which one can infer that the observed scores reflect the theoretical constructs they are supposedly measuring. Therefore, it is good practice to use the derived factor structure to inform the formation of questionnaire subscales and where this is not the case, clear justification should be provided.

1b) Questionnaire sensitivity and specificity

In addition to ensuring psychometric support for scale structure, good to excellent sensitivity and specificity must be established if the questionnaire is to be used for screening purposes (Swift et al., 2020). In the context of autism screeners, sensitivity is the likelihood of correctly identifying autistic people, whereas specificity is the likelihood of correctly identifying non-autistic people. Sensitivity and specificity trade off against each other (e.g. a low cutoff score would favour sensitivity at the expense of specificity, and vice-versa for a high cutoff score).

2. Comprehensively assess the diverse range of autistic traits

It is important to understand which autistic traits a questionnaire is measuring when deciding whether to use it. Based on the DSM-5-TR (American Psychiatric Association, 2022), the core diagnostic criteria of autism are persistent differences and difficulties in social interaction and communication (Criterion A), and patterns of highly focused or repetitive behaviours, speech, or interests, or sensory processing differences (Criterion B). As shown in Table 1, there are three sub-criteria for Criterion A and four sub-criteria for Criterion B. An autism diagnosis requires that all three of the Criterion A sub-criteria and at least two of the four Criterion B sub-criteria are met. While we acknowledge some associated autistic features may fall outside the formal diagnostic criteria, the present review aims to evaluate questionnaires within the context of current diagnostic practices. For this reason, we focus on the DSM-5-TR, though we consider our assessment of autistic trait coverage similarly applicable to the International Classification of Diseases, eleventh edition, criteria (World Health Organization, 2022), given the concordance between the two diagnostic systems.

3. Clarity of item wording

The wording of questionnaire items is critical for ensuring their usefulness, reliability, and validity. Ambiguous or complex items may lead to inaccurate responses, skipped questions, or respondents abandoning the questionnaire entirely (Nicolaidis et al., 2020).

Further, ambiguous items can worsen the psychometric properties of the questionnaire due to respondents interpreting the items' meanings differently than intended, while technical and jargon-heavy language limits the accessibility of questionnaires (Clark & Watson, 2016).

Additionally, in autistic trait questionnaires, it is important to avoid non-literal language (e.g., metaphors, turns of phrase), which autistic people often find challenging to interpret (Nicolaidis et al., 2020). This point remains pertinent in caregiver-report questionnaires, given respondents may be autistic or have autistic traits themselves, due to the high heritability of autism (Sandin et al., 2017).

4. Use of respectful and neurodiversity-affirming language

Using respectful neurodiversity-affirmative language in the wording of items is also important due to potential implications for response accuracy, as well as wider societal consequences. Language referring exclusively to impairments and difficulties can reduce the accuracy of responses due to social desirability bias (Tourangeau, 2018) and risks offering a prejudiced understanding of what "autistic traits" encompass (Bottema-Beutel et al., 2021).

At the societal level, the use of deficit-based language in both research and clinical practice, rooted in a medical model of autism, gave rise to the earlier public view of autism as a disease (Kapp et al., 2013). Concurrently, this public view of autism, legitimised by the deficit-based language used by researchers and clinicians, contributed to the marginalisation and discrimination against autistic people (Bottema-Beutel et al., 2021). Therefore, as the use of language within research and clinical practice has clear societal consequences, there is an ethical obligation to employ respectful language throughout. This includes the wording of autistic trait questionnaires.

5. Use of inclusive research practices in questionnaire development

Referred to as a "hallmark of excellence in research" in a recent editorial (pg. 1501 , Fletcher-Watson, Bölte, et al., 2021), inclusive research practices (also termed "participatory research") refer to research which is done with autistic people, rather than done on them (Israel et al., 2005). There are different levels of inclusive engagement of autistic people in research, such as partnership/co-production or consultation (Arnstein, 1969; den Houting et al., 2021). In addition to being morally and ethically right (Fletcher-Watson, Brook, et al., 2021; Wright et al., 2014), including autistic people in research decisions, methodology design, and the interpretation of findings benefits research in many ways (Keating, 2021). For example, inclusive practices in questionnaire development can help ensure the items reflect the actual experiences of autistic people (Nicolaidis et al., 2019 (Nicolaidis et al., , 2020) ) as well as offer insight into the respectfulness and clarity of items. Further, while inclusive research practices are not in themselves a statistical method, they do have direct and important implications for the psychometrics of the questionnaire as they are key in ensuring content validity; that is, the extent to which items measure the construct they are meant to.

Overview

Research synthesising and critically appraising existing questionnaires against the above characteristics is necessary to provide clinicians and researchers with information on the most sensitive, specific, and contemporary measures for identifying and assessing autistic traits. While several reviews of autistic trait questionnaires exist, these have generally addressed narrower or different questions from those considered in the present review. For example, Norris and Lecavalier (2010) reviewed the diagnostic accuracy of five caregiver completed Level 2 screening measures, while Chesnut and colleagues (2017) focused specifically on the screening accuracy of the Social Communication Questionnaire. Hirota and colleagues (2018) examined the validity of screening tools for children and adults, and Fekar Gharamaleki and colleagues (2021) provided a broader descriptive review of autism screening tests, including their administration, scoring, and reported psychometric properties.

Therefore, although previous reviews provide valuable information regarding the availability and diagnostic performance of autism questionnaires, they do not comprehensively evaluate how contemporary caregiver-report questionnaires for children were developed, how well questionnaire content represents the current diagnostic construct of autism, or the adequacy of their underlying measurement structure.

Furthermore, while DSM criterion coverage has received some attention in previous literature, it has not been systematically evaluated across caregiver-report autistic trait questionnaires. For example, Thabtah and Peebles (2019) identified DSM-5 fulfilment as one of the characteristics considered in their review and concluded that many existing screening measures did not fully represent DSM-5 criteria. However, they did not report a systematic procedure for evaluating criterion coverage, such as mapping questionnaire items to individual DSM-5 criteria, and they did not provide questionnaire-level results demonstrating the extent of coverage. Indeed, they subsequently identified such item-to-criterion mapping as an important area for future work. Criterion-level analysis has been undertaken for some standardised diagnostic instruments: for example, Evers and colleagues (2021) mapped items from three standardised diagnostic instruments onto DSM-5 sub-criteria, identifying substantial variation in how these diagnostic instruments represented the criteria. However, their analysis did not extend to caregiver-report questionnaires.

Consequently, to the best of our knowledge, there is no existing review that has systematically evaluated criterion-level DSM-5-TR coverage across caregiver-report questionnaires for autistic traits in children. Similarly, existing reviews do not offer substantive insight into the adequacy of the analysis of scale structure of the questionnaires or the use of inclusive research practices in questionnaire development. Further, there is no existing review which assesses the clarity and respectfulness of item wording in existing questionnaires. Therefore, the present review critically analyses and synthesises research on the development and subsequent psychometric evaluation of existing caregiver-report questionnaires for measuring autistic traits in children (aged 4 to 12 years) against these characteristics.

Method Search for Relevant Questionnaires and Studies

The literature search was conducted in two phases: (1) search for relevant autism questionnaires; and (2) search for studies reporting the development or evaluation of those measures. Both of the search phases were conducted by author GH.

Phase One: Identifying Relevant Questionnaires

A conventional PRISMA search strategy was considered. However, due to wide variability in potential terminology (e.g., "Autis*", "Asperger*", "PDD-NOS", combined with "tool", "scale", "inventory", etc.

), trial systematic searches returned thousands of irrelevant hits. For example, a single Web of Science search string produced over 14,000 records. Following librarian consultation, and drawing on methods used in a prior review (Hayden-Evans et al., 2022), we instead identified relevant caregiver questionnaires via multiple sources: Relevant caregiver questionnaires were initially identified via internet searches, websites of major publishers (Pearson, WPS), existing reviews (e.g., Fekar Gharamaleki et al., 2021; Thabtah & Peebles, 2019), the test libraries of a local children's institute and a university clinic for training clinical psychologists and neuropsychologists, and measures known to the authors (researchers and clinicians working in the field of autism). Questionnaires were included in the review if they were: (1) available and designed for use in English; (2) autism focused (e.g., rather than an autism subscale of a larger questionnaire); (3) caregiver report; (4) developed for use with school age children (4-to 12years-old); (5) developed after the release of the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, Fourth Edition (DSM-IV; American Psychiatric Association, 1994); (6) accessible to the research team (where a measure was not freely available and not available within either of the local test libraries, the authors and publishers were contacted for access); and (7) not an older version of an otherwise included questionnaire. It is important to note that questionnaires did not need to be developed as screeners to be included in the review. This was to ensure the inclusion of common questionnaires used in autism research. The decision to include questionnaires developed after the release of the previous diagnostic framework (i.e., the DSM-IV) rather than the introduction of the current diagnostic framework (i.e., the DSM-5) was to ensure the inclusion of the most commonly used measures to date. Measures were not included if they were: (1) reported only in grey literature; (2) a version of an original English questionnaire adapted for use in a different language; or (3) an adapted version of an existing questionnaire otherwise eligible for inclusion (e.g., a shortened version).

Phase Two: Identifying Relevant Studies

Targeted searches were conducted to identify peer-reviewed studies reporting on the development or subsequent psychometric evaluation (factor or component analysis, sensitivity, specificity) of the included questionnaires. Again, a conventional search strategy was initially considered. We trialled targeted systematic searches for each questionnaire (using full titles and acronyms, combined with psychometric terms such as "factor analysis").

However, this approach was inconsistent in performance. For some measures, acronyms are common words (e.g., "AIM" for the Autism Impact Measure), yielding over a million irrelevant hits within a single database. For others, using only the full title missed key papers that referred to the questionnaire solely by acronym. Running multiple such searches across databases resulted in impractically large numbers of records (in the thousands per measure).

Therefore, a less conventional, though still systematic and rigorous, approach was conducted.

This involved forward citation searches for each questionnaire using Google Scholar. All searches were conducted on December 29 th , 2025.

For studies reporting on factor or component analysis, data were extracted on sample size, age range, gender, ethnicity, IQ, and diagnostic status of participants. For studies evaluating sensitivity and specificity, information on age range, comparison group type, and sample size was extracted. All studies describing the development of the questionnaires were inspected for explicit mention of inclusive research practices.

Approach for Assessing Items of Included Questionnaires

Coverage of Autistic Traits

The items of each questionnaire included in the review were independently inspected and sorted into the seven diagnostic sub-criteria of the DSM-5-TR by the first and fifth authors, a clinical psychologist registrar and a clinical neuropsychologist. Items were either (1) allocated to a single sub-criterion, (2) assessed as tapping more than one diagnostic subcriterion and so marked as "More than one", or (3) could not be categorised under a single sub-criterion or "More than one" and so were marked as "Unclear/None". This last category occasionally included items which were assessing behaviours or traits associated with autism but not part of the diagnostic criteria (e.g., an item asking whether the child tends to focus on details). Inter-rater reliability was calculated as percentage agreement. Disagreements were resolved through discussion.

Wording Clarity

Items were first independently reviewed for wording clarity by two non-autistic authors. Inter-rater reliability was calculated as percentage agreement. Disagreements were resolved through discussion and when necessary, brought to two additional non-autistic authors. Items were then independently reviewed for wording clarity by an autistic author.

This review was compared against the consensus decision reached by the two non-autistic authors. Disagreements were resolved through discussion.

Clarity of wording was coded as "Acceptable" (i.e., no issues with clarity), "Unclear Wording/Meaning" (i.e., contained technical jargon, such as "idiosyncratic", and/or could reasonably be interpreted in several different ways, such as "becomes upset in a situation with lots of things going on"), "Double-Barrelled" (i.e., a single item enquiring about more than one behaviour, such as "does s/he prefer imaginative activities such as play-acting or storytelling, rather than numbers or lists of facts"), "Internal Experience" (i.e., an item which is asking about a child's internal experience and therefore not observable by an informant, such as "if s/he tries to imagine something, s/he finds it very easy to create a picture in her/his mind") or "Other" (i.e., issue with clarity not otherwise captured, such as referring to the dated social game The Mulberry Bush). We considered questionnaires with at least 90% of items coded as "Acceptable" as having satisfactory clarity.

Flesch Reading Ease (FRE; Flesch, 1979) was also calculated using Microsoft Word (Microsoft Corporation, 2024) to offer a standardized rating of readability. FRE is based on the average number of words and syllables per sentence in a given passage, with fewer words per sentence and fewer syllables per word resulting in a higher FRE rating. A FRE rating of 60 indicates a 'standard' reading level, equivalent to an 8 th or 9 th grade reading level (Flesch, 1979). Therefore, scores of 60 or above were considered acceptable, with a higher score indicating the questionnaire is easier to read. However, while FRE can provide a general idea of reading difficulty, it should not be confused with understandability. A short sentence containing only one syllable words may still be nonsensical, irrespective of a high FRE.

Therefore, it is beneficial to consider the FRE in combination with our assessment of clarity.

Respectfulness of Items

To the best of our knowledge, there is no existing standardised methodology for assessing the use of respectful and neurodiversity-affirming language in item wording.

Therefore, we drew from available guidance on respectful and neurodiversity-affirming language use (Bottema-Beutel et al., 2021; Dwyer et al., 2022; Monk, 2022). Items were coded as "Appropriate" (i.e., no issues with respectfulness), "Could be Improved" (i.e., used potentially insensitive wording for which there are respectful alternatives, such as "rigid" or "inflexible"), or "Problematic" (i.e., used clearly insensitive language, and/or upheld harmful and inaccurate stereotypes about autistic people, such as lacking empathy). As with item clarity, items were first independently reviewed by two non-autistic authors. Disagreements were resolved through discussion and when necessary, brought to two additional non-autistic authors. Items were then independently reviewed by an autistic author. This review was compared against the consensus decision reached by the two non-autistic authors.

Disagreements were resolved through discussion. Inter-rater reliability was calculated as percentage agreement. We considered questionnaires with at least 90% of items coded as "Appropriate" as adequately respectful.

Autism Impact Measure (AIM)

The 41-item caregiver-report AIM questionnaire (Kanne et al., 2014) is intended as an intervention outcome measure of change of core autistic traits in children aged 2 to 17 years.

Unlike the other questionnaires, the AIM's intended purpose is to detect small short-term behavioural changes. As such, each item is rated on two 5-point Likert scales (Frequency: how often the behaviour occurred within the past two weeks; and Impact: how much the behaviour interfered with the child's everyday functioning over the past two weeks).

Only a total score (i.e., summed frequency and impact scores) is calculated, and the authors did not propose subscales.

1. Psychometric Adequacy of Questionnaire

Forward citation searches using Google Scholar for each included questionnaire yielded a total of 11,716 results: ABII-PQ (n = 17), AIM (n = 139), AQ-Child (n = 820), ASDQ (n = 16), CAST (n = 612), GARS-3 (n = 810), SCDC (n = 989), SCQ (n = 1,932), SRS-2 (n = 6,333), SSDS (n = 48). Screening of these citations identified 51 peer-reviewed studies reporting on the development or subsequent psychometric evaluation (factor or component analysis, sensitivity, specificity) of the included questionnaires.

1a) Psychometric Support for Scale Structure

We narratively summarise analytic work on the scale structure for each questionnaire (e.g., EFA, PCA, ESEM) and note whether the analysis supports the proposed structure of the given questionnaire. An overview of key details about scale structure analyses (sample size and characteristics, type of analysis, rotation and extraction method, models tested, and models supported) conducted on the included questionnaires is presented in Supplementary Table 1.

ABII-PQ

No scale structure analytic work could be located for the ABII-PQ.

AIM

Five studies reported factor analysis on the AIM for autistic samples. The original AIM development study favoured an EFA-derived four-factor model that included 25 of the 41 items (Kanne et al., 2014). However, four subsequent studies have demonstrated consistent support through EFAs and CFAs for a five-factor model using 29 of the 41 items (Grimm et al., 2023; Houghton et al., 2019; Mazurek et al., 2020; Zhong et al., 2023).

Furthermore, this 29-item five-factor model demonstrated measurement invariance across sex (Zhong et al., 2023).

Therefore, the research on the AIM to date suggests a 29-item version of the questionnaire with five subscales (Repetitive Behaviour, Communication, Atypical Behaviour, Social Reciprocity, and Peer Interaction) would have stronger construct validity than the existing 41-item version.

AQ-Child

In the initial development of the AQ-Child (Auyeung et al., 2008) with autistic and non-autistic children, three items were deemed difficult to assess in young children and were eliminated from subsequent analysis. The 47 remaining items were submitted to PCA. A four-component solution ─ Mind-Reading (16 items), Attention to Detail (9 items), Social Skills (15 items), and Imagination (7 items) ─ was identified as optimal. Auyeung and colleagues (2008) reported that these components were highly correlated with four of their five original, theoretically derived subscales (Communication, Attention to Detail, Social Skills, and Imagination). Although three of the component names are identical to three of the subscale names, it is important to note the items that mapped onto these components do not align with the subscales.

A subsequent study by Gomez and colleagues (2019) used CFA in an ADHD child sample to test Auyeung and colleagues' (2008) 47-item four-factor model as well as the fivefactor model (with all 50 items) corresponding to the theoretically proposed subscales.

Results indicated poor fit for either model. Subsequently, Gomez and colleagues conducted EFA for one to five-factor models (using 47 items) and found support for a four-factor model using 32 items.

While there is a lack of consistency between the results of the two scale-structure analytic studies conducted on the AQ-Child, neither provides support for the purported five subscale (with 10 items per subscale) structure of the questionnaire.

ASDQ

The development of the ASDQ included exploratory and confirmatory ESEMs, and CFA, to investigate its psychometric structure, with support reported for a nine-factor model with an additional general factor (i.e., bifactor; Frazier et al., 2022). These analyses were conducted with both autistic and non-autistic child participants. Additional reported analyses indicated strict measurement invariance across age groups, sex, race, and ethnicity, providing psychometric support for the nine subscale scores and total score.

In a follow-up study, Frazier and colleagues (2025) used a series of CFAs and ESEMs to replicate their previous psychometric findings. Support was found for the nine-factor model with an additional general factor. Additionally, further evidence of scalar invariance was reported across sex, age groups, race, ethnicity, and diagnosis.

CAST

No scale structure analytic work could be located for the English version of the CAST.

GARS-3

Gilliam (2014b) used EFA on an autistic sample in the development of the GARS-3 scale. The results of their factor analysis, which supported a six-factor model, formed the six GARS-3 subscales. It should be noted that the sample used for the factor analysis included multiple types of informants (e.g. caregivers, teachers, allied health professionals, other family members, advocates and others) and was not limited to primary caregivers of autistic children. Additionally, the factor analysis is only published in the GARS-3 Examiner's

Manual (Gilliam, 2014b), which is not a peer reviewed source. We were not able to locate any peer reviewed studies on the scale structure of the English version of the GARS-3.

SCDC

No scale structure analytic work could be located for the SCDC. While Skuse and colleagues (2005) refer to their earlier work (Skuse et al., 1997) as the development of the SCDC and state that this study reports on a PCA, we could not find any indication or mention of a PCA in Skuse and colleagues (1997). There was, generally, little description of the development of the SCDC.

SCQ

Factor analytic work on the SCQ has produced mixed results. Testing the Lifetime form with participants with a Pervasive Developmental Disorder diagnosis, the original development of the SCQ favoured an EFA-derived four-factor model (Berument et al., 1999).

This four-factor model was supported by Wei and colleagues (2015) in their CFA on the Lifetime form (with an autistic sample). However, Wei and colleagues' CFA on the Current form found particularly poor fit for one of the four factors (Social Interaction). Additional studies have supported a two-factor model (Magyar et al., 2012), using EFA and CFA on the Lifetime form for participants with Down syndrome, with and without autism, and a threefactor model (Grove et al., 2019), using CFA on the Current form with an autistic sample.

More recently, Ozcan and colleagues (2025) were unable to identify a stable factor structure for the Current form using either EFA or CFA. Taken together, these findings indicate substantial inconsistency in the latent structure of the SCQ, particularly for the Current form.

While some EFAs and CFAs have supported two-, three-, or four-factor solutions, other studies have been unable to identify a stable factor structure. To date, research has not examined higher-order or bifactor models to evaluate whether a single total scale score is psychometrically justified.

SRS-2

PCAs on the SRS-2 have yielded results consistent with a single component solution in general population samples (Constantino, Hudziak, et al., 2003 and Wigham et al., 2012) and in a mixed sample of autistic children and children with other psychiatric diagnoses (Constantino et al., 2004). Although a CFA conducted by Frazier and colleagues (2014) in a mixed sample of autistic children, non-autistic siblings, and general population found support for a five-factor model, this model did not show measurement invariance between autistic children and non-autistic siblings. Constantino and Gruber (2012a) note that while the SRS subscales may be useful for measuring response to interventions, the subscales should not be interpreted individually.

SSDS

The development of the SSDS used exploratory ESEM (in autistic participants) and confirmatory ESEM (in non-autistic and other neurodevelopmental and neuropsychiatric diagnoses groups), with results favouring a five-factor model using 40 of the 58 items (Phillips et al., 2019). The five subscales for the SSDS correspond to the five factors identified. Therefore, while there is psychometric support for using the five subscales, use of a total scale score with all 58 items (or 71 items from the extended version) currently lacks psychometric support. It is also worth noting the small sample size used for the exploratory ESEM (N = 175). As structural equation modelling requires five to ten participants per item (Nicolaou & Masoner, 2013), ideally 290-580 participants would be needed.

1b) Questionnaire Sensitivity and Specificity

Aside from the SCQ and SRS-2, there were limited (or in some instances no) peer reviewed studies on the sensitivity and specificity of included questionnaires. We summarise key findings below. An overview of all located studies reporting on the sensitivity and specificity of included questionnaires is presented in Supplementary Table 2.

ABII-PQ

For a cut-off score of 5, the ABII-PQ has shown sensitivity of 97% and specificity of 95% (Ward et al., 2017) in differentiating autistic and non-autistic children.

AIM

No sensitivity and specificity analysis on the AIM could be located.

AQ-Child

For a cut-off score of 76, Auyeung and colleagues (2008) reported the sensitivity and specificity for the AQ-Child to be 97% and 95%, respectively, in differentiating between autistic and non-autistic samples. Frazier and colleagues (2022) reported that at a cut-off score of 2.7, the ASDQ had sensitivity and specificity of 88% and 91%, respectively, in differentiating an autistic sample from a mixed neurotypical and non-autism developmental disability sample.

ASDQ

In a follow-up study, Frazier and colleagues (2025) reported that at a cut-off score of 2.54, the ASDQ had a sensitivity and specificity of 82% and 87%, respectively, in differentiating an autistic sample from a population sample comprised of neurotypical children and children with other developmental disabilities.

CAST

While the sensitivity of the CAST varied greatly between the Allison and colleagues (2021) and the Williams and colleagues (2005) studies (56% and 100%, respectively), the reported specificity was consistently high (100% and 97%, respectively) in differentiating between autistic and non-autistic samples. However, the small autistic sample (n = 9) in Williams and colleagues' study raises questions about the interpretability of their reported sensitivity.

GARS-3

We were not able to locate any peer reviewed studies reporting on the sensitivity and specificity of the GARS-3. However, the approach taken in the GARS-3 Examiner's Manual (Gilliam, 2014b) to systematically differentiate autism groups (autism only or with cooccurring conditions) from various other groups (non-autistic, ADHD, speech-language impairments, emotional or behavioural difficulties) is commendable. Gilliam (2014b) reported sensitivity and specificity as 96% and 95%, respectively, when differentiating autistic children (without co-occurring diagnoses) from neurotypical peers. Skuse and colleagues (2005) reported that at a cut-off score of 9, the SCDC had sensitivity and specificity of 90% and 69%, respectively, to differentiate between autistic and non-autistic (combination of clinical and non-clinical) samples. However, the specificity drops to 35% when differentiating between autistic and non-autistic clinical samples.

SCDC

SCQ

Twenty-seven studies reported on the sensitivity and specificity of the SCQ across various cut-off scores (see Supplementary Table 2), with large variations in results and methodology across the 27 studies making it difficult to discern an overall pattern. Of the 27 studies, six reported on the Current form and 16 reported on the Lifetime form (the remaining five either combined data from the Lifetime and Current forms or did not indicate which form was used).

At the recommended cut-off score of 15, sensitivity of the SCQ Current form exceeded 70% in only one study (DiGuiseppi et al., 2010, reported 100% sensitivity when differentiating autistic and non-autistic groups of children with Down syndrome). In contrast, sensitivity at the cut-off of 15 on the SCQ Lifetime form was generally above 70% (though exceeded 90% in only two studies; Berument et al., 1999; Witwer & Lecavalier, 2007).

Studies reporting sensitivity lower than 70% in the Lifetime form were generally characterised by differentiating autistic children from children with other neurodevelopmental conditions (e.g., Guttentag et al., 2022; C. M. Lee et al., 2023).

Relatedly, the same cut-off of 15 showed specificity values on the SCQ Current form generally above 70%, reaching 92% when differentiating between autistic and non-autistic young children (3 to 5 years; L.-C. Lee et al., 2007). Specificity for the Lifetime form showed greater variation, with six studies reporting specificity below 70%, and the remaining studies reporting values primarily falling within the 70-79% range. The lower overall specificity for the SCQ may be attributable to the complex comparison groups used in most studies (e.g., non-autistic children with other neurodevelopmental conditions).

SRS-2

Eight studies reported on the sensitivity and specificity of the SRS-2 across the multiple specified cut-offs used to indicate different levels of likelihood/severity (60-65 = Mild; 66-75 = Moderate; 76+ = Severe). At the lowest cut-off of 60, the SRS-2 demonstrated a sensitivity of 95% and specificity of 96% when differentiating autistic and non-autistic siblings (Schanding et al., 2012). However, in the context of co-occurring diagnoses, the specificity of the SRS-2 is quite low. For example, Kidd and colleagues (2020) reported a high sensitivity (97%) but low specificity (21%) when differentiating between autistic people with Fragile X Syndrome and non-autistic people with Fragile X Syndrome (4 to 52 years).

Similarly, when screening children referred for an autism diagnostic assessment (for whom there were behavioural concerns), the SRS-2 demonstrated high sensitivity (96%) but low specificity (25%) for a cut-off score of 60 (Moul et al., 2015). However, the ability to differentiate autistic and non-autistic children in a referred sample did improve at the highest cut-off of 76+, with Moul and colleagues (2015) reporting sensitivity and specificity at 82% and 72% respectively. In short, while the lower cut-off of 60 appears adequate for differentiating autistic children from children without a developmental concern, the higher cut-off of 76 may be needed to differentiate autistic children from non-autistic children for whom there is, or may be, some other form of behavioural concern.

Clearly, items relating to more than one sub-criterion confound one's ability to ascertain which specific trait is being measured. Similarly, the incidence of "Unclear/None" items may pose a cause for concern given that endorsement of these items could lead to an inflated score for reasons other than having pronounced autistic traits.

3. Clarity of Item Wording

Inter-rater reliability between the two non-autistic authors was 89%. Inter-rater reliability between the non-autistic authors and the autistic author was 81%. The results for the FRE metric and our item clarity analysis for each included questionnaire are presented in Table 4.

The ABII-PQ had the highest FRE rating (83), with 91% of items deemed to have "Acceptable Clarity". This reflects the simple language used in the questionnaire. However, simple language use does not constitute relevance. For example, while it is easy to understand the item "Do/did you find your child difficult to soothe?", this content is not related to a specific autistic diagnostic sub-criterion. It is therefore important to keep in mind that while readability and clarity were high, 27% of the ABII-PQ's items could not be sorted into one of the DSM-5-TR autism sub-criteria (see Table 3). Whereas the SCQ and SSDS similarly scored well for item clarity, but also contain a greater proportion of relevant items.

The AIM and GARS-3 did not score well on item clarity. FRE indicated complex language across these questionnaires (FREs of 53.7 and 50.2, respectively). Also, some GARS-3 items included technical terms (e.g., "stereotyped" or "affect"), limiting their clarity.

Notably, while the ASDQ has a low FRE, indicating greater reading difficulty, we considered this measure to have high clarity in item wording. This apparent discrepancy is understandable when one notes the items of the ASDQ often include examples to provide clarity. This results in longer sentences and subsequently, a lower FRE.

The SSDS 58-item and 71-item versions had FRE scores of 62.7 and 64, and clarity ratings of 93% and 92%, respectively, based on our evaluation. In addition to our evaluation, Phillips and colleagues (2019) provide further valuable insight into the clarity of the original 58 SSDS items. During their initial administration of the SSDS, respondents provided feedback on items, including item clarity. Phillips and colleagues reported that 88% of the 175 parent respondents indicated no clarity issues, with 12% suggesting some items were unclear.

4. Use of Respectful and Neurodiversity-Affirming Language

Inter-rater reliability between the two non-autistic authors was 82%. Inter-rater reliability between the non-autistic authors consensus decision and the autistic author was 88%. An overview of the respectfulness of item wording in the included questionnaires is presented in Table 5.

Ninety per cent or more of ABII-PQ, AQ-Child, SCQ, and SSDS items were rated as "Appropriate". It is noteworthy that the AQ-Child and SCQ received these high ratings for respectful language despite their earlier development dates. The ABII-PQ and SSDS are among the most recently developed questionnaires, whereas the questionnaires rated lowest for respectful language, the SCDC and the SRS-2 (identical to the original SRS released in 2005), are two of the oldest questionnaires. Thus, there appears to be a positive trend towards increased use of neutral, respectful and neurodiversity-affirming language over time. Irrespective of respectfulness rating, questionnaire items generally portrayed autistic traits negatively. For example, although respectfully worded, the item "Has more difficulty than other children with changes in his or her routine" is portraying a negative side of preference for routines (whereas one could instead ask if a child strongly prefers following routines). In another instance, playing with toys in ways for which they were not intended (e.g., spinning toy cars) was described by a questionnaire item as "inappropriate". Similarly, descriptions of differences in speech ranged from "unusual" to "odd" to "abnormal".

Items relating to sensory processing differences often asked if a child was being "overly sensitive" to sensory stimuli. The use of the term "overly" was considered an unnecessary qualifier which could invalidate a child's distress. The same issue was raised in recent revisions of the Comprehensive Autistic Trait Inventory, an adult autistic trait questionnaire (English et al., 2021), where community reference groups indicated a preference for alternative phrasing (English et al., 2025; Hechler et al., 2025). We suggest these items could be improved by simply removing the term "overly". Alternatively, another appropriate example is one item in the ASDQ which respectfully assesses sensory processing differences by asking whether the child "dislikes" certain sensory stimuli.

Relatedly, though not directly to do with item wording, is the use of the medicalised word 'symptom' in the title of the ASDQ. While it is important to acknowledge difficulties and differences, it is still possible to capture these without resorting to ableist or pathologizing language.

Although not captured in our review of item wording, the items of several questionnaires (AQ-Child, CAST, GARS-3, SCDC, SCQ, SRS-2, SSDS) unnecessarily contained gendered pronouns (e.g., "S/he", "him or her"). In addition to complicating the readability, it excludes non-binary people. Using gender-neutral pronouns may be especially relevant for autistic trait questionnaires, as research indicates autistic people are more likely to be gender-diverse than non-autistic people (e.g., 5.1% of autistic participants expressed gender variance compared to 0.7% of non-autistic participants in Janssen et al., 2016).

Questionnaires such as the ABII-PQ, AIM and ASDQ have demonstrated that using genderneutral language is feasible.

5. Use of Inclusive Research Practices Throughout Questionnaire Development

Inclusive research with autistic individuals was not reported in the development of any of the included questionnaires. In terms of including allies, parents of autistic children were included during the development of four questionnaires with parental feedback collected after the development of another questionnaire.

Though limited detail is given, the authors of the ABII-PQ indicated that questionnaire items were modified after pilot testing with 10 parents (five of whom had autistic children and five who had non-autistic children; Ward et al., 2017). While the ASDQ authors provided the clearest detail on inclusive research practice, the feedback of parents and caregivers was similarly only used to modify items (Frazier, Dimitropoulos, et al., 2023).

In contrast, although the authors of the SSDS did collect parental feedback on the meaningfulness and clarity of items, this was done following questionnaire administration (Phillips et al., 2019) and so did not contribute to item development.

The authors of the AIM stated that while an initial item pool was developed by two of the primary authors, additional items were developed following consultation with autism experts and parents (Kanne et al., 2014). It is unclear how many items were developed based on this consultation or how many parents were involved. Conversely, an initial item pool for the GARS-3 was reduced based on importance as rated by university professors, parents, and clinicians (Gilliam, 2014b). The reduced item pool formed the prototype for initial data collection. The number of parents (or professors or clinicians) included in this process is not provided.

Overall, there was minimal mention of inclusive research. Where this was done, it appears limited to members of the wider autism community (i.e., parents) rather than autistic people themselves. Based on the available information, the level of inclusion never exceeded consultation (Arnstein, 1969). However, it is important to acknowledge that the absence of explicitly described inclusive research practices does not necessarily mean that such practices were not used. We also cannot be certain that any of the authors are not themselves autistic, as it would have been unconventional for this to be disclosed within academic literature historically. That said, given the use of inclusive research practices in autism research has grown considerably only recently, it seems unlikely that earlier work was inclusive at any level. Fortunately, convention is changing. Leading autism journals now encourage or require authors to include a community involvement statement in research submissions, and emerging research indicates there has been a noticeable increase in the report of inclusive research (Tan et al., 2024).

Discussion

In this review we critically analysed ten caregiver-report questionnaires designed for measuring autistic traits in children aged 4 to 12 years. Each was examined in relation to psychometric support for scale structure, sensitivity and specificity in differentiating autism and non-autism groups, item coverage of DSM-5-TR autism sub-criteria, clarity of item wording, use of respectful and neurodiversity-affirming language, and use of inclusive research practices in questionnaire development.

Progress in understanding autism is likely to be impeded if research is based on questionnaires with inadequate scale-structure support. However, with the exception of the ASDQ, GARS-3, and SSDS, factor analytic studies did not support purported scale structure for the included questionnaires. For the ASDQ, GARS-3 and SSDS, the purported subscales were based on factor analyses conducted during scale development. As the development of the GARS-3 (Gilliam, 2014b) included exploratory analysis only, subsequent factor analytic studies are necessary to support the findings. In contrast, the development of the ASDQ (Frazier, Dimitropoulos, et al., 2023) and SSDS (Phillips et al., 2019) included confirmatory analyses. Regardless, further factor analysis conducted by researchers other than the questionnaire developers would still be desirable.

While sensitivity and specificity in differentiating autistic from non-autistic groups are always important considerations, the purpose of using a given questionnaire will influence how stringently one decides acceptable values. Regardless, well-informed questionnaire selection necessitates several studies investigating sensitivity and specificity across groups with different characteristics and for different purposes (as is the case with the SCQ and SRS-2). Decisions on using questionnaires for classification or screening are challenging when there are only a few published studies (e.g., as for the ABII-PQ, AQ-Child, ASDQ, CAST, GARS-3, and SCDC), and especially problematic when there is a complete absence of published specificity and sensitivity research (e.g., as for the AIM and SSDS).

Based on the available literature, and striking a balance between sensitivity and specificity in differentiating between autistic and non-autistic children, adequate sensitivity and specificity has been reported for the ABII-PQ, AQ-Child, ASDQ, GARS-3, SCQ (Lifetime form), and SRS-2. However, when aiming to differentiate between autistic children and children with other neurodevelopmental conditions or behavioural concerns there is considerably less choice due to the limited literature currently available. For the GARS-3, excellent specificity was reported in differentiating between autistic children and children with other neurodevelopmental conditions; however, the reported specificity for differentiating autistic children from children with behavioural concerns was below adequate (Gilliam, 2014b). While the reported specificity for SRS-2 was similarly poor in differentiating autistic children from those with other behavioural concerns, specificity improved to acceptable levels when using the higher cut-off of 76 (Moul et al., 2015).

Following review of DSM-5-TR autism sub-criteria coverage, the ASDQ and GARS-3 were identified as the most comprehensive questionnaires as they included at least three items for all seven diagnostic sub-criteria. Alternatively, for clinicians and researchers focused on Criterion A (social communication and interaction traits), the SSDS presents a thorough option.

While the ASDQ and SSDS demonstrated adequate wording clarity, the GARS-3 did not. This may impact the reliability and validity of the GARS-3, which consequently may impact its usefulness. Similarly, while the SSDS uses respectful language, the ASDQ and GARS-3 had 15-19% of items that could be improved in this regard. While language issues may appear inconsequential to some, the accumulation of even slightly judgemental and invalidating descriptions of autistic traits promotes the marginalisation and discrimination of autistic people (Bottema-Beutel et al., 2021) and may negatively affect the informants' perception of autism and ratings of autistic features.

Finally, based on published information in the included studies, inclusive research practices were lacking in questionnaire development, and if present, were likely restricted to members of the wider autism community. Additionally, where wider community members were included, reported involvement was limited. Input from parents on the ABII-PQ, AIM, ASDQ, and GARS-3 resulted in some changes to item wording, whereas parental feedback on the SSDS was collected only as a part of initial administration, and therefore after item development. While input from members of the wider autism community is a welcome and valuable resource, their input should be alongside rather than in place of the input of autistic people (Fletcher-Watson, Brook, et al., 2021).

Summary of Best Practice Recommendations for Future Questionnaire Development

To guide developers of future questionnaires, we provide a summary of best practice recommendations. Some of the desirable features were not expanded upon in the review due to gaps and limitations in the existing literature. Nonetheless, they are essential for ensuring a robust instrument. These recommendations are therefore intended to complement the findings of this review and offer guidance for more rigorous scale development.

Item Development

Initial item development should begin with a clearly defined conceptualisation of the latent construct(s) being measured. In this review, this conceptualisation corresponded to alignment with DSM-5-TR criteria. It is also critical that items are worded in a manner that is both clear and respectful. Clarity ensures that respondents interpret the items consistently, while respectful language may reduce social desirability bias and support neuro-affirming practices.

Inclusive research practices are especially valuable in this phase. Involving members of the target population, such as autistic people and caregivers of autistic children, can improve the content validity of the item pool and provide essential feedback on item clarity and tone.

Scale Structure and Psychometric Evaluation

Once an initial item pool has been developed, the scale's structure should be examined through appropriate psychometric analyses. In the absence of an a priori model, EFA is typically the first step, followed by CFA in independent samples. Continuing from this, invariance testing should be routinely incorporated to determine whether the factor structure holds across key subgroups (e.g., by age, gender, or diagnostic status; Boateng et al., 2018). Invariance testing was not a feature of the present review because of the limited existing evidence for most of the included questionnaires. Nevertheless, the four reported instances of invariance testing are documented in Supplementary Table 1 (Frazier, Dimitropoulos, et al., 2023; Frazier et al., 2014 Frazier et al., , 2025;; Zhong et al., 2023).

In addition to testing for invariance, the validity of identified factors should be evaluated. In essence, once research has systematically established a stable factor structure, subsequent research should examine whether the resulting factors show meaningful associations with more direct measures of the purported underlying constructs (i.e., convergent validity; Boateng et al., 2018). For example, a factor labelled "social motivation"

should ideally correlate with an independent, validated measure of social motivation.

Commendably, Frazier and colleagues (2025) recently reported convergent validity for the factors of the ASDQ.

Screening Utility

For questionnaires intended for use in screening or diagnostic contexts, it is essential to systematically evaluate their sensitivity and specificity to differentiate the target population from other relevant groups. The approach taken in the GARS-3 Examiner's Manual provides an example of good practice in this area, having established its capacity to distinguish autistic children (with and without co-occurring conditions) from those with other developmental or behavioural presentations (Gilliam, 2014b).

Given diagnostic assessments often integrate information from multiple informants (e.g., caregivers, teachers), future research should also consider whether incorporating questionnaire data from additional sources enhances screening precision. For instance, it may be useful to investigate whether combined caregiver-and teacher-report questionnaire scores improve the ability to distinguish autism from other neurodevelopmental conditions.

Conclusions

Based on the findings from our review and considering all reviewed characteristics together, it appears that the SSDS may be a good option for clinicians and researchers focusing on Criterion A traits, or the ASDQ for coverage of both Criteria A and B. Both of these measures are freely available, making these questionnaires particularly accessible and therefore appealing. However, there are also important limitations to consider. The current psychometric support for the SSDS raises questions about using more than the 40 items from the five factor-derived subscales. Whereas the ASDQ development bodes strong psychometric support, the low number of items within all but one subscale may be a drawback. Additionally, it appears that autistic people were not explicitly included in the development of either questionnaire.

To summarise, there is a need for further development of caregiver-report autistic trait questionnaires for use with school-aged children. As well as ensuring strong psychometric support for scale structure, appropriate sensitivity and specificity, coverage of the diverse range of autistic traits, and clear and respectful use of language throughout (including in the name of the measure, its instructions and items), it is crucial that future questionnaires are developed with autistic people.
