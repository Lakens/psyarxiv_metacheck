Reporting p values imprecisely (e.g., p < .05) reduces transparency, reproducibility, and re-use (e.g., in p value meta-analyses). Best practice is to report exact p-values with three decimal places (e.g., p = .032) unless p values are smaller than 0.001, in which case you can use p < .001.

What we found

We found 8 non-significant p values that should be checked for appropriate interpretation.

Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

Power Analysis Check

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine, Daniel Lakens and Cristian Mesquida

Overall, among all instances flagged as power statements, 90.6% were correct (positive predictive value).

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download information from other preregistration sites, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). Statcheck might still miss one-sided tests that are described in another sentence, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. A TRO uses them for each trov:TransparentResearchPerformance. * run_results$exit_status: the exit status of the process that ran the script (0 = ended normally); NA when the process did not report one (a time limit, or Docker failing to start the container). * artifacts: one row per file, with stage (deposit: as downloaded from the repository, hashed from the downloaded file; before_run: the run folder just before the first script ran, after metacheck’s own changes, such as rewritten paths and unpacked archives; after_run: only the files the scripts made (status new), changed (changed) or removed (removed), as an unchanged file is the one listed before the run), path (relative to the run folder, as valid UTF-8, a byte of another encoding written as <xx>), size (bytes), sha256, mime_type (by extension, NA when unknown), source (the file’s address in the repository, for deposit). The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). Files metacheck itself copied or made between passes (see modifications, output_copied, file_placed) show in after_run too. A TRO uses the rows as trov:ResearchArtifacts and each stage as a trov:ArtifactArrangement. * modifications$sha256_before, modifications$sha256_after: the hash of the changed script as downloaded and as it ran; NA when the script is not at the same path in both (a script made from an R Markdown file). * environment: the system that ran the code (trov:TransparentResearchSystem in a TRO): metacheck_version, started_at, ended_at, host (R version, platform, operating system), sandbox, network_isolated (TRUE only when every Docker run had --network none; the process sandbox does not stop the network), internet, timeout_per_script, total_timeout, python_local (the Python run outside Docker and its version), and docker: runs (for each kind of run, r_run, r_install, python_run, python_install, the image and the docker run arguments, such as --network none, --read-only, --cap-drop ALL, no-new-privileges, the process limit and the memory and processor limits) and images (for each image, its ID and digests from docker image inspect, its creation time and the R and Python versions its environment declares).

It is NA for files run inside another script, files that are not an analysis, files not read, and a Docker failure. summary_table counts each group (repro_ran_n, repro_code_error_n, repro_dependency_n, repro_time_n, repro_not_checkable_n), and the report’s first sentence gives them. The traffic light reflects the static readiness signals by default; a script in code_error that stopped with an error makes it red, as a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure, and a script in one of the other three groups keeps it from being green. Calls that need a screen, which the run does not have (dev.print(), dev.copy2pdf(), dev.copy2eps(), savePlot(), windows(), win.graph(), x11(), quartz(), View()), are replaced before the run by calls that need none, which modifications records as screen_call_replaced. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

While research on children's number development largely focuses on number words (e.g., "two") as an indicator of children's knowledge, less is known about how children acquire knowledge of other number systems, such as cardinal number gestures (e.g., holding up two fingers to represent two objects). Examining children's learning across multiple number systems can shed light on what aspects of number learning may be specific to a particular system or may reflect underlying conceptual development. In the current study, we tested 2.5-to 4-year-olds' knowledge of number gestures and number words (n = 175) and found that children may learn number gestures through a series of knower level stages, similar to the well-established process of number word development. Following approaches used by studies with bilingual children, we found that children's knowledge of number gestures predicted their knowledge of number words, statistically controlling for age and counting ability. We observed this relationship among both the full sample and among only subset-knowers (i.e., children who only understand a subset of the numbers that they can count to), suggesting that children's number word and gesture development may be linked throughout the developmental process. These findings provide insight into the structure of children's number gesture knowledge and how this knowledge may relate to their broader number development.

[div-01]

Developing an early understanding of numbers can pose a significant challenge for children: It typically takes children years to grasp the meanings of just a few small numbers (Sarnecka & Carey, 2008; Sarnecka & Lee, 2009; Wynn, 1990 Wynn, , 1992)). The number knowledge children have gained by the time they enter kindergarten is an important predictor of their future academic achievement and success (e.g., Davis-Kean et al., 2022; Duncan et al., 2007; Geary et al., 2018). While understanding and accelerating this slow progression has been an important area of interest to researchers and educators (see Wege et al., 2025 for a review), these efforts often overlook the fact that most children actually master at least two systems for communicating about numbers by the time they enter kindergarten. In addition to using number words, many children also learn to use gestures as a way to represent quantities (e.g., holding up two fingers to represent two objects; Oswald et al., 2026). Some young children may even begin to learn about numerals as an additional number system (Knudsen et al., 2015).

These number systems provide different affordances that may shape how children learn about them. For instance, in American English, number words are arbitrarily related to the quantity they represent, but number gestures have an exact correspondence between the number of fingers in the gesture and the quantity the gesture represents. This feature makes number gestures a particularly interesting number system to study, as it may provide an easier entry point for children beginning to understand small quantities (Gunderson et al., 2015). More broadly, understanding how children's knowledge of these different number systems develops can help us uncover which aspects of children's number learning depend on which features of any particular system. Comparing children's understanding of two number systems can give insight into why number learning is difficult for children (Wagner et al., 2015) and what factors may support their early development.

Development of number word and number gesture knowledge

Decades of research have identified key features of children's number development (see Wege et al., 2025 for a review). Children learn numbers through a protracted series of steps (Sarnecka & Carey, 2008; Sarnecka & Lee, 2009; Wynn, 1990 Wynn, , 1992)); it can often take several years for children to connect number words with the quantities that they represent.

The dominant understanding of number knowledge pioneered by Wynn (1990 Wynn ( , 1992) ) suggests that most children under two years of age do not yet understand what number words mean. These children, referred to as "pre-knowers," might be able to produce words like "one" and "two" as part of a count list, but they do not understand that these words refer to exactly one or exactly two objects. Children then progress through stages (called "knower levels") as they sequentially learn the meanings of small number words, starting with "one." Their knower level is the highest number word that they understand (e.g., "1-knower," "2-knower"). Children at these stages, which can continue up to "4-knower" or "5-knower," are collectively referred to as "subsetknowers," as they only understand the meanings of a subset of the number words that they may be able to recite when counting. Only after children have progressed through these stages do they learn the cardinal principle (i.e., that the last number reached when counting represents the quantity of any set), meaning that they can accurately create a set of objects of any number within the range that they can count. At this point they are considered "cardinal principle (CP) knowers."

Previous work has overwhelmingly focused on children's knowledge of number words as an indicator of their conceptual understanding of numbers. However, children do not learn about numbers solely through spoken language but instead are exposed to multiple systems for representing numbers. For instance, the word "three," the written numeral "3," and a hand holding up three fingers are all common ways to represent the concept of exactly three things.

In this paper, we focus on number gestures. Both parents and young children use gestures when communicating about numbers (Bender & Beller, 2012; Oswald et al., 2026; Suriyakham, 2007). Number gestures can include many actions, such as pointing to objects as you count or raising fingers as you count. Here we are primarily interested in cardinal number gestures: holding up a certain number of fingers to represent a quantity.

While words for small numbers are arbitrary symbols for representing quantity, cardinal number gestures have iconic qualities: in many gesture systems, including among American English speakers, the number of fingers raised corresponds exactly to the quantity represented. In theory, this should allow children to reason about cardinal number gestures (from here on referred to as just "number gestures") by using one-to-one correspondence, i.e., matching each finger to an object. However, it appears that young children may not fully recognize the iconicity of number gestures. For instance, Gibson et al. (2023) found that children were better at labeling the value of number gestures than the quantities of arrays of shapes, suggesting that they don't simply view number gestures as sets of items (i.e., fingers). An additional experiment indicated that children judged number gestures based on the overall hand configuration rather than the exact number of fingers raised, suggesting that children may view number gestures holistically, similarly to symbolic representations such as number words.

There is some evidence that children may be able to use gestures to label quantities even before they can accurately use number words (Gunderson et al., 2015). Accordingly, researchers have begun examining whether number gestures might support children's broader number development. Some evidence suggests that number gestures may play a role in facilitating children's acquisition of small number words. For example, a study by Gibson et al. (2019) found that when asked to label a quantity, children who produced gesture/speech mismatches (e.g., saying "two" while holding up three fingers) were more often correct in their gesture than their speech. These children were also particularly likely to learn a new number word from an intervention. These findings provide initial evidence of a relationship between children's use of number gestures and their knowledge of number words (see also Orrantia et al., 2022).

However, it is not clear whether children acquire number gestures similarly to number words: specifically, do children learn number gestures through a similar process of knower level stages? To the best of our knowledge, it has not yet been established whether children's number gesture development aligns with a knower level structure. A previous study by Nicoladis et al. (2010) developed a number gesture version of the Give-N task, a typical measure for determining children's knower level in spoken languages. However, they only assessed children's overall accuracy on the task and did not attempt to categorize children into gesture knower levels. Additionally, several studies have conducted Give-N with deaf and hard-ofhearing children using American Sign Language (ASL), another manual number system with some similar affordances to cardinal number gestures (Carrigan et al., 2022; Quam et al., 2025).

However, these studies only differentiated between CP-knowers and non-CP-knowers and did not directly investigate knower levels. In this study, we therefore sought to understand whether hearing children's number gesture knowledge aligned with a knower level structure in order to better understand how gestures may relate to children's number word learning.

Number learning across multiple number systems

In addition to shedding light on an understudied aspect of children's early number (Wagner et al., 2015) and Singapore (Cheung et al., 2025) have found a relationship between children's number knowledge in their primary and secondary languages: Children who know larger numbers in one language also tend to know larger numbers in the other language.

Taken together, these studies suggest that at least some parts of children's early number development may be linked across languages. For instance, children who learn a new number word or concept in one language may be able to transfer and apply that knowledge in a second language. However, these two studies diverge in terms of which stages of number development appeared linked across languages. While in both studies, children who were CP-knowers in one language also tended to be CP-knowers in the other language, they reported conflicting evidence about the relationship in number knowledge across languages among subset-knowers. In Wagner et al.'s (2015) analysis, there was no such relationship among subset-knowers; instead, subsetknowers often varied in knower level between their two languages (e.g., a child who was a 2knower in one language was fairly often a 1-knower or a 3-knower in their other language). In contrast, Cheung et al. (2025) did find weak evidence of a relationship in children's number knowledge across languages among subset-knowers. Wagner et al. (2015) interpret their findings as evidence that in the early stages of number development, learning proceeds independently in each language, suggesting that the protracted process of number development detailed above might reflect the language-specific challenge of matching a quantity (e.g., two) to the corresponding number words (e.g., "two" and "dos"), rather than a language-independent process of constructing a conceptual representation of the quantity two. While Cheung et al.'s (2025) contrasting findings with subset-knowers suggest that more work is needed with children at this developmental stage, both studies support the possibility that learning the cardinal principle may be a conceptual (rather than linguistic) process that children can transfer across languages.

While these studies provide a useful framework for examining questions about the nature of children's number development, they rely only on spoken languages among bilingual children.

Expanding this work to consider the relationship between different modalities-such as speech and gesture-can contribute in several ways to this growing body of literature. Specifically, it offers another opportunity to test the questions raised by previous studies about the challenges of children's number development. Additionally, as number gestures are commonly used in the US, we are able to study a broader population of children, not just those in bilingual families. Finally, the unique affordances of number gestures (e.g., iconicity) may cause them to be learned through a different trajectory from spoken language, potentially allowing us to gain broader insight into how children's knowledge develops across multiple representational systems.

Current study

The current study sought to understand how children develop knowledge of cardinal number gestures and how this development may relate to their acquisition of number words, using the approaches pioneered by studies on bilingual children's number development (Cheung et al., 2025; Wagner et al., 2015). By examining children's knowledge across both modalities, we investigate the nature of number gesture acquisition, how it compares to number word acquisition, and whether knowledge of number gestures may relate to knowledge of number words.

Measures

Give-N Number Words

We ran the widely used Give-N (or "Give-a-Number") task to measure children's understanding of number words (Wynn, 1990 (Wynn, , 1992)). The task assesses children's ability to provide a specific number of objects that the experimenter requests. It is considered a highly diagnostic assessment of children's understanding of small number words and their eventual knowledge of the cardinal principle. To introduce this task, the experimenter showed the child 15 plastic fish and a blue plate, which they described as a pond for the fish. In each trial, the experimenter requested a certain number of fish (i.e., "Can you put [one] fish in the pond?") and waited for the child to respond. Once the child had finished putting fish on the plate, the experimenter asked, "Is that [one]? Can you count to make sure that's [one]? Is that [one]?" If the child indicated that they had not given the correct number, the experimenter removed all fish from the plate and said, "Let's try that again. Can you put [one] fish in the pond?" This process was repeated until the child confirmed that they had added the correct number of fish to the plate.

The child's corrected response was used as their final response.

In the first trial, the experimenter asked the child to provide one fish. Regardless of whether the child's response was correct or incorrect, for the second trial they requested two fish.

Starting with the second trial, the experimenter used a titrated approach, so that if the child answered N incorrectly, the experimenter asked for N-1, and if the child answered correctly, the experimenter asked for N+1, with six being the highest possible number requested (Sarnecka & Carey, 2008; Wynn, 1990). The experimenter concluded the task when the child's knower level could be determined. Children were defined as an N-knower (e.g., a "3-knower") if they: • Succeeded on giving N (e.g., three) objects on at least two out of three trials, • Failed at giving N+1 (four) objects on at least two out of three trials, and • Did not give N (three) objects when asked for N+1 (four) objects more than once.

If children answered correctly on numbers all the way up to six, we considered them a cardinal principle knower (CP-knower). Children who did not meet the criteria for a 1-knower were marked as pre-knowers.

Give-N Number Gestures

We adapted the Give-N task to use number gestures instead of number words (Nicoladis et al., 2010). The introduction to the task was identical to the number words version, but instead of using a number word to ask for a certain number of fish, the experimenter used their fingers to display a canonical American number gesture (e.g., representing two by holding up the index and middle fingers; Gibson et al., 2023). For each trial, the experimenter asked, "Can you put this many fish in the pond?" The experimenter continued holding the number gesture throughout the whole trial. Once the child had added fish to the pond, the experimenter asked, "Is that this many? Can you count to make sure that's this many? Is that this many?" Children's gesture knower levels were determined using the same criteria as their word knower level.

Highest Count

In this task, the experimenter asked the child to count as high as they could. If the child did not immediately start counting, the experimenter said, "We can start together and then you will keep going. Let's start counting with one…" to encourage the child to continue on their own.

If at any point the child stopped counting without having made a mistake, the experimenter prompted them by saying, "Do you know what comes next?" The experimenter concluded the task when the child made a mistake, said they did not know what number came next, or reached 120. Children's Highest Count was scored as the highest number they reached without making a mistake. If children did not continue counting after the experimenter began the task, their Highest Count was scored as zero.

Gesture ID

The Gesture ID task was used to measure children's familiarity with number gestures and, in particular, assess the degree to which they mapped number gestures onto number words.

In this task, the experimenter placed a laptop in front of the child and said, "I'm going to show you some pictures of hands. For this game, please tell me the number in each picture." In each trial, a picture of a number gesture (e.g., a hand with two fingers raised) was shown to the child, and the experimenter asked, "What number is this?" As with the Give-N number gestures task, we used canonical American number gestures for all trials. There were ten trials, one for each number gesture from one to ten. The gestures were presented in a fixed, pseudo-random order. If the child provided multiple responses, their final response was recorded. For gestures six through ten, children commonly gave responses for each hand separately (e.g., saying "one and five" for the six number gesture). In these cases, the experimenter pointed to both hands and said, "What about all together? Can you tell me what number is in this picture?" We calculated children's overall percent of correct responses as well as the highest number gesture they correctly identified without having made mistakes on any of the smaller numbers.

Procedure

Children were tested in a quiet area of the site they were recruited from (e.g., preschool, community center). One experimenter led the child through the tasks while a second researcher recorded the data. Before testing began, children's parents signed a consent form and completed a survey about their child's demographic information. All children completed tasks in a similar order, with the two Give-N tasks first, followed by two other number tasks not included in the current analyses (word and gesture versions of a number comparison task), and then the Highest Count and Gesture ID tasks. The trial order was counterbalanced such that roughly half of participants completed number word-related tasks first (i.e., Give-N words, Give-N gestures, Highest Count, Gesture ID) and the other half completed number gesture tasks first (i.e., Give-N gestures, Give-N words, Gesture ID, Highest Count).

Results

Descriptive statistics and preliminary analyses

Children's average Highest Count was 20.83 (range = 0-120, SD = 25.25). In the Gesture ID task, they answered an average of 54.51% of trials correctly (range = 0-100%, SD = 25.48%).

The highest number gesture that children correctly identified without having made a mistake on any smaller number was, on average, 4.33 (range = 0-10, SD = 2.78). We used this number (from here on referred to as their "Gesture ID score") in subsequent analyses, as it better aligns with the scoring method for the Highest Count task. Analyses using the percent of correct Gesture ID responses are included in the supplemental materials.

There were no order effects between children who received number word or gesture tasks first (p's > .237); we did not include order in any further analyses.

Evidence of cardinal number gesture knower levels

To investigate the extent to which knower levels capture children's cardinal number gesture knowledge comparably to children's number word knowledge, we first classified children into gesture knower levels using the same criteria used to classify children into word knower levels (Sarnecka & Carey, 2008). Then, we examined whether the distribution of gesture knower levels, as well as children's performance on quantities above and below their gesture knower level, were comparable to what we observed for children's word knower levels.

Participants' gesture knowledge ranged across all knower levels (see Table 2 for the distribution of knower levels in each modality). Relationship between number word and number gesture knower levels

The subsequent analyses follow the approach used by previous studies examining number development in bilingual children (Cheung et al., 2025; Wagner et al., 2015). We began by examining whether participants' number word and gesture knowledge tended to align. In the full sample, children's word and gesture knower levels were significantly positively correlated (Kendall's τ = .64, p < .001). We were particularly interested in children who were actively in the process of learning small numbers in at least one modality; therefore, in a follow-up analysis, we excluded children who were pre-knowers in both modalities (n = 13) or CP-knowers in both modalities (n = 52) and found that the positive relationship between children's knower levels remained (n = 110, Kendall's τ = .41, p < .001). Among the full sample, 91 children (52.00%) had the same knower level across modalities, while 62 (35.43%) had a higher word knower level and 22 (12.57%) had a higher gesture knower level.

The percentage of children with the same knower level was somewhat driven by pre-knowers and CP-knowers; when excluding children who were pre-knowers or CP-knowers in either modality, 26 children (49.06%) had the same knower level, 13 (24.53%) had a higher word knower level and 14 (26.42%) had a higher gesture knower level; see Figure 2. Although these analyses suggest that children may often be at the same stage of number development across modalities, previous studies have argued that correlations are not sufficient to suggest that these developmental trajectories are actually linked above and beyond the general increase in knowledge associated with children getting older (Cheung et al., 2025; Wagner et al., 2015). To more directly address the possible relationship between these modalities, we examined whether number gesture knowledge helped predict number word knowledge, accounting for variation by age and counting ability. We chose to set up our models in this direction due to our primary interest in children's number word development, but we also ran the analyses in the reverse direction (i.e., number word knowledge predicting number gesture knowledge); these results are included in the supplemental materials, along with analyses that treat 5-knowers as CP-knowers and analyses including only monolingual English learners.

We first ran ordinal logistic regressions predicting word knower level and gesture knower level separately, including age and the modality-specific index of familiarity with the number system (Highest Count for number words, Gesture ID score for number gestures). These initial models allowed us to examine each type of number knowledge separately before exploring the relationship across modalities (Cheung et al., 2025; Wagner et al., 2015). Analyses were conducted using the polr function from the MASS package in R (Venables & Ripley, 2002).

Following Cheung and colleagues, we collapsed 1-and 2-knowers for the following analyses, due to our small sample of 1-knowers. We also mean-centered our predictors to improve the interpretability of our regression coefficients.

In this analysis, children's word knower level was positively associated with age (ꞵ = 1.08, SE = 0.30, adjusted odds ratio [AOR] = 2.95 [95% CI: 1.64, 5.31], p < .001) and Highest Count (ꞵ = 0.15, SE = 0.03, AOR = 1.17 [95% CI: 1.10, 1.24], p < .001). Similarly, gesture knower level was predicted by age (ꞵ = 1.42, SE = 0.27, AOR = 4.12 [95% CI: 2.42, 7.01], p < .001) and Gesture ID score (ꞵ = 0.43, SE = 0.07, AOR = 1.53 [95% CI: 1.34, 1.76], p < .001). In sum, both number word and gesture knowledge increased with children's age and familiarity with the modality.

Discussion

In a large sample of 2.5-to 4-year-old children, we investigated children's acquisition of number gestures and how this process relates to number word development. Our findings suggest that children may learn number gestures through a similar process as they learn number wordsthat is, by proceeding through a series of protracted knower level stages before gaining an understanding of the cardinal principle. The within-subjects nature of this study also allowed us to observe the connections between these learning processes at an individual level: In this dataset, children's number gesture knowledge was positively related to their number word knowledge even after statistically controlling for age and counting ability. We observed evidence of this relationship even when looking only at subset-knowers, suggesting that number word and gesture acquisition may be linked throughout the early stages of number development.

Children's acquisition of number gestures

We found that the pattern of children's responses on the gesture Give-N task mirrored the word version of the task. In both modalities, children showed high accuracy on trials with lower numbers followed by a steep decline in accuracy for numbers greater than a certain level. This pattern is characteristic of verbal number knowledge, where children have learned number words up until their knower level but have not yet acquired the numbers above it (Sarnecka & Lee, 2009).

This finding suggests that the well-documented process through which children learn number words may also extend to children's acquisition of number gestures. Previous work has shown that the process of children's number word development is similar across different languages and cultural contexts (e.g., Barner et al., 2009; Piantadosi et al., 2014; Sarnecka et al., 2007). However, to the best of our knowledge, it has not yet been shown that children may also learn number gestures through this process. This finding is striking, given the inherent differences in words and gestures as systems for communicating about numbers. In addition to being an entirely different modality (hand gesture vs. verbal utterance), the number gestures featured in this study were iconic, in that the form of each number gesture (i.e., the number of fingers raised) corresponded to the meaning of the gesture, while the number words featured here were arbitrary symbols. The roles of modality and iconicity might be further elucidated by future work exploring the acquisition of number gestures in contexts where gestures are less iconic, such as Chinese number gestures (e.g., Nicoladis et al., 2019), or among children learning signed languages such as ASL (e.g., Carrigan et al., 2022; Quam et al., 2025; Spaepen et al., 2011).

We consider our findings to provide some evidence that children treat number gestures as an additional set of abstract symbols, similar to number words, and may not initially understand that gestures are also iconic (Gibson et al., 2023; Nicoladis et al., 2010). If children fully appreciated the iconicity of number gestures, they should have been able to use one-to-one correspondence to solve gesture trials for sets of every size in this study, matching fingers to objects to provide the specified quantities even beyond their current word knower level. In this case, we would expect to see relative consistency in children's trial accuracy across all number gestures, perhaps with a gradual decrease in accuracy for larger numbers, which present more opportunities for matching errors; by extension, we would expect to see children mostly assigned to higher gesture knower levels, and to observe many children with higher gesture knower levels than word knower levels. In contrast, in our sample we observed a stepwise shape to children's gesture Give-N performance (rather than a gradual decrease), a distribution of children across gesture knower levels that was similar to that of word knower levels, and no systematic advantage of gestures over words in children's Give-N performance. Taken together, we see these findings as largely inconsistent with the possibility that children are taking advantage of the iconicity of number gestures to map gestures to quantities, and instead more consistent with the possibility that children treat number gestures as abstract symbols (akin to number words).

Relationship between number word and gesture acquisition

When looking at individual-level effects, we found that children's number gesture knowledge was significantly related to their number word knowledge, even after statistically controlling for age and counting ability. Our findings build on those from studies with bilingual children, which have found that children's number word knowledge in their primary language predicts their number knowledge in their secondary language (Cheung et al., 2025; Wagner et al., 2015). Following these studies, our findings indicate that the processes of number gesture and word acquisition may be similarly connected.

A key question raised in both studies with bilingual children is when and how number knowledge in one language may transfer to another language. While Wagner and colleagues (2015) observed a significant relationship between children's number knowledge in their two languages among children who had learned the cardinal principle but not among subset-knowers,

Cheung and colleagues (2025) reported weak evidence of this relationship even among subsetknowers. In our sample, we observed a relationship between number gesture and number word knowledge among both our full sample and among subset-knowers: Models including gesture knower level were significantly better at predicting word knower level compared to models including only age and counting ability. While the regression analyses included here may give some insight into what gesture knower levels are particularly associated with increases in word knower levels (following Wagner et al., 2015), our cross-sectional design makes it difficult to draw conclusions about whether and when knowledge transfer between modalities may have occurred. Further work could employ longitudinal or microgenetic designs to more closely examine the development of number word and gesture knowledge and how these learning processes are linked across different knower levels. Nevertheless, our findings can contribute to Wagner et al.'s (2015) broader inquiry into why number learning is difficult for children. As previously discussed, when Wagner et al. (2015) observed no relationship between knower levels across languages among subset-knowers, they concluded that delays in children's small number acquisition may be due to languagespecific challenges mapping number words onto the appropriate concept. Interestingly, our findings and those of Cheung et al. (2025) provide contrasting evidence that subset-knowers' number knowledge may in fact be linked across languages or modalities. For instance, our findings could suggest that once children learn a certain number concept and are able to map at least one symbol onto that concept (e.g., learning that the gesture for "two" refers to exactly two objects), it may be much easier for them to map an additional symbol onto that concept (e.g., the word "two"). In this view, the months spent at each knower level might largely reflect the construction of the next concept (e.g., exactly three) rather than the process of identifying which symbols represent that concept.

Alternatively (or additionally), it is possible that children directly map from one modality to the other (i.e., connecting a number gesture to the corresponding number word, or vice versa).

This possibility is supported by evidence that children and parents most commonly use number gestures in conjunction with number words (e.g., saying "three" while holding up three fingers) rather than using gestures on their own (Oswald et al., 2026). This type of input, which could help children make a direct connection between the two symbols, may help to explain why we saw clear evidence of a relationship between number gesture and word knowledge among subset-knowers. Simultaneous input in these two symbolic systems-gesture and speech-is made possible by the fact that they are occurring in different modalities. This offers a contrast to the case of bilingual children, who may primarily hear number words in different languages during separate utterances (perhaps uttered by different speakers); even if a single speaker uses both languages in the same utterance, these words would be uttered sequentially, not simultaneously. We speculate that these kinds of considerations may help to shed light on why studies of bilingual children have yielded weak or no evidence of a relationship between number knowledge across languages among subset-knowers (Cheung et al., 2025; Wagner et al., 2015).

Implications for supporting children's number development

Our findings also contribute to a broader understanding of how best to support children's number development. Some previous research has suggested that children are able to communicate about quantities using gestures even before they learn the corresponding number words (Gunderson et al., 2015). In our sample, we did not see a clear group-level advantage in gesture Give-N as compared to the number word version of the task; the majority of children had the same knower level across both modalities, with only a small subset (13%) having a higher gesture knower level than word knower level (and 35% having a higher word knower level than gesture knower level). However, this may be at least partly due to our relatively small sample of word 1-knowers, as the previous study observed the advantage of gesture over words primarily among children who had not yet learned the words "two" and "three" (Gunderson et al., 2015).

In addition, even comparable levels of knowledge between number words and gestures are notable, given that young children and their parents use number gestures significantly less often than number words (Oswald et al., 2026). In this context, our findings are consistent with the possibility that, at least for some children, number gestures may be an easier number system to acquire.

Regardless of whether children learn number gestures before number words, gestures may play a role in supporting their continued number development (Gibson et al., 2019; Orrantia et al., 2022). If children's early number acquisition involves developing conceptual representations of small quantities, experiencing frequent input related to those concepts may be important for children (for discussion, see Wagner et al., 2015). Experiencing input in different modalities could provide even richer input for children's conceptual development. For instance, learning both a word and gesture for the concept "two" could help children gather enough information to understand that these symbols both refer to exactly two objects. Furthermore, number gestures may be a particularly useful additional system for helping children understand small quantities; previous work has hypothesized that gestures' combination of iconic and symbolic properties may facilitate children's ability to connect quantities to number words (Gunderson et al., 2015).

In addition to their potential role in directly supporting conceptual development, number gestures may also support children's learning indirectly, by making children's learning process more visible to their most common interlocutors (e.g., parents and caregivers). Gestures may be an easier modality for adults to observe than children's early speech, providing an earlier or clearer window into their developing understanding of number words. In fact, previous research indicates that children's use of number gestures might be especially informative when they are on the cusp of learning a new number, at which point they might particularly benefit from greater number input (Gibson et al., 2019).

Conclusion

Many children, not just those in multilingual environments, spend their early years learning multiple systems for communicating about numbers. The findings from the current study suggest that children's knowledge of number gestures may provide a useful window into their underlying number knowledge (Gunderson et al., 2015). We found evidence that children's number gesture knowledge follows a similar trajectory to their number word learning and that these two developmental trajectories may be linked. Continuing to examine how children's number gesture and word knowledge unfolds could provide broader insight into how learning multiple symbolic systems shapes children's acquisition of abstract concepts.
