Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard), also Internal Review Board - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term); not IEC followed by a number, which is a technical standard - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee/Commission, in the singular or plural - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee, Bioethics Committee/Commission, Ethikkommission - Ethical Review Authority/Unit/Office/Agency - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Human Research Protection Office/Program, Office for Human Subjects Protection - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee(s) on/for (Medical and Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt - Ethics review that was not needed: Ethics review was not required, did not require further ethical review, exempt from formal human research ethics review, no new institutional ethics review

A template line the authors have not filled in (“[Add ethics approval … here.]”) is not a statement.

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

When a statement is expected

The module sorts each paper by where its data came from (the internal .data_source(); the signs and how the rule was tested are described in R/data-source.R): - own: the authors collected data from people or animals, for this paper, in an earlier study of their own, or (a protocol) will collect them; - existing_records: records about individual people that the authors obtained for this study, such as hospital records or a cohort; - existing_unclear: existing data about people from a source the module could not identify; - open_resource: an open resource any researcher can use, such as UK Biobank or NHANES; - none: no data about people or animals (a review, simulation, commentary, theory, tutorial, coding of published papers, or figures about groups); - uncertain: the signs were missing or pointed both ways; - no_text: the paper has no text.

A statement is expected for own, existing_records and existing_unclear. A paper without a statement in these classes, or in uncertain, is flagged. The module reads the text, and the results of power, stat_p_exact, stat_check, repo_check, data_check and prereg_check when they ran before it (run it after them); without stat_p_exact, it finds the p-values in the text.

On 199 PsyArXiv and medRxiv preprints labelled after the rule was fixed, the module decided right whether a statement is needed for 86.9% (the earlier live-data search: 83.4%). Its main errors: other people’s studies described in the words of a new study, records and specimens described only as counts, and open resources the paper does not call open.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 685 tests with 34 instances of inconsistent reporting, Statcheck correctly detected 34 of them, and incorrectly identified 26. Therefore 0% of true instances were missed, and 43% of detections were false positives. See https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/ for more details of the validation.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on 226 Psychological Science papers with 5,019 hand-coded p-values, 438 of them imprecise (scienceverse/validation, branch stat_p_exact). Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). Of the misses, 13 are starred table notes (“*p < .05”) that the module leaves out on purpose and 11 are p-values it did not find; of the wrong reports, 20 are p-values the ground truth does not contain (such as “ps < .05”) and 6 use ≤ or ≥. How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on three hand-coded sets of papers (scienceverse/validation: 249 Psychological Science, 250 Economics and 250 MDPI papers). Of 1,783 non-significant p-values in the ground truth, version 0.4.4.3 found 1,719 (3.6% missed), and 94.8% of its 1,814 detections were in the ground truth. How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. A TRO uses them for each trov:TransparentResearchPerformance. * run_results$exit_status: the exit status of the process that ran the script (0 = ended normally); NA when the process did not report one (a time limit, or Docker failing to start the container). * artifacts: one row per file, with stage (deposit: as downloaded from the repository, hashed from the downloaded file; before_run: the run folder just before the first script ran, after metacheck’s own changes, such as rewritten paths and unpacked archives; after_run: only the files the scripts made (status new), changed (changed) or removed (removed), as an unchanged file is the one listed before the run), path (relative to the run folder, as valid UTF-8, a byte of another encoding written as <xx>), size (bytes), sha256, mime_type (by extension, NA when unknown), source (the file’s address in the repository, for deposit). The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). Files metacheck itself copied or made between passes (see modifications, output_copied, file_placed) show in after_run too. A TRO uses the rows as trov:ResearchArtifacts and each stage as a trov:ArtifactArrangement. * modifications$sha256_before, modifications$sha256_after: the hash of the changed script as downloaded and as it ran; NA when the script is not at the same path in both (a script made from an R Markdown file). * environment: the system that ran the code (trov:TransparentResearchSystem in a TRO): metacheck_version, started_at, ended_at, host (R version, platform, operating system), sandbox, network_isolated (TRUE only when every Docker run had --network none; the process sandbox does not stop the network), internet, timeout_per_script, total_timeout, python_local (the Python run outside Docker and its version), and docker: runs (for each kind of run, r_run, r_install, python_run, python_install, the image and the docker run arguments, such as --network none, --read-only, --cap-drop ALL, no-new-privileges, the process limit and the memory and processor limits) and images (for each image, its ID and digests from docker image inspect, its creation time and the R and Python versions its environment declares).

run_results$checked says what the run of each script shows about the code, in five groups, so that a script that does not reproduce is kept apart from one the check could not test: ran (it ran to the end); code_error (it did not reproduce: it stopped with an error of the code or the deposit, could not be read, or needs a file the deposit does not hold); dependency (a package or program was not available: a package that could not be installed or was not loaded, a program outside R and Python, a system library, or R crashing in a package’s compiled code); time (it did not finish within the time limit for one script, or was not started because the time for all scripts had run out); and not_checkable (it could not be checked automatically: it needs a person, the internet, a key or another setting of the authors’ computer, one platform such as Google Colab, Windows or OpenSAFELY, more memory than the run allows, a file that was not downloaded, a screen, or a program that runs it, or it is a file of a package of the code that Python runs only as a module of that package). It is NA for files run inside another script, files that are not an analysis, files not read, and a Docker failure. summary_table counts each group (repro_ran_n, repro_code_error_n, repro_dependency_n, repro_time_n, repro_not_checkable_n), and the report’s first sentence gives them. The traffic light reflects the static readiness signals by default; a script in code_error that stopped with an error makes it red, as a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure, and a script in one of the other three groups keeps it from being green. Calls that need a screen, which the run does not have (dev.print(), dev.copy2pdf(), dev.copy2eps(), savePlot(), windows(), win.graph(), x11(), quartz(), View()), are replaced before the run by calls that need none, which modifications records as screen_call_replaced. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

Algorithmic bias-the systematic discrimination of disadvantaged social groups by AI systemsis widely documented, yet its psychological foundations remain poorly understood. Here, we describe how algorithmic bias originates from human biases in social cognition and perception as these processes interact with AI systems. We introduce the psychology-centered Human-AI Loop model, which details the psychological mechanisms through which biases are transmitted between humans and AI during both the creation and use of AI systems. We then describe how these mechanisms create feedback loops through which AI systems amplify, perpetuate, and obscure existing human prejudices and inequalities. Finally, we outline psychology-informed strategies for disrupting these cycles and discuss implications for advancing theories of prejudice in the digital age.

Bias in the Machine

From the use of large language models (LLMs) to facial recognition systems and recommender algorithms, AI systems (see glossary) are increasingly used to aid human decisions: what to read, who to date, where to shop, and who to hire, surveil, or punish [1]. Yet growing evidence reveals that the operations of these systems are not neutral; rather, they frequently replicate and amplify existing patterns of discrimination against women, racial minorities, and other marginalized groups-a phenomenon known as algorithmic bias [2] [3] [4] [5] [6].

Examples of algorithmic bias have been seen in many highly-publicized incidents: a criminal risk-assessment tool that disproportionately labeled Black defendants as high risk for recidivism [7]; a hiring algorithm developed by Amazon that penalized résumés containing indicators of being female [8]; and facial recognition systems used by police that were more likely to misidentify people with darker skin tones [9, 10]. While each of these cases concerns errors in an AI system, crucially, these error patterns are not random; they reflect systemic distortions that disproportionately harm socially-vulnerable populations [2, 4, 11].

Concerns about algorithmic bias have prompted new regulatory policy, such as the EU's AI Act, as well as computational interventions [12, 13]. However, most current efforts to mitigate algorithmic bias are limited by an incomplete account of its fundamental source-humans-and the psychological and behavioral pathways through which human biases infiltrate AI.

In this article, we describe how algorithmic bias is rooted in human social cognition and prejudice. Indeed, human biases affect nearly every stage of the AI lifecycle: AI systems are conceived, trained, deployed, interpreted, and consumed by humans whose judgments are shaped by well-documented biases in social perception, evaluation, and decision-making. This suggests that the successful mitigation of algorithmic bias requires an understanding of the psychological mechanisms through which human biases interact with AI systems.

A Psychological Perspective on Algorithmic Bias

From a psychological perspective, algorithmic bias can be viewed as not merely a computational artifact, but as a novel expression of human prejudice via algorithmic computation. That is, it represents the transmission of prejudice between individuals and AI systems via training data, models, and decision-support tools, which in turn generate outputs that reinforce, legitimize, and propagate existing social inequalities-a self-perpetuating loop of bias-promoting feedback [6, 14, 15]. This perspective broadly aligns with sociotechnical accounts, which view the properties and consequences of technology as emerging from its interactions with people, organizations, and social structures-a sociotechnical system [16] [17] [18]. Algorithmic bias can be understood in terms of such a system, such that it involves recursive feedback between AI systems, individuals, and social structures and thus can be mitigated most effectively by interventions that target components of the broader system [18, 19]. For example, Barocas et al.'s Machine-Learning Loop generally describes how societal phenomena, represented in training data, are learned by machine models, which then inform decisions and actions of individuals which, in turn, influence society [19].

However, while sociotechnical frameworks conceptualize algorithmic bias as an interplay of social and computational processes, what remains missing is an account of how, specifically, human biases interface with AI systems to produce and propagate algorithmic bias. Without a mechanistic account of this interface, rooted in psychology, it is difficult to fully explain the nature of algorithmic bias, predict its expression, or design effective interventions.

To this end, we present the Human-AI Loop model (Figure 1, Key Figure ), which emphasizes the human cognitive and behavioral contributions to algorithmic bias within a broader sociotechnical system. Human Sources of Bias in AI Creation

AI systems can only be as fair as the data on which they are trained. When training data are generated, selected, or annotated by humans, prejudices and stereotypes inevitably shape what data are produced, how they are labeled, and which data are ultimately included. Moreover, because these effects are often subtle, they can be difficult to detect. In this section, we describe psychological mechanisms through which human biases enter AI systems during the creation of training data.

Human Biases in Categorization, Judgment, and Decision Making

Many AI systems-including facial recognition, content moderation, hiring, and medical diagnostic tools-are trained on data labeled by human annotators. Because annotation often requires subjective judgments, it is susceptible to the same stereotypes and prejudices that shape human perception and behavior [20, 21]. As a result, the same social biases observed in behavioral studies are likely to be embedded in training data and subsequently reproduced by AI systems-a form of annotator bias [22] [23] [24] [25]. Below, we describe well-documented patterns of human bias that can influence data annotation (Figure 2).

Sociocognitive Biases in Face Perception and Classification

Face classification systems rely on human judgments of facial attributes, such as race, gender, and emotion expression-judgments that can be influenced by a perceiver's social identity, the stereotypes they hold, and their prejudiced attitudes [21, 26], and their cultural background [27]. As a result, face annotation represents a critical entry point for the cultural transmission of inequality into AI systems [28]. Social psychology has documented several ways in which intergroup bias shapes the perception and classification of faces. Cross-Race Effect. People are more likely to confuse or misidentify faces from other racial groups-a robust phenomenon known as the cross-race effect, which is driven by both perceptual expertise and the motivation to individuate ingroup members [29, 30]. These findings suggest that when human annotators label face images used to train AI face classification models (e.g., on their identity or race category), they would be less accurate when labeling outgroup faces, leading models trained on their data to perform more poorly for these groups. In one study examining the cross-race effect in AI systems, face recognition algorithms developed in Western countries performed better in identifying White than East Asian faces, whereas the opposite was true for algorithms developed in East Asian countries-a pattern that may stem, in part, from cross-race biases in data labeling [31].

Hypodescent Bias. Humans tend to classify multiracial faces as belonging to their lower-status groups, particularly when forced to assign a single racial category (as is typical for AI training data labeling) [32] [33] [34]. The bias is stronger among individuals endorsing right-wing ideologies, zero-sum beliefs, and racial essentialism [35] [36] [37]. In the context of AI, this bias would lead human annotators to systematically classify mixed-race faces as belonging to their lower-status category. Existing evidence is consistent with such a tendency: in a study using a visual semantic model (Contrastive Language Image Pretraining; CLIP) to assign racial labels to face images, the model was found to label biracial faces according to their lower-status identity in a hypodescent-like pattern [38].

Gendered Race Bias.

In humans, racial biases also influence gender perception-a bias known as the gendered-race effect [39]. For example, Black faces are more likely to be perceived as masculine, whereas East Asian faces are perceived as more feminine, consistent with White American stereotypes of Black individuals as aggressive and Asian individuals as submissive [40]. This pattern has been observed in studies of gender classification AI, such that gender was more accurately detected for Black men and Asian women relative to Black women and Asian men [41, 42]-a pattern that could be due, in part, to gendered race biases in data annotation.

Stereotyped Emotion Perception.

Emotion judgments are systematically shaped by racial and gender stereotypes: White perceivers more readily perceive anger in male and Black or Arab faces, and sadness or fear in female and Asian faces [43] [44] [45] [46]. Consequently, annotators may systematically mislabel expressions, causing AI systems to learn stereotype-consistent mappings between social groups and emotions.

Perceptual Dehumanization.

One's group membership even influences the extent to which an individual is judged to be human [47, 48]. This bias emerges early in the neural process of face perception, with ingroup faces encoded more readily as faces than outgroup faces [49] [50] [51]. As a result, annotators may be less likely to label women and racial minorities as "people." An extreme example occurred when Google Photos labeled images of Black people as "gorillas" [52]. More subtle forms of dehumanization have been observed in the context of gender dehumanization, such that the category "person" is more strongly associated with men than women in Google image searches [6] and LLMs [53]. Furthermore, image tagging algorithms used for automated annotation, trained on existing human-labeled datasets, have been found to mislabel faces of marginalized group members as "not human" more often than faces of majority group members [54].

Intergroup Bias in Judgments of Harm Prejudices, stereotypes, and ideologies shape people's judgments of harm, such that harmful acts are judged more severely when committed by members of racial or ideological outgroups [55] [56] [57]. This asymmetry has been shown to reflect greater empathy for ingroup members and the tendency to make dispositional, rather than situational, attributions for outgroup members' transgressions [58] [59] [60]. In AI systems, these biases are likely to influence the annotation of transgressive social media posts used to train content moderation systems. Research shows that posts authored by Black or Arab individuals are more likely to be labeled as offensive or hateful, particularly by annotators with conservative ideologies [61], and models trained on these labels reproduce these biases [62]. Similar distortions have been observed when LLMs are used as annotators, presumably reflecting human biases already embedded in their training data [24, 63].

Stereotyping and Dehumanization in Health Care

Historical racial disparities in U.S. health care may, in part, reflect prejudiced, stereotypical, or dehumanizing responses of physicians, which lead them to spend less time with minority patients, underestimate their pain, and provide lower-quality care [64] [65] [66] [67] [68]. Similarly, medical AI systems-trained on data in which such biased clinical decisions and treatment disparities are embedded-have been shown to reproduce and perpetuate these disparities [69] [70] [71] [72] [73] [74] [75]. In one case, an AI health-care allocation system trained on historical data in which Black patients were undertreated was found to significantly underpredict the care they required-an effect that, in turn, reinforced existing health disparities [76].

Stereotyping in Employment Decisions

Gender and racial disparities in employment are driven in part by stereotypes portraying women and minorities as less qualified for workplace roles [77, 78]. Studies show that even when résumés are identical, women and ethnic minorities are less likely to be interviewed, evaluated positively, or hired [79, 80]. Furthermore, stereotypes portraying women and minorities as less agentic and competent create barriers to their promotion and leadership [81, 82]. When a company's hiring and promotion data are used to train AI systems for human resources, these systems learn and replicate these disparities [83, 84]-an effect famously illustrated by Amazon's résumé-screening tool, which penalized female applicants because it was trained on the company's historically male-dominated hiring patterns [8].

Effects of Mental States, Instructions, and Incentives

Annotator judgments are further shaped by situational factors, which can amplify the expression of bias in data labeling. Fatigue, cognitive load, and low accountability increase people's reliance on stereotypes [85, 86], while task instructions and incentive structures influence how annotators interpret tasks and who chooses to perform them [25, [87] [88] [89]. These factors, which can systematically enhance bias in labeling behavior, are rarely considered in dataset construction. Moreover, their risks may be exacerbated by a reliance on annotators in developing countries who may work under conditions of high pressure, precarity, and powerlessness [90].

Human Biases in Language

Human language is imbued with social information, including stereotypes, prejudices, culture, and status-in vocabulary, word choice, dialect, narrative structure, grammar, and syntax [91] [92] [93] [94]. Indeed, when LLMs are trained on human text corpora, they typically reproduce forms of these biases [95] [96] [97]. Research suggests these biases arise through three broad features of language: disparities in who produces text, disparities in who and what is discussed, and systematic associations between social groups and attributes.

Author Disparities

LLMs are often trained on texts from books and newspapers with longstanding representational disparities, in addition to Internet-based texts. Historically, women and minorities have been underrepresented as authors, and many text corpora used to train LLMs (e.g., the Wall Street Journal) remain dominated by male authors writing for older, high-SES, politically conservative audiences [98]. Models trained on such data perform less well on language produced by younger and more diverse populations [99]. An implication of this author disparity is that minority groups are often represented through the lens of majority-group authors rather than by minority authors themselves, further skewing group portrayals [100].

Language data also reflect an overrepresentation of Western, Educated, Industrialized, Rich, and Democratic (WEIRD) populations [101]. Although multilingual resources continue to expand [102, 103], lower-resource languages remain underrepresented and are served by lower-quality models [104, 105]. As LLMs are increasingly trained on AI-generated text, a concern is that these disparities may become further entrenched, potentially leading to cultural homogenization or "model collapse" [106].

Content Disparities

Authorship imbalances are compounded by disparities in who and what is written about in text. Men are discussed far more frequently than women, and these disparities are amplified at intersections of gender, race, and class: for instance, only 5% of common trait descriptors are associated with Black women, whereas 59% are associated with White men [107]. Research has found that words referring to men (rather than to women) are more likely to be closely associated with a default "person" concept (e.g., to the words person or people) in the texts used to train LLMs, indicating that men are typically represented as the default within the resulting models [53, 108].

These disparities are especially pronounced in social media, where political, moralized, emotional, and ingroup-focused content is overrepresented [109]. Engagement-optimizing algorithms can exacerbate these patterns by promoting such content and shaping users' perceptions of what is normative, creating a self-reinforcing cycle of biased content generation.

Group-Attribute Associations

Linguistic biases also arise from systematic differences in how social groups are described in training text. Human language disproportionately associates dominant groups with positive attributes such as competence and leadership, while linking marginalized groups to negative or subordinate attributes. Gender is especially salient because it is explicitly encoded in many languages (e.g., pronouns and grammatical gender) [110]. As a result, gender stereotypes-such as associations of women with domestic roles and men with leadership or technical roles-are reproduced in word embeddings and LLMs [12, 111]. These associations often exaggerate contemporary labor-market realities: for instance, while occupations such as economist, pharmacist, and manager are now approximately 50% women, they remain malestereotyped in LLMs because of persistent associations between men and work [111].

Comparable patterns appear for race, immigration status, social class, and health-related stigmas, with marginalized groups more strongly associated with poverty, criminality, animalistic language, and negative affect [63, 107, 112, 113]. Although the specific stereotypes attached to these groups change over time, their negative valence has remained remarkably stable [114]. Thus, biased group-attribute associations are a pervasive feature of human language and a persistent source of bias in language-based AI systems.

Human Biases in Training Data Selection

The choice of training data is itself a human decision, guided in part by developers' awareness of the social consequences of their systems. Even without intending to introduce bias, developers may select datasets that systematically underrepresent or exclude certain populations, producing models that disproportionately harm those groups.

The tendency to recognize bias in others while overlooking it in oneself has been referred to as the bias blind spot [115]. In the context of prejudice, members of socially dominant groups, such as men or White majority group members, are less likely to notice a lack of diversity [116, 117] or acknowledge inequality and discrimination [118]. This bias would contribute to similar blind spots in AI development, such that developers from socially dominant groups could unintentionally select training datasets that lack diversity or contain systematic biases (Box 1).

Blind Spots in Image Data Selection

Historically, face-image datasets have been heavily skewed toward White faces, leading models to perform worse for individuals with darker skin tones, especially women of color [42, 119]. By contrast, the use of demographically balanced datasets such as FairFace [42] and DiveFace [120] has been shown to improve accuracy across race, gender, and age while reducing performance disparities.

Blind Spots in Language Data Selection

The composition of text corpora-which, as we described above, contain a multitude of human stereotypes and disparities-likewise shapes LLM behavior, and thus the selection of a corpus for model training has implications for bias in a model's performance [95, 96, 121]. Although the increasing inclusion of training data from online sources such as blogs, forums, and social media has helped to broaden demographic representation, these sources have been found to disproportionately contain political, moralized, emotional, and polarized content [122, 123]. As a result, when such datasets are selected to train an AI system, models have been found to overestimate the prevalence, normativity, or acceptability of extreme or divisive content, including overt racism and sexism.

Proxy Effects

Bias blind spots can also contribute to proxy effects-hidden correlations between protected social categories (e.g., race, gender, or age) and other variables that produce discriminatory outcomes despite efforts to remove demographic information from training data [124]. For example, zip code, occupation, education, and consumer behavior often serve as proxies for race, gender, or socioeconomic status because they systematically covary with these characteristics [125]. Recidivism tools such as COMPAS have illustrated this dynamic: although race was excluded from the training data, geographic variables correlated with race were found to reproduce racial disparities in risk scores [126]. Because proxy effects are difficult to detect, bias blind spots can further obscure their role, allowing discriminatory outcomes to persist despite apparently unbiased models.

Temporal Effects

Training data selection is also shaped by when data were produced. Because language, norms, and social attitudes change over time, older texts often include stronger stereotypes than more contemporary texts [63, 99, 127]. Consequently, LLMs trained on decades-old books and news articles are likely to inherit historical prejudices. These temporal effects may be further amplified by the pursuit of scale: expanding training datasets with older sources may inadvertently increase social bias. Like proxy effects, temporal effects arise from structural properties of the data that, due to blind spots, may go unrecognized by developers.

Multimodal Compounding of Biases

So far, we have described distinct human sources of bias and their independent effects on AI systems. However, many contemporary AI systems are multimodal, integrating text, images, numerical data, and human-generated judgments [128]. In such systems, biases introduced at different stages and in different modalities do not merely coexist-they compound through multiplicative statistical effects during algorithmic tuning [129, 130]. For example, a medical decision-support system may combine radiological images, clinical notes, prior diagnoses, and demographic or environmental risk factors. If each input includes human bias-such as underrepresentation of minority patients in imaging datasets, biased language in clinicians' notes, and historically unequal access to care encoded in medical records-the resulting model may amplify disparities more strongly than any single data source [131]. Multimodal compounding effects have been observed particularly in text-to-image systems, such that textbased prompts have been shown to produce exaggerated disparities in AI-generated images [132] and image-based search results [130, 133].

Multimodal compounding also obscures the human origins of algorithmic bias. Errors may appear to arise from complex model interactions rather than identifiable human inputs, diffusing accountability and contributing to algorithmic implicit bias (Box 2). Understanding how human biases accumulate across modalities will be essential for diagnosing algorithmic bias and designing effective interventions.

Human prejudices are transmitted to AI systems through multiple psychological mechanisms that shape data annotation, language production, and other human behaviors involved in creating training data (Table 1). Although these mechanisms often interact in complex ways that obscure their effects, identifying them provides a foundation for understanding, predicting, and ultimately reducing algorithmic bias.

Human Sources of Bias in AI Consumption

The second major entry point for human sources of algorithmic bias occurs during the consumption of AI outputs: how users perceive, interpret, and act on algorithmic recommendations. These responses are shaped by well-established processes in social cognition, motivation, and decision making that influence whether AI outputs are trusted, scrutinized, or rejected. They also generate behavioral feedback-for example, through clicks, accepted recommendations, and interactions with generated content-that is incorporated into subsequent model training and optimization [134]. Thus, psychological processes not only determine whether biased outputs influence human judgments and behaviors; they also contribute to the continual reinforcement of AI bias through human feedback.

Overreliance and Trust in AI

A key determinant of whether biased AI outputs translate into real-world discrimination is the degree to which they are trusted and accepted by users. Although trust in AI systems varies across individuals and contexts, most studies find that human users trust results more when they come from an AI than a human [135] [136] [137]. This perception of computational outputs as inherently impartial has been referred to as AI's veneer of objectivity [138].

When AI systems are perceived as fair, objective, or technically superior, users are more likely to accept their recommendations uncritically, allowing biased outputs to influence consequential decisions [139, 140]. Indeed, studies show that people judge decisions containing race and gender disparities to be more fair when these decisions are attributed to an algorithm as opposed to a human [141]. Although people generally object to the use of AI for morallyrelevant decisions [142, 143], they nevertheless express less outrage in response to discriminatory results when made by an algorithm compared with a human [144].

Trust in AI also varies across individuals: users with less knowledge or experience with AI show greater trust in algorithmic outputs [145, 146]; trust differs by age, with children and older adults relying more on internet search results than younger adults [147]; and men report greater trust in AI than women on average [148].

Trust effects are further reinforced by cognitive offloading-the tendency to delegate cognitive effort to external tools. Research shows that when people perceive an automated system as reliable, they monitor its performance less carefully and are therefore less likely to detect errors or question its recommendations [149]. Similarly, in the context of AI, greater reliance on AI decision tools has been associated with lower critical thinking, particularly among younger and less-educated users [150]. These findings suggest that cognitive offloading could impair the detection of algorithmic bias, given psychology research showing that bias detection is critical for engaging the self-regulation of prejudiced responses [151, 152].

Finally, disparities in AI trust and adoption may themselves generate inequality. Differences in access to, familiarity with, or willingness to use AI can produce unequal benefits from AIassisted decision making, potentially reinforcing existing social inequalities [153]. For example, gender differences in internet use have been linked to wage disparities in China [154], suggesting that unequal adoption of AI technologies may reinforce existing social inequalities.

Motivated Reasoning and Confirmation Bias

Even when users trust AI, they do not trust all outputs equally. People's decisions are often shaped by motivated reasoning-the tendency to process information in ways that serve one's personal or group-based goals [155, 156]. This tendency reflects confirmation bias, whereby individuals seek and accept information that supports preexisting beliefs [124, 157]. Consistent with a confirmation bias in AI, research finds that people are more likely to trust and implement algorithmic outputs that align with their existing beliefs, while discounting or rejecting outputs that conflict with them [158] [159] [160]. This pattern of confirmation bias is exacerbated by the confident tone of many generative AI systems [161], and bias blind spots may further prevent users from recognizing these influences [115]. Although research has also identified an algorithm aversion effect-the greater tendency to avoid an algorithm after it is observed to err, relative to a human information source-other work suggests that users will prefer an AI when they believe a human's decisions could be biased against them (e.g., when a man evaluates a female job candidate) [160]. These findings suggest that, as a result of confirmation bias, stereotype-reinforcing AI outputs are more likely to be accepted, whereas stereotypeinconsistent outputs are more likely to be discounted.

Cognitive Heuristics, Ranking Effects, and Social Norms

Even in the absence of motivated reasoning, cognitive heuristics shape how AI outputs influence judgment. The availability heuristic causes people to rely disproportionately on information that is salient or easily retrieved, whereas anchoring causes early information to exert disproportionate influence on subsequent judgments [162]. Because AI systems prioritize and rank information rather than presenting it neutrally, they can exploit availability and anchoring heuristics to shape users' judgments. These decision biases have been applied to the context of AI to explain patterns of user interaction, such as the tendencies to rely more on topranked search results, default recommendations, and the first response generated by a language model [124, 163].

Algorithm-driven search results and recommendations also communicate social norms. For example, these systems have been shown to exhibit a popularity bias, such that they disproportionately promote already-popular items, thus reinforcing existing exposure inequalities [164]. In the context of social stereotypes, experimental studies show that when search results show a greater proportion of male or White individuals working in a particular job, a user is more likely to believe that men and White people are more suitable for that profession [6, 139]. Popularity indicators such as most viewed, trending, or highly ranked recommendations further signal what other people appear to value or endorse, creating a conformity bias [165]. Decades of social psychology show that individuals conform to perceived norms, particularly under uncertainty [166, 167], suggesting that ranking effects and popularity indicators can strongly influence a user's beliefs and decisions.

Individual Differences in Prejudice and Ideology

Up to this point, we have described cognitive biases that can lead well-intentioned users to adopt or internalize discriminatory AI outputs. However, explicit prejudices, extreme ideologies, and opposition to equality can also directly shape AI use. Decades of research show that individuals higher in prejudice are more likely to endorse stereotypes, discriminate against outgroups, and justify existing social hierarchies [168, 169]. Although overt expressions of prejudice have decreased since the mid-20th century due to changing norms, blatant racism and sexism still persist and continue to drive discrimination [170] [171] [172].

Our review of human biases in the consumption of AI results suggests at least two ways explicit prejudice could influence AI use. First, prejudiced users might deliberately seek out and implement AI outputs that promote discriminatory aims or confirm existing stereotypes while discounting contradictory recommendations. Second, AI systems can provide justification and plausible deniability for intentional discriminatory decisions. Rather than expressing prejudice directly, users could attribute unequal outcomes to an ostensibly objective algorithm-a deliberate form of bias laundering (see Box 2).

Although these predictions will require empirical investigation, they suggest that individual differences in prejudice and ideology shape not only whether biased AI outputs are accepted, but also whether they are used to maintain and legitimize existing social inequalities. Within the Human-AI Loop model, prejudice serves as a motivational force that perpetuates bias between AI systems, individuals, and society.

Section Summary

The effects of AI depend not only on algorithms but also on the psychology of their users. Trust, motivated reasoning, cognitive heuristics, and prejudice determine how algorithmic outputs are interpreted and acted upon (Table 2). Because these responses also generate behavioral data that inform future model training, these psychological processes both implement and reinforce algorithmic bias through the Human-AI Loop.

Closing the Loop: Amplification of Bias Through Human-AI Interactions

The human sources of bias we have described in AI creation and AI consumption are not independent-they reinforce one another over time. Human prejudices are embedded in training data; biased data shape model outputs; and these outputs, in turn, influence human beliefs and behavior, propagating societal disparities while generating new data that feed subsequent models. Over time, this feedback loop can entrench and amplify inequalities while producing qualitatively new forms of bias (Box 3). Empirical studies are now beginning to show evidence for full-loop effects-between social systems, AI, and human users-as well as an amplification effect attributable to the use of AI.

A full-loop account of algorithmic bias was proposed and tested by Vlasceanu and Amodio in the context of gender stereotypes and workforce disparities, such that societal-level gender inequality would shape AI-driven search engine results, which in turn would lead users to act in ways that reinforce this inequality [6]. To test Stage 1 of this loop-from societal disparities to AI system performance-they first obtained national gender inequality rankings from the World Economic Forum's annual Gender Gap Report [173]. They then conducted Google Image searches for the term "person" (via VPN, in each country's dominant language) and found that searches conducted in countries with higher gender inequality rankings yielded higher proportions of men to women in search results. Next, to test Stage 2 of the loop-from search output disparities to users' gender beliefs and discriminatory hiring decisions-they conducted a series of experiments in which participants were exposed to Google image search results depicting people in novel occupations, with gender proportions matched to those of either high-inequality or low-inequality countries. Participants exposed to search results pegged to high-inequality countries were more likely to assume workers in the occupation were male, believe men were more likely to be hired in that occupation, and choose men over women when making their own hiring decisions-beliefs and actions that would reinforce the societallevel disparities observed in Stage 1.

Although outside the context of social bias, this pattern of bias propagation through human-AI loops has been demonstrated in a closed-loop system of human-AI interaction. In studies of perceptual judgments-regarding the movement of dot patterns or appearance of facial emotions-subtle biases in human judgments were found to be amplified in an AI classification model trained on these data [139]. When a new sample of human participants was exposed to the exaggerated outputs from this AI model, these participants formed even more biased beliefs. These findings show that human biases are not merely reproduced through human-AI loops, but amplified. Similar amplification effects are likely to emerge in the context of social biases.

A key implication of human-AI loops is that the effects of human-AI interactions extend beyond individual users to reshape broader social and cultural structures [6, 18, 19]. As people increasingly rely on AI to select information, recommend opportunities, and guide consequential decisions, algorithmic outputs influence the distribution of resources, social interactions, and cultural representations. For example, biased hiring algorithms can alter workforce composition, recommender systems can amplify media exposure and political polarization, and search engines or generative AI can reinforce stereotypes by repeatedly presenting them as normative or accurate [6, 123, 130, 134, 139]. Thus, AI systems do not merely reflect existing prejudice-they reshape the social world. The result is a self-reinforcing Human-AI Loop in which psychological biases, algorithmic outputs, and societal structures continually coevolve, potentially producing novel forms of discrimination.

Although our emphasis has been on human sources of algorithmic bias, these processes operate alongside biases that may emerge from the algorithm itself. Even holding human inputs constant, technical choices-including model architecture, feature representations, objective functions, optimization procedures, hyperparameters, and evaluation criteria-shape what patterns models learn, which errors they prioritize, and what information is amplified or ignored [2]. The modality of information represented in an AI system can itself contribute to bias; for example, the use of imagery, compared with language, tends to accentuate gender differences, and research shows that gender disparities in occupational associations are exaggerated in image-based internet searches compared with language-based searches [130]. Thus, algorithmic bias reflects not only human social and psychological patterns but also technical decisions and data characteristics that shape how information is represented and transformed [2, 11, 13, 18, 174, 175].

These three major levels of analysis-individual-level psychological processes that shape AI creation and consumption, computational processes that transform human biases into algorithmic outputs, and societal structures that are shaped by these outputs-are integrated by the Human-AI Loop model proposed here into a unified account of algorithm-mediated discrimination (see Box 3). By specifying the psychological mechanisms operating at each stage of this cycle-particularly at the points of AI creation and consumption-our framework helps to explain how these levels become dynamically interconnected to reproduce, transform, and amplify social disparities through human-AI interactions.

Breaking the Loop: Implications for Bias Reduction

Traditional human-in-the-loop approaches assume that human oversight improves AI systems [176]. The caveat, however, is that humans are themselves prone to bias, and thus human oversight is not inherently bias-reducing [18, 19, 177]. Our analysis suggests that reducing algorithmic bias requires interventions targeting the psychological mechanisms that introduce, amplify, and perpetuate bias throughout the Human-AI Loop, rather than relying on technical fixes alone. By describing these psychological mechanisms at different points in the loop, this framework helps identify where human interventions are most likely to be effective. Here, we describe samples of intervention strategies from psychology targeting both the creation and consumption stages of algorithmic bias.

Interventions Targeting the Creation of AI Interventions targeting AI creation focus on the social-cognitive processes underlying data generation, dataset selection, and institutional priorities.

Promoting Fair and Diverse Annotation

Because many AI systems rely on human-labeled data, reducing bias in annotation is critical. Recruiting demographically and culturally diverse annotators broadens the perspectives informing labels and reduces the dominance of majority viewpoints. Annotation protocols can incorporate bias-awareness prompts, clear task instructions, and structured practice with feedback-procedures shown to reduce reliance on heuristics and stereotypes [88, 89]. Practical factors such as fatigue, time pressure, distraction, and incentives should also be managed, as these conditions increase reliance on cognitive shortcuts.

Alignment procedures that discourage models from assigning different labels solely because of group identity can further improve fairness when biases are well defined and measurable [13]. However, they are less effective when bias depends on indirect, culturally specific, or contextdependent meanings that cannot be isolated through simple label or identity swaps [95] (Bai et al., 2025). Transparency is therefore essential: documenting annotator demographics, cultural context, and task instructions can clarify the interpretive frame embedded in training labels and inform downstream model evaluation [178].

Reducing Bias in Dataset Selection

Bias can also enter AI systems through the choice of training data. The choice of dataset should therefore be guided by the intended use of the system. For example, AI tools designed for cross-cultural deployment require training data that adequately represent relevant populations. Moreover, proportional representation is often insufficient; achieving comparable performance across demographic groups frequently requires equal representation in training data, as demonstrated for face classification models [42].

For LLMs, curating training corpora to reduce prejudice, expanding multilingual resources, and incorporating underrepresented communication modalities (e.g., sign language) can improve demographic and cultural coverage during model alignment [179] [180] [181] [182]. As with annotation, documenting dataset composition, geographic origin, annotator demographics, and historical context helps developers and users identify potential sources of bias [178]. Collectively, these practices shift dataset construction from maximizing scale alone toward maximizing representativeness and fairness.

Interventions Targeting the Consumption of AI

Although human prejudice may exert its strongest effects during AI creation, those input-stage biases are often difficult to detect and modify. Psychology-based interventions may therefore be more effective at the output stage, where users interpret and implement AI recommendations through their judgments and behavior. Here, we describe individual-level and structural-level interventions, keeping with prejudice reduction research that suggests a combination of both is most effective [183, 184].

Individual-Level Interventions

Although we discussed the risks of value alignment procedures above, several studies show that exposure to AI outputs that challenge a user's stereotypes or prejudices can reduce bias in their beliefs and decisions (Box 4) [6, 132, 185]. Thus, to the extent value alignment is effective, it can further disrupt algorithmic bias at the consumption stage by eliciting a user's thoughtful engagement with AI system outputs.

Prejudiced responses can be mitigated effectively through self-regulation-that is, through interventions that enhance a person's awareness of bias and ability to detect and avoid it through cognitive control [151, 152, 186]. This work suggests that, in the context of AI, bias detection could be enhanced by increasing a person's awareness of the harms of algorithmic bias and the ways in which it manifests in AI systems. Additionally, the success of a selfregulation intervention benefits from a clear plan of action, such as an implementation intention (e.g., "If I encounter an AI recommendation involving a protected social group, then I will verify it using an independent source") or other proactive control strategies (e.g., to avoid forms of AI altogether) [187] [188] [189].

Insights from judgment and decision-making research further suggest strategies to reduce overreliance on biased AI outputs. For example, instructions that encourage users to consider alternative hypotheses, generate counterfactuals, or explicitly justify decisions have been shown to reduce anchoring and confirmation biases and the overreliance on salient algorithmic outputs [163, 190]. Other work suggests that interventions can be used effectively to calibrate one's trust in AI, rather than to simply reduce it, by helping users distinguish situations in which algorithmic recommendations are likely to be reliable from those requiring greater scrutiny [140, 191].

Structural Interventions

Structural interventions reduce bias by altering decision environments and procedures rather than targeting individual-level attitudes and self-regulation [192, 193]. Similar strategies can be applied to AI use. For example, user interface designs that present multiple alternatives, display uncertainty estimates, restrict systems to specified decision contexts (i.e., to avoid inappropriate uses), or require verification against non-AI sources may reduce overreliance on single outputs [164, 194].

At the policy level, transparency requirements, documentation standards, and algorithmic impact assessments can serve as structural guardrails that shape how AI systems are interpreted and applied [195]. Such measures not only constrain technology but also raise awareness of how AI outputs are perceived, evaluated, and implemented. These measures can be complemented by technological tools such as automated bias detection systems [196, 197] and personalized AI agents designed to steer users away from harmful outcomes [198, 199]. However, the widespread adoption of regulations to reduce algorithmic bias across platforms and industries will ultimately depend on their inclusion in national and international legislation [200, 201].

Section Summary

From the perspective of the Human-AI Loop, algorithmic bias cannot be mitigated by changing algorithms alone. Because human psychological processes shape both the creation and consumption of AI systems, they should be viewed not only as a source of algorithmic bias but also as a primary target for intervention. Accordingly, effective bias mitigation requires coordinated psychological, computational, organizational, and policy interventions across the AI lifecycle.

Toward a Psychology of Algorithmic Bias

More broadly, our analysis highlights the central role of psychology-its theories, methods, and perspectives-in understanding and addressing algorithmic bias. If biases expressed by AI systems originate in human cognition, motivation, and social structure, then algorithmic bias cannot be fully explained or mitigated without psychological science. This perspective calls for closer collaboration between computer scientists, data scientists, and social and cognitive psychologists. Psychology offers mechanistic theories of perception, categorization, stereotyping, motivated reasoning, judgment, and decision making that explain how human biases interact with AI systems during both their creation and consumption. Furthermore, it illuminates how aspects of model design that may seem irrelevant to human bias-for example, data selection, model design, and evaluation-also rely on human judgments that are vulnerable to bias [2]. A psychological perspective therefore complements computational analyses by explaining how technical failures emerge from, interact with, and ultimately influence human cognition and behavior.

Advancing a psychology of algorithmic bias will require studying the full Human-AI Loop rather than its isolated components. This includes understanding how psychological biases shape dataset construction, how people interpret and act on AI outputs, how these interactions generate new training data, and how AI systems reshape social norms and institutions over time (see Outstanding Questions). Addressing these questions will require closer collaboration across disciplines as well as training that integrates computational methods with theories of human cognition and social behavior.

Concluding Remarks

Despite its computational expression, algorithmic bias is fundamentally rooted in human psychology. The psychology-centered model of the Human-AI Loop described here addresses the specific ways in which algorithmic bias emerges from dynamic interactions between humans and AI. By identifying the psychological pathways through which human biases enter AI systems during both their creation and use, this model provides a more complete account of how algorithmic discrimination emerges and persists. Ultimately, achieving fairer AI will require treating algorithmic bias not only as a computational challenge but also as a psychological one. Incentives and Market Pressures

Organizational culture interacts with market pressures to exacerbate bias toward historically disadvantaged groups. AI systems are often optimized for engagement, growth, or efficiency rather than fairness or broader social impact [109]. In the absence of regulatory constraints, bias mitigation may be deprioritized, particularly when harms affect groups with limited economic or political power.

Organizational Power and Accountability

Organizational power dynamics also shape algorithmic bias through relationships with annotators, contractors, and users. Annotators-often low-paid and geographically distanthave limited power to raise ethical concerns [90, 207], increasing the likelihood that problematic assumptions remain embedded in AI systems. The emergence of AI auditing reflects growing recognition that organizational structures are critical targets for mitigating algorithmic bias [208].

Changing Organizational Cultures

Reducing algorithmic bias requires organizational cultures that value fairness alongside accuracy, efficiency, and profitability. This includes increasing demographic and disciplinary diversity, incentivizing the identification of potential harms, empowering employees and external auditors to raise concerns, and embedding fairness into organizational evaluation [209]. Regulatory frameworks such as the EU AI Act can reinforce these changes by aligning organizational incentives with societal goals.

Box 2. Algorithmic Implicit Bias & Bias Laundering (334/400 words)

According to the Human-AI Loop model, algorithmic bias is not merely an artifact of computation, but a novel expression of human prejudice via the mediating interface of AI. This perspective reimagines the construct of implicit bias within the context of AI: whereas psychological theories conceptualize implicit bias as an intra-individual phenomenon-the indirect expression of a person's prejudices or stereotype associations in their behavior [210] [211] [212]-algorithmic bias can be viewed as a multi-agent phenomenon in which prejudice is transmitted between independent human and computational entities; that is, an algorithmic implicit bias.

Research using the Word Embedding Association Test (WEAT), AI chatbots, and Google search has demonstrated the first stage of algorithmic implicit bias: the transmission of human social biases to AI systems [6, 95, 96, 130, 133]. Complementary work showing that biased AI outputs alter users' stereotypes and social judgments demonstrates the second stage: transmission of bias from an algorithm to human cognition [6, 139]. The full expression of algorithmic implicit bias occurs when prejudice passes from one set of humans to AI and, unwittingly, to another human who is unaware of its origin.

Unlike human forms of implicit bias, which may involve a lack of awareness or intention [213, 214], algorithmic implicit bias refers not to processes within the mind of a single actor, but to the indirect transmission of prejudice between separate human and computational agents. This indirect, multi-agent transmission makes algorithmic implicit bias particularly insidious: because prejudice becomes obscured by AI's opacity and veneer of objectivity [114, 191], its origins in human prejudice are easily masked-an effect we term algorithmic bias laundering Although algorithmic bias laundering often occurs inadvertently, it can also be exploited intentionally. Developers, organizations, or end users may knowingly deploy biased training data or invoke biased outputs to advance discriminatory goals while attributing the results to an ostensibly objective AI system. Whether inadvertent or intentional, algorithmic bias laundering obscures responsibility and makes discrimination harder to detect and correct.

This perspective extends the concept of implicit bias from a property of individual cognition to one of sociotechnical systems. In doing so, it provides a psychological framework for understanding how prejudice is transmitted, obscured, and perpetuated through interactions among humans, AI, and social institutions. AI systems learn, represent, and express social bias in ways that, while distinctly computational, make contact with both individual and structural processes. Like individuals, they learn from experience-in this case, large collections of human behavior, language, and judgments-and express these learned associations in interactions with users. But like societal structures, AI systems encode statistical regularities aggregated across collectives rather than the beliefs of any single individual, akin to culturally shared stereotypes that persist even when rejected by individuals. Because algorithmic decisions are often perceived as objective or institutionally sanctioned, AI systems can shift responsibility for discriminatory outcomes from people to opaque technological infrastructures, legitimizing and stabilizing existing inequalities.

By bridging these levels, AI systems provide a new pathway through which prejudice is transmitted between individual minds and social structures. By formalizing social biases into computational representations and reinserting them into everyday decisions, they link cognition, culture, and institutions in a recursive feedback loop. This computational level expands theories of prejudice beyond traditional individual-societal frameworks, positioning AI not merely as a reflection of existing bias but as an active component in its generation, maintenance, and amplification.

Box 4. Can AI Reduce Bias in Humans?

An early hope for AI was that it would help to reduce human prejudices by removing bias from the decision process. Yet, so far, it appears the opposite has been true. Although efforts to remove bias from algorithms themselves (e.g., through value alignment) have met with limited success [95, 215], emerging research points to alternative ways AI can be used to reduce bias.

Changing the Information People Encounter

Studies show that exposure to fair or counter-stereotypic AI outputs reduces bias in a user's judgment [6, 132, 185]. For example, users exposed to Google search results showing a higher proportion of women in a profession were more likely to view women as prevalent and hirable in that occupation [6]. Similarly, counter-stereotypical AI recommendations have been shown to reduce or reverse gender-biased first impressions, particularly when accompanied by explanations [185]. These findings suggest that exposure to AI results presenting diverse and counter-stereotypical representations-which may also be more accurate [133]-can reduce bias in a user's judgments and decisions.

Using AI to Open Minds

AI may also help people recognize their own biases and engage more thoughtfully with social issues. For example, people were more likely to recognize and revise bias in their own thinking when the bias was seen as originating from an algorithm rather than themselves [216]. This suggests that AI can be used as a tool to illuminate bias blind spots. AI can also deliver personalized interventions targeting the psychological processes underlying prejudice. Building on moral reframing research [217], one study found that AI conversations tailored to participants' moral values increased support for transgender rights [218]. Other research found that chatbots trained to challenge participants' political views reduced issue polarization, while chatbots demonstrating conversational receptiveness improved attitudes toward political opponents [219]. These uses of AI suggest scalable interventions to promote greater awareness about bias and outgroup perspectives-responses which, in past work, have been shown to support sustainable prejudice reduction [188].

Challenges and Future Directions

Although promising, these approaches face formidable challenges from biases already entrenched in both AI systems and human judgment. Moreover, whether AI-based interventions produce lasting change remains unclear. Thus, an important goal for future research is to explore such alternative uses of AI for prejudice reduction, in ways that are both ethical and sustainable.
