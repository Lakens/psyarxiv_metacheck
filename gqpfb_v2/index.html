Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

Overall, among all instances flagged as power statements, 90.6% were correct (positive predictive value).

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on 226 Psychological Science papers with 5,019 hand-coded p-values, 438 of them imprecise (scienceverse/validation, branch stat_p_exact). Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). Of the misses, 13 are starred table notes (“*p < .05”) that the module leaves out on purpose and 11 are p-values it did not find; of the wrong reports, 20 are p-values the ground truth does not contain (such as “ps < .05”) and 6 use ≤ or ≥. These figures measure agreement with the hand-coding, which has known errors in both directions (scienceverse/validation#48).

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on three hand-coded sets of papers (scienceverse/validation: 249 Psychological Science, 250 Economics and 250 MDPI papers). Of 1,783 non-significant p-values in the ground truth, version 0.4.4.3 found 1,719 (3.6% missed), and 94.8% of its 1,814 detections were in the ground truth. How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download files from additional data repositories reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. Full text

Abstract

When people think spatially, they adopt a frame of reference (egocentric or allocentric). People within a community often rely on the same frame of reference, but there is considerable variation between communities. How are these norms of spatial thinking transmitted? Linguistic relativity posits that they are transmitted through language; evidence for this account is mixed. We investigate a complementary mechanism, gestural relativity, whereby norms of spatial thinking are transmitted by gestures that express spatial information. Experiment 1 (N = 62 US Americans) tested whether exposure to a norm favoring a particular frame of reference causes people to adopt that frame of reference in their gestures. Experiment 2 (N = 70) tested whether observing such gestures causes people to adopt that frame of reference in their spatial reasoning. We find evidence for both effects. Gesture can thus express and transmit spatial frames of reference, establishing gesture as a viable mechanism of cultural transmission.

Introduction

Reasoning about space is mostly a private affair. When we plan routes, remember where we left our keys, or imagine the layout of a room, our mental representations are invisible to others. Yet spatial thinking is not entirely idiosyncratic. Within communities, people converge on shared ways of thinking about space. These norms are seldom explicitly taught and yet somehow are maintained and transmitted. How do private habits of spatial thinking become public and shared?

Spatial frames of reference are a canonical example of this puzzle. A computer mouse, for instance, could simultaneously lie to the left and to the north of a keyboard. These descriptions reflect different ways of organizing space in a particular spatial frame of reference (FoR). The first anchors the spatial coordinate system to the speaker's body in an egocentric FoR. The second anchors it to external features of the world in an allocentric FoR. Many communities favor one over the other. Speakers of English and Spanish, for instance, default to egocentric descriptions of small-scale space, while speakers of Tzeltal and Guugu Yimithirr prefer allocentric ones (Haviland, 1993; Brown & Levinson, 1993; Levinson, 2003; Calderón et al., 2019). These preferences manifest not only in language but also in memory and reasoning (Majid et al., 2004). This community-wide convergence on shared preferences for spatial frames of reference is striking, since the underlying cognitive convention is rarely taught explicitly. Some public channel must be conveying this private practice.

One account of these shared spatial norms is linguistic relativity (Whorf, 1956; Levinson, 2003; Majid et al., 2004), the proposal that habitually using a particular frame of reference in language causes community members to preferentially adopt that frame of reference in non-linguistic thought. On this account, spatial descriptions produced or encountered within a community function as cues that nudge speakers and listeners toward thinking spatially in a particular way. Through repeated exposure, these cues stabilize into durable cognitive habits. Evidence for this account comes from cross-cultural correlations between language and non-linguistic thinking: Communities that habitually describe space egocentrically also tend to remember and reason egocentrically, while communities that describe space allocentrically tend to remember and reason allocentrically (Levinson, 1996; Levinson, 2003; Majid et al., 2004).

But linguistic relativity can't be the whole story, since a community's spatial language sometimes diverges from its spatial thought (Le Guen, 2011; Adamou & Shen, 2017; Calderón et al., 2019; Kısa et al., 2026). Yucatec Mayan women in Mexico, for instance, lack knowledge of cardinal direction terms used by the men, yet both women and men adopt an allocentric FoR in non-verbal spatial tasks (Le Guen, 2011). Zapotec-Spanish bilinguals in Mexico will stick to their preferred frame of reference when recalling spatial information regardless of whether they're speaking Spanish, an egocentric language, or Isthmus Zapotec, an allocentric one (Marghetis et al., 2014; Marghetis et al., 2020). And over the past two decades, the Hai||om of Namibia have shifted their spatial preferences in non-linguistic thought toward egocentric encoding, despite their spatial language still relying on an allocentric FoR (Kısa et al., 2026). Linguistic relativity cannot explain these dissociations between spatial language and spatial thought. Communities must have additional channels by which shared spatial conventions are expressed and transmitted.

Here we investigate one such channel: gesture. Gesture has properties that make it a particularly potent vehicle for transmitting a community's norms for adopting a frame of reference in spatial thinking. First, gesture is intrinsically spatial. Gestures are enacted in space and are thus natural vehicles for information about movement and spatial relationships. Indeed, people are more likely to gesture when thinking about and communicating spatial information (Alibali, 2005; Hostetter & Alibali, 2008), and gestures can express fine-grained spatial information not available in speech (Cook & Tanenhaus, 2009). Second, the spatial frame of reference adopted in gesture has been claimed to align with the normative frame of reference in a speaker's community (Haviland, 1993), although so far the evidence for this claim has been qualitative and correlational. Third, gesture has been shown to influence thinking about other aspects of space (e.g., Kita et al., 2017; Alibali, 2005; Zhang et al., 2024; Van Wermeskerken et al., 2016; Kang & Tversky, 2016) and about other conceptual domains such as time (Tversky & Jamalian, 2021; Lewis & Stickles, 2017) and mathematics (Singer & Goldin-Meadow, 2005; Lorson et al., 2025). Fourth, and critically, a few studies have found that the frame of reference adopted in gesture matches the frame of reference adopted for non-communicative spatial thinking, even when the frame of reference adopted in speech is entirely different (e.g., Yucatec Maya women, Le Guen, 2011; Ngigua-Spanish bilinguals, Calderón et al., 2019).

The evidence for a link between gesture and community-level FoR norms, however, has thus far been correlational. We lack evidence of a causal connection between gesture and norms of spatial thought -a problem also faced by much of the evidence for linguistic relativity of spatial frames of reference (Marghetis et al., 2020; Pitt et al., 2022).

If gesture is to function as a channel by which spatial norms are maintained within a community, then two complementary processes must occur. First, speakers' gestures should reliably express local norms for spatial thinking. If members of a community prefer to think about space egocentrically, then this should cause them to gesture more egocentrically; if they prefer to think allocentrically, then this should cause them to gesture more allocentrically. Second, people who observe these gestures should adopt the encoded frame of reference in their own reasoning. If both processes occur, then gesture would be a viable channel by which implicit spatial conventions are made public and transmitted within a community. We refer to this hypothesis as the gestural relativity of spatial cognition.

Here, we test both processes. In Experiment 1, we manipulated the frame of reference that groups used normatively to reason non-linguistically about small-scale space. We then investigated whether people in those groups spontaneously adopted that normative frame of reference in their subsequent gestures. In Experiment 2, we manipulated the frame of reference that a researcher used in their gestures to describe small-scale space. We then investigated whether participants adopted that frame of reference in their own spatial reasoning. Together, these two experiments test the viability of gestural relativity as a mechanism for the cultural transmission of norms of spatial thinking.

Design

In a between-subjects design, we implicitly manipulated the normative frame of reference for spatial reasoning (egocentric vs. allocentric) using a social learning paradigm. Participants completed two rotation-based spatial tasks: a Cup Task and a Map Task. For each task, they first completed a Training phase, followed by a Test phase (Fig. 1). Here we describe the overall design, which was the same for both tasks. In the next section we describe the procedural details for the Cup and Map tasks, which both measure reasoning about small-scale spatial relationships.

The Training phase for each task was designed to transmit the local norm for spatial reasoning through observation and social learning, without explicit references to a particular frame of reference. On each Training trial (n = 4 trials/task), an experimenter (the "Director") demonstrated a spatial movement on a table. This movement was observed by the participant and a second experimenter (the "Player"). All three then rotated 180° to a second, identical table, and the Director asked them to reproduce the motion "the same way" on this second table. After the rotation, egocentric and allocentric strategies yield contrasting responses: an egocentric solution reproduces motion trajectories from the observer's perspective (e.g., a motion to their left), while an allocentric solution preserves them relative to external coordinates, so a motion that was originally observed going from left-to-right should be reproduced going from right-to-left (i.e., maintaining the motion's absolute orientation). On the first Training trial, only the Player attempted to recreate the Director's motion, modeling the normative frame of reference for the participant. On subsequent Training trials, both the participant and the Player attempted to reproduce the Director's motion, in that order. If the participant's response did not reproduce the Director's motion trajectory according to the local norm (i.e., egocentrically or allocentrically), the Director provided generic verbal feedback, saying, "No you moved it differently, [the Player] did it correctly. Make sure you move it the same way." The assigned frame of reference was never named or described explicitly; it was conveyed only through the Player's behavior and the Director's feedback. Researchers never gestured.

The Test phase was designed to investigate whether participants, having learned the local norm, would spontaneously convey that norm in their gestures. On each trial of the Test Phase (n = 4 trials per task), the participant adopted the role of the Director and improvised a motion trajectory. Unlike the Training trials, during the Test trials the Player turned away so they could not see the motion. Participant and Player then rotated to face each other and the participant described the motion trajectory to the Player. The Player then turned 180º to an identical table where they attempted to reproduce the participant's motion based on their description. An occluding barrier was inserted between the tables to ensure that participants received no visual feedback from the Player's motion on the second table. Participants were never encouraged to gesture.

Critically, for each Test trial, we analyzed whether the participant gestured during their description of the motion trajectory and, if so, whether their gestures adopted an egocentric or allocentric FoR. Participants' descriptions were coded online by the Director and also video-recorded for offline reliability coding. During each Test trial, the Director noted whether the participant used their hands or head to convey the motion trajectory, and if so, the primary direction of these gestures. If the primary direction of gestures on a trial was consistent with the participant's egocentric perspective (e.g., a leftward motion was conveyed by a leftward gesture), the gesture for that trial was categorized as egocentric. If the primary direction was consistent with an external frame of reference (e.g., a motion toward the wall was conveyed by a gesture toward the wall), the gesture for that trial was categorized as allocentric. Trials in which the gestures lacked any lateral motion component (n = 11) and trials in which gestures conveyed motion in both lateral directions (n = 4) were coded as neither egocentric nor allocentric. To assess reliability of the online coding, a researcher who was not present for data collection re-coded 20% of participants from video-recordings. Interrater reliability was high (Cohen's kappa: κ = .87).

After completing both tasks, participants answered a series of demographic and individual difference questions (e.g., gender, age, language use, urban/rural environments they grew up in, and ability to judge cardinal directions). See SM for a list of all questions.

Procedures for Cup and Map tasks

While the overall design was identical for the Cup and Map tasks, each task used a different procedure to probe spatial reasoning. Task order was fixed (Cup Task then Map Task). For each task, we used a fixed list of items, with list order counterbalanced across participants (Fig 1). Here we describe the procedural details for each task.

The Cup task involved reproducing the motion of a small ball. Both tables had four cups in a row and a small ball. On a trial, a ball was first moved through the air from one cup to another (e.g., by tracing a zig-zag motion). The task then consisted of trying to reproduce this motion trajectory on a second table. During the Training Phase, the Director used preset trajectories for the ball, with the primary directionleftward or rightward -manipulated between trials (Fig. 1A/1B). During the Test Phase, the participant was free to improvise the ball's trajectory from one of the outermost cups to the other outermost cup.

The Map task involved reproducing an exit route from a maze with multiple possible exit routes. Both tables had identical mazes that were symmetric under 180° rotation. The map on the first table had a prescribed route marked with a blue line. The map on the second table was identical but the prescribed route was unmarked. The task consisted of trying to reproduce the prescribed route on the second table. The routes consisted primarily of motion along the lateral axis, with the primary direction -leftward or rightward -manipulated between trials (Fig. 1C/1D).

Critically, both tasks can be completed in two ways: egocentrically or allocentrically. The egocentric solution involves anchoring spatial relations to the observer's own body. For instance, participants could reproduce a map escape route on the second table using the same sequence of motions from their perspective. The allocentric solution involves anchoring spatial relations to external coordinates. For instance, participants could reproduce a map escape route on the second table using movements anchored to the room's layout (e.g., moving away or toward a salient wall). While neither solution is "wrong" in any objective sense, the Training phase conveyed implicitly that a particular frame of reference was normative in the local context.

Analysis

We analyzed the spatial frame of reference adopted in participants' gestures (egocentric or allocentric) during their Test phase descriptions. We fit a multilevel Bayesian logistic model for each spatial frame of reference (egocentric or allocentric). For each trial on which participants gestured, the model predicted whether they adopted that frame of reference, with fixed effects of the norm condition (allocentric or egocentric, dummy coded), task (Cup or Map, contrast coded), their interaction, and by-participant random intercepts. Models were fit in R using the brms package (Bürkner, 2017) with default priors. There were no divergent transitions and all R-hat values were 1.00, indicating good convergence across 4 MCMC chains. All data and analysis code are available on Research Box: https://researchbox.org/9190 (CODE: ZCWIQE)

Results

Participants adopted a consistent frame of reference in their spontaneous gestures Participants gestured often. Across 496 total trials (62 participants × 8 trials/participant), participants almost always gestured while describing their solutions (86% of all trials). All subsequent analyses focus on trials that were accompanied by gesture. On trials accompanied by gesture, participants' gestures were nearly always consistent with either an egocentric or an allocentric FoR (96.5% of trials; Fig. 2). See Figure 2 for examples of egocentric or allocentric gestures. Consistent with known egocentric biases among US American speakers of English (Li & Gleitman, 2002; Levinson, 2003), there was an overall tendency to gesture more egocentrically (average across participants: M = 63% of trials, SEM = 4%), more pronounced on the Map task (M = 80%, SEM = 4%) than the Cup task (M = 49%, SEM = 4%).

We next investigated whether participants adopted a consistent frame of reference in their gestures while describing motion. We identified each participant's predominant frame of reference as the one they adopted on the most trials. Participants were internally consistent in their adoption of a particular frame of reference. On trials where participants encoded a frame of reference in gesture, they adopted their predominant frame of reference 77% of the time, on average. This consistency is much higher than expected by chance: We simulated the experiment (N = 10,000 replicates), with 62 participants randomly selecting an egocentric or allocentric FoR on each of eight trials; the mean internal-consistency in FoR was greater in the empirically-observed sample than in every single simulated run.

The local norm for spatial reasoning shaped the frame of reference adopted in gesture

We next addressed our main question: whether the local norm for spatial thinking shaped how participants gestured spontaneously while describing motion events. As predicted, participants' gestures were shaped systematically by the local norm for spatial thinking that was modeled during training (Fig. 2). Participants exposed to an egocentric norm were more likely to gesture about spatial relationships egocentrically (proportion of trials with gesture: M = .73 ± .04 SEM) than allocentrically (M = .26 ± .04 SEM). Participants exposed to an allocentric norm, by comparison, produced fewer egocentric gestures (M = .52 ± .05 SEM) and more allocentric gestures (M = .40 ± .05 SEM) than those in the egocentric condition, although they still exhibited an overall egocentric bias. Brief exposure to a local norm for spatial thinking, therefore, shaped how participants encoded motion events in their spontaneous gestures.

This effect of the local spatial norm was confirmed by multilevel Bayesian logistic models of the frame of reference adopted in gesture. We fit two models, one of whether participants gestured egocentrically on each trial, and one of whether they gestured allocentrically (see Methods). Compared to participants exposed to the allocentric norm, participants exposed to the egocentric norm were reliably more likely to gesture egocentrically (b = 1.14, 95% Bayesian Credible Interval = [0.27, 2.07]) but less likely to gesture allocentrically (b = -0.93, 95% BCI = [-1.84, -0.12]). These effects were consistent across both tasks (Condition x Task in the model of egocentric responses: b = -0.06, 95% BCI = [-1.12, 1.00]; model of allocentric responses: b = -0.45, 95% BCI = [-1.51, 0.60]). Experiment 2: Gesture shapes observers' spatial frames of reference Methods

Participants

Students at an American research university participated in exchange for partial course credit (N = 74; 45 female, 29 male; M age = 19 years, range [18, 29]). A minimum sample size of 60 was chosen by doubling the sample size of work using rotation-based measures of spatial FoR (e.g., Li & Gleitman, 2002; Levinson et al., 2002; Haun et al., 2006; Kısa et al., 2026), with data collection continuing until the end of the academic semester in which we reached the minimum sample size. All participants were proficient in English. The majority reported English as their primary language (n = 65); most of the remainder reported Spanish (n = 6). Nearly a third of participants reported some proficiency in Spanish (30%). Four participants were excluded prior to analysis, three due to prior knowledge of the study and one due to experimental error, leaving a final sample of 70 analyzed participants.

Design

Participants completed a rotation-based "hide and seek" spatial search task, adapted from Haun et al. (2006) and Pitt et al. (2022). Each trial consisted of a Hide phase, a Tell phase, and a Seek phase (Fig. 3). In the Hide phase, the participant watched an experimenter hide a ball on the Hide table. Next, in the Tell phase, the experimenter described the ball's location to the participant. Finally, in the Seek phase, the experimenter instructed the participant to find a ball hidden "in the same location" on the Seek table, and the participant chose one location to search.

Critically, the experimenter's description of the ball's location during the Tell phase sometimes included co-speech gestures. The first trials were Gesture trials (n = 8), during which the experimenter's descriptions included co-speech gestures that adopted a specific frame of reference (Gestural FoR condition: Egocentric vs. Allocentric) to convey the ball's location. This gestural frame of reference was manipulated between subjects and counterbalanced; speech was scripted and identical between conditions. If gestures can shape the frame of reference adopted by an observer, then participants should search more often under the cup that is consistent with the frame of reference adopted in the experimenter's gestural description. On subsequent No Gesture trials (n = 4), the researcher's descriptions were identical except they did not include gesture. No-Gesture trials assessed whether the effect of the gestural manipulation persisted in the absence of ongoing gestural cues.

Procedure

Before the start of each trial, a divider was placed to hide the Seek table, and the experimenter set up the Seek table by hiding two balls, one under the cup corresponding to an egocentric search strategy, the other under the cup corresponding to an allocentric search strategy.

Each trial then began with the Hide phase, in which the participant watched the experimenter hide a ball under one of four identical cups arranged in a row on the Hide table (Fig. 3, left). The experimenter hid the ball under each cup an equal number of times, randomized within each trial block (n = 4).

The experimenter then turned 180º to face the participant for the Tell phase. The experimenter described the ball's location (Fig. 3, middle) by stating, "On the table behind me, there are four cups. And under this cup, there is a prize." On Gesture trials, the experimenter accompanied their speech with a series of gestures that represented the ball's location according to a particular frame of reference, either egocentric or allocentric, and we manipulated this gestural FoR between-subjects. While saying "there are four cups," the experimenter produced a sequence of iconic gestures (hands cupped downward) that depicted the row of four cups. They accompanied the phrase "this cup" with a gesture that represented the ball's location using either an egocentric or an allocentric FoR. In the Egocentric condition, the experimenter's gestures represented the ball's location relative to their body (e.g., if they hid the ball to their left, after rotating 180º they gestured to their left). In the Allocentric condition, the experimenter's gestures represented the ball's location relative to external coordinates (e.g., if they hid the ball to their left, after rotating 180º they gestured to their right).

Finally, in the Seek phase, the divider was removed to reveal the Seek table behind the participant, and the participant rotated 180° to search for a ball on this table by lifting one of four cups (Fig. 3, right). The Seek table was identical to the Hide table. Recall that, before the start of each trial, while the Seek table was hidden from view behind a divider, an experimenter hid balls under the two cups on the Seek table that were consistent with an egocentric or an allocentric response. An egocentric response consisted of searching under the cup in the same position from the searcher's perspective (e.g., if the experimenter hid the ball under the rightmost cup, then the participant would search under the rightmost cup). An allocentric response consisted of searching under the cup in the same position from an external perspective (e.g., if the experimenter hid the ball in the cup closest to the wall, then the participant would search under the cup closest to the wall). While the participant believed there was only one hidden ball, by actually hiding two balls we allowed the participant to succeed with either an allocentric or an egocentric response. Once the participant lifted the cup of their choice, either revealing a ball or nothing, the trial ended.

Each session began with Gesture trials (n = 8), followed by No-Gesture trials (n = 4). No-Gesture trials were procedurally identical to Gesture trials except the experimenter never gestured, and the spoken description changed from "under this cup" to "under a cup."

Analysis

We analyzed the trial-by-trial search strategy (egocentric vs. not) as our primary dependent variable. A multilevel Bayesian logistic model predicted trial-by-trial search strategy (egocentric vs. not), with fixed effects of Gestural FoR (Egocentric vs. Allocentric), Trial Type (Gesture vs. No-Gesture), and their interaction, and random intercepts for participants and items. In a second Bayesian logistic model, we re-baselined Trial Type on the No Gesture trials where the experimenter did not gesture (trials 9-12). Models were fit in R using the brms package (Bürkner, 2017) with default uninformative priors. There were no divergent transitions and all R-hat values were 1.00, indicating good convergence across 4 MCMC chains. Results

Systematic and internally consistent spatial frames of reference

Since there were four cups under which they could search, but only two corresponded to egocentric or allocentric solutions for any given trial, random responding would only land on the allocentric or egocentric target locations half the time. Instead, participants searched in either the allocentric or the egocentric location on 99% of trials (n = 829 of 840 trials). Overall, participants were slightly more likely to adopt an egocentric FoR (M = .54 ± .06 SEM) than an allocentric FoR (M = .44, ± .05 SEM), consistent with an egocentric bias in this American population.

Participants were also internally consistent, maintaining the same search strategy across trials: 87% of participants used their preferred spatial frame on 11 or 12 of the total 12 trials.

Observing gesture shaped participants' spatial frame of reference Critically, the experimenter's gestural frame of reference systematically influenced participants' search behavior (Fig. 4). When the experimenter's gesture encoded the target location egocentrically, participants predominantly searched in the egocentric location (proportion of egocentric responses: M = .69 ± .07 SEM). When the gesture encoded the location allocentrically, participants predominantly searched in the allocentric location (proportion of allocentric responses: M = .58 ± .08 SEM). The effect was confirmed by a multilevel Bayesian logistic model: being in the Egocentric condition substantially increased the probability of an egocentric search response (effect of Gestural FoR on the Gesture trials: b = 8.53, 95% BCI = [2.63, 16.18]).

There was even evidence that the effect of the gestural FoR persisted after the experimenter stopped gesturing (Fig. 5). On the final four No-Gesture trials, participants in the Egocentric condition continued to prefer the egocentric location (proportion of egocentric responses: M = .65 ± .08 SEM), while participants in the Allocentric condition continued to prefer the allocentric location (proportion of allocentric responses: M = .56 ± .08 SEM). When we rebaselined the Bayesian mixed effects model on the No-Gesture trials, the effect of the experimenter's gestural FoR remained positive, with positive estimates of the gesture effect that were positive but more uncertain (effect of Gestural FoR on the No-Gesture trials: b = 5.14, 95% BCI = [-0.66, 12.47]), consistent with an attenuated effect of gesture. This attenuated effect after gesture stopped was confirmed by the interaction between Gestural FoR and Trial Type, with the effect of the experimenter's gestural FoR greater on Gesture trials (b = 3.40, 95% BCI = [1.64, 5.43]). Discussion

Two experiments tested whether gestural relativity is a viable mechanism for propagating and perpetuating spatial frames of reference. Experiment 1 demonstrated that the frame of reference participants adopted in the privacy of their minds surfaced publicly in their gestures. Experiment 2 investigated whether those public traces were causally consequential for observers' thinking, and found that spatial gestures shape the frame of reference that they use to reason spatially. Gesture is thus a channel through which spatial frames of reference can be expressed and transmitted.

These results complicate the relation between spatial language, gesture, and thought. Linguistic relativity holds that habitual language use shapes habitual thought (Whorf, 1956), although evidence is mixed for the case of spatial frames of reference (e.g., Le Guen, 2011; Adamou & Shen, 2017; Calderón et al., 2019; Kısa et al., 2026). Our results suggest that gesture may be a complementary mechanism. Just as repeated exposure to frames of reference in spatial language may reinforce those frames of reference in non-linguistic spatial cognition, so too could repeated exposure to spatial gesture. Gesture may thus function as a "spotlight" (Wolff & Holmes, 2011), repeatedly drawing attention to certain spatial relationships. Over time, repeated exposure to a particular spatial frame of reference in gesture could instill a habitual bias, thus privileging that frame of reference in non-communicative thinking (cf. Amir & Pitt, 2026). Future work should investigate the long-term effects of habitual exposure to spatial gesture.

Recall from the Introduction that there are communities in which the habitual frame of reference in speech diverges from the habitual frame of reference in thought (Le Guen, 2011; Marghetis et al., 2020; Calderón et al., 2019). Linguistic relativity cannot explain the existence of norms of spatial thinking in these communities. These divergences between language and thought make sense, however, if norms for spatial thinking are also products of gestural relativity (Le Guen, 2011; Calderón et al., 2019). Yucatec Mayan women, for instance, think allocentrically even though they lack competence with spoken allocentric terms (Le Guen, 2011 ). Le Guen (2011) speculates that this community may rely more on gesture for communicating spatial information because they often need to accurately convey allocentric cardinal directions, and it is easier to convey precise information about angles in gesture than in speech. This suggests one scenario in which gestural relativity may play an outsized role in transmitting community norms of spatial thinking: When a community values precision in allocentric orientation, norms of spatial thinking may be conveyed more through gesture than through speech.

The ecosystem of spatial thinking

Gesture is thus one possible mechanism by which a community maintains shared norms of spatial thinking, but it is unlikely to be the only one. For example, some built environments may favor egocentric thinking (Mishra, Dasen, & Niraula, 2003; Majid et al., 2004; Dasen & Mishra, 2010). A study of Tamil speakers in India found that those living in a city favored an egocentric FoR in their thinking while those in rural areas favored an allocentric FoR (Pederson, 1995), and language communities in urban environments are statistically more likely to reason egocentrically (Marghetis, Ortega, & Holmes, 2024). Other aspects of material culture may also play a role in regimenting a community's spatial thinking. The Hai||om of Namibia, for instance, have shifted toward egocentric spatial thinking while their spatial language has remained predominantly allocentric, and this shift in spatial thinking has been hypothesized to reflect exposure to material artifacts such as books, maps, and digital screens (Kısa et al., 2026).

Community norms of spatial thinking, therefore, are unlikely to reflect a single, isolated cause. This suggests the need for an ecological perspective that reflects the multicausality of spatial cognition (Marghetis et al., 2020; Palmer et al., 2017; cf., Hutchins, 2010). An ecological perspective emphasizes how the spatial reasoner is embedded in a broader ecosystem of mutually-constraining practices and resources. Norms of private spatial thinking may reflect the confluence -and sometimes the conflictof all these factors, including language (Majid et al., 2004; Levinson, 2003; Whorf, 1956), gesture (Le Guen, 2011; Calderón et al., 2019), material artifacts (Kısa et al., 2026), the larger built environment (Marghetis et al., 2024), the natural landscape (Palmer et al., 2017), limitations of visual perception (Pitt et al., 2022), the pragmatics of the task itself (Li & Gleitman, 2002), and innate biases (Haun et al., 2006). None of these operate in isolation. Some may have an outsized role in establishing and sustaining spatial habits -"keystone species" in the ecosystem of spatial thought -and the relative influence of any particular factor may vary between communities. We speculate that gesture may often occupy a privileged position within this ecosystem because of its intrinsic spatiality and ubiquity in face-to-face interaction, but the full ecology of influences on spatial cognition remains to be characterized.

Conclusion

How are private conventions for thinking about space made public and thus shared? We have shown that gesture can be part of the answer. When speakers internalized a local norm for spatial reasoning, they expressed it spontaneously in their gestures; when observers encountered such gestures, they adopted the encoded frame of reference in their own spatial reasoning. Taken together, these two findings demonstrate the viability of gesture as a mechanism for transmitting community norms of spatial thinking. Repeated, habitual exposure to frame-of-reference-encoding gestures may transmit and sustain shared spatial preferences within communities. This suggests that some cross-cultural variation in spatial thinking could be driven by gestural relativity, rather than linguistic relativity, a possibility that should be explored in future field research. More generally, gestural relativity may be an underappreciated channel of cultural transmission, with cultural norms shaping how people gesture within the community, and exposure to these gestures then biasing observers toward those community norms.
