Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard), also Internal Review Board - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term); not IEC followed by a number, which is a technical standard - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee/Commission, in the singular or plural - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee, Bioethics Committee/Commission, Ethikkommission - Ethical Review Authority/Unit/Office/Agency - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Human Research Protection Office/Program, Office for Human Subjects Protection - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee(s) on/for (Medical and Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt - Ethics review that was not needed: Ethics review was not required, did not require further ethical review, exempt from formal human research ethics review, no new institutional ethics review

A template line the authors have not filled in (“[Add ethics approval … here.]”) is not a statement.

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

When a statement is expected

The module sorts each paper by where its data came from (the internal .data_source(); the signs and how the rule was tested are described in R/data-source.R): - own: the authors collected data from people or animals, for this paper, in an earlier study of their own, or (a protocol) will collect them; - existing_records: records about individual people that the authors obtained for this study, such as hospital records or a cohort; - existing_unclear: existing data about people from a source the module could not identify; - open_resource: an open resource any researcher can use, such as UK Biobank or NHANES; - none: no data about people or animals (a review, simulation, commentary, theory, tutorial, coding of published papers, or figures about groups); - uncertain: the signs were missing or pointed both ways; - no_text: the paper has no text.

A statement is expected for own, existing_records and existing_unclear. A paper without a statement in these classes, or in uncertain, is flagged. The module reads the text, and the results of power, stat_p_exact, stat_check, repo_check, data_check and prereg_check when they ran before it (run it after them); without stat_p_exact, it finds the p-values in the text.

On 199 PsyArXiv and medRxiv preprints labelled after the rule was fixed, the module decided right whether a statement is needed for 86.9% (the earlier live-data search: 83.4%). Its main errors: other people’s studies described in the words of a new study, records and specimens described only as counts, and open resources the paper does not call open.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

Overall, among all instances flagged as power statements, 90.6% were correct (positive predictive value).

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download information from other preregistration sites, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How Marginal Significance works

The marginal module searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The list of terms is a subset of those listed in a blog post by Matthew Hankins. The module returns all sentences that match terms describing ‘marginally significant’ results.

Some of the terms identified might not be problematic in some contexts, and there are ways to describe ‘marginal significance’ that are not detected by the module.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 685 tests with 34 instances of inconsistent reporting, Statcheck correctly detected 34 of them, and incorrectly identified 26. Therefore 0% of true instances were missed, and 43% of detections were false positives. See https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/ for more details of the validation.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on 226 Psychological Science papers with 5,019 hand-coded p-values, 438 of them imprecise (scienceverse/validation, branch stat_p_exact). Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). Of the misses, 13 are starred table notes (“*p < .05”) that the module leaves out on purpose and 11 are p-values it did not find; of the wrong reports, 20 are p-values the ground truth does not contain (such as “ps < .05”) and 6 use ≤ or ≥. These figures measure agreement with the hand-coding, which has known errors in both directions (scienceverse/validation#48).

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on three hand-coded sets of papers (scienceverse/validation: 249 Psychological Science, 250 Economics and 250 MDPI papers). Of 1,783 non-significant p-values in the ground truth, version 0.4.4.3 found 1,719 (3.6% missed), and 94.8% of its 1,814 detections were in the ground truth. Some differences come from the ground truth: it lacks p-values with a correction name (“p Bonf = .061”), and counts partial eta squared written with its subscript after it (“η 2 p = .05”), “p = .05”, and some table notes and figure legends (“† p < .10, *p < .05”), which this module leaves out, as non-significant p-values. How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Introduction

Student protest movements represent important contexts for understanding how collective action shapes individual and social experience and the development of young people. Beyond their political objectives, such movements can transform participants' identities and social relationships, as well as profoundly influence everyday routines and psychological well-being. The student blockades that emerged in Serbia during the 2024/25 academic year provide a valuable context for examining these processes. The present study explores how participants made sense of these experiences, focusing on the intersections between collective engagement, personal development, identity transformation, social reconfiguration, and mental health.

The 2024/25 Student Protests in Serbia

The 2024/25 student protests in Serbia were triggered by the collapse of a canopy at the newly reconstructed railway station in Novi Sad, which resulted in the deaths of sixteen people.

Students and other civic actors interpreted the tragedy as the result of and evidence of systemic corruption problems (Beširević, 2025).

The first form of collective action of students was organizing and initiating group vigils in the street, at crossroads, in front of faculties, at the same hour that the canopy collapsed. Following physical attacks on students participating in these gatherings, faculty blockades began in late November 2024 and quickly spread across universities throughout Serbia (Beširević, 2025; Stevanović, 2024). Students occupied faculty and university buildings and sustained the movement for nine months. Social media and digital networks facilitated communication and coordination across universities (Enikolopov et al., 2020; Jost et al., 2018; Lenhart, 2013).

The relationship between students and faculty staff was one of the substantial changes during this period. Professors and the governing bodies of many faculties and several universities formally and actively supported the student blockades, reversing the previous structure of academic authority. Supporting professors took on a helping role when needed, while decisionmaking power and authority rested with the students. On the other hand, they had the opportunity to see them powerless and without a response, which sometimes made students feel as if they were losing the stability pillar.

Organizationally, the movement relied on plenums, open assemblies grounded in direct democracy taking place every day during the blockades, consensus decision-making, and the rejection and absence of formal and informal leadership and leaders (Beširević, 2025; Spasojević et al., 2025). The movement also distanced itself from any political parties or organizations.

Students intentionally avoided individual representation, appearing instead as "students of a faculty," thereby foregrounding collective identity over personal visibility.

The movement actions included sustained blockades, protests, and symbolic cross-country marches. It also included international efforts, such as bicycle rides to Brussels to raise awareness of the crisis.

The movement received broad support from diverse social groups, including professors, workers, farmers, lawyers, artists, and pensioners (Beširević, 2025; Subotić, 2025b). The support had various forms: petitions and public statements, participation in protests, donations of money and food, transport to protests, accommodation on marches, and free legal and psychological support for those arrested or abused. Subotić (2025a) describes the movement as 'catch-all', bridging social divides that previous protest cycles in Serbia had failed to span. State responses included violence by police and parapolice groups, including physical, emotional, and sexual violence, procedural violations, arrests, and detainments (Jakovljević & Pajović, 2026). Students were also subjected to continuous defamatory campaigns in government-aligned media.

The data in this research were collected during the period when the student movement was at its peak and functioned in the previously described manner. At the end of the summer of 2025, the movement changed form, as it was necessary for the faculties to begin working and to compensate for missed academic obligations. At the stage of drafting this text, the blockades of faculties are not active and the movement shifted toward direct political engagement as the government showed no substantive intention to meet the students' demands (Subotić, 2025a (Subotić, , 2025b)). Serbian universities continue to serve as arenas for political deliberation and critical consciousness, echoing Habermas's (1989) conception of the public sphere and Gill and DeFronzo's (2009) analysis of campuses as incubators of protest.

Young Adults and the Emergence of Political Subjectivity

Emerging adulthood (roughly 18-25) is a developmental period characterized by identity exploration and heightened openness to social influences (Arnett, 2000; Hutchison et al., 2016).

Historical context and political upheavals can act as catalysts for personal development and civic engagement (Committee on Improving the Health, Safety, and Well-Being of Young Adults, 2015; Wood et al., 2018).

Within this developmental window, universities and students occupy a distinctive position.

The transition into higher education has a politicising effect, fostering independence, critical thinking, tolerance, and a belief in one's capacity to contribute to change (Brooks et al., 2020; Loader et al., 2015; Ustyuzhanin et al., 2022; Welzel, 2013). Universities function as important sites of social mobilization, because they concentrate available time, dense peer networks, and communication resources (Gready & Jackson, 2025; Loader et al., 2014). In the contemporary context, students operate through formal channels and institutionalized organizations, such as student parliaments and unions, but also through informal ones, in which protests can serve as spaces for students to express dissatisfaction and demonstrate their social role (Proteasa & Andreescu, 2019).

The process of collective meaning-making is consistent with theories of politicization, whereby perceived injustice and illegitimacy activate a transition from personal concern to collective action (Simon & Klandermans, 2001). Peer networks facilitate the transition from political attitudes to collective action, helping youth overcome practical barriers to attendance within a movement's mobilization potential (Klandermans and Oegema, 1987). Once engaged, sustained participation fosters political subjectivity by strengthening civic identity and developing a sense of oneself as a capable political actor. Consequently, activism simultaneously reflects and shapes identity during political socialization (Behrens, 2024; Wray-Lake, 2019). This process involves a reorganization of the self in relation to the social world, an emerging sense that one is the kind of person who notices injustice, who acts, and who carries responsibility for collective outcomes.

Emotions and social identity are central for youth activism as belonging functions as both a motivation for and an outcome of participation, particularly for young people still consolidating their identities during the transition to adulthood (Behrens, 2024). Furthermore, activism generates a sense of personal significance, which sustains commitment beyond the initial grievance (Jasko et al., 2019). This involves a reorganization of the self in relation to the social world, an emerging sense that one is the kind of person who notices injustice, who acts, and who carries responsibility for collective outcomes. Research confirms that participation in historically significant protest movements can produce enduring changes in political identity (Vestergren et al., 2017).

Collective Identity and Social Reconfiguration in Protest

Collective action has been extensively explained through social identity approaches, which emphasize that group membership becomes salient in shaping perceptions and actions (Tajfel & Turner, 1979; Turner et al., 1987). The Social Identity Model of Collective Action (SIMCA) integrates this perspective with perceived injustice and group efficacy as key predictors of participation (van Zomeren et al., 2008).

These constructs sit within a broader social-psychology-of-protest framework that van Stekelenburg and Klandermans (2013 Klandermans ( , 2017) ) consolidate around five pillars: grievances, efficacy, identification, emotions, and social embeddedness. Dual-pathway models combine these elements into instrumental routes (driven by efficacy and instrumental cost-benefit appraisals) and identityexpressive routes (driven by politicised identity and moral indignation) with protests and participants varying in the relative dominance of each pathway (van Stekelenburg & Klandermans, 2013). A complementary quantitative study of the same movement suggested that identification with protesting students and perceived group efficacy played an important role in sustained engagement (Branković et al., 2026).

Protest participation deepens these bonds through repeated, emotionally saturated encounters. The social identity model of crowd behaviour suggests that shared group identification fosters alignment with collective values, coordinated action, and empowerment (da Costa et al., 2023; Drury & Reicher, 2000). Sharing similar emotions and values fosters social identification within activist groups and generates a sense of belonging, a process particularly consequential for young people navigating the transition to adulthood (Behrens, 2024). Recent work distinguishes between group identification and identity fusion: both can predict sustained collective action, with fusion reflecting a deeper integration of personal and social identity (Wibisono et al., 2022).

Collective identity is also shaped through boundary processes that define in-groups and out-groups and by position movement in opposition to dominant cultural practices (Hunt et al., 1994; Taylor & Whittier, 1992). Solidarity is also actively rewarded from within, with participants who contribute more to the movement receiving higher in-group status, which motivates further involvement (Bélanger et al., 2018; Willer, 2009). Existing literature on student-led forms of collective action repeatedly shows that participation in such movements is not driven by individual cost-benefit calculations but rather the nature and quality of intergroup relations (da Costa et al., 2023; Hirsch, 1990). Prior research indicates that these kinds of identification processes are central to both mobilization and its enduring psychological consequences (Klandermans, 2004; Vestergren et al., 2017).

The Emotional Architecture of Collective Action

Emotions are central to protest participation and operate throughout all phases of social movements: shaping motivation, enabling and disrupting collective identity, informing strategic decisions, and determining the trajectory of sustained engagement (Jasper, 2011). Rather than opposing rationality, emotions and cognition are deeply interwoven processes through which participants interpret injustice, maintain commitment, and navigate collective action.

Within this framework, moral shocks occupy a distinctive place. Jasper (2006) describes moral shocks as sudden experiences of injustice or norm violation that trigger acute emotional reactions and draw previously uninvolved individuals into collective action. The collapse of the Novi Sad station canopy in November 2024, followed by institutional misinformation and the deflection of accountability, constituted precisely this kind of shock, transforming private grief into collective indignation and organized political action.

Once engagement begins, protest generates its own dynamics. Shared emotional experiences of unity and synchrony predict stronger group identification, collective efficacy, positive affect, and social integration (Pizarro et al., 2022). Collective emotions, therefore, strengthen attachment to the group and foster empowerment and moral elevation (Collins, 2004; Da Costa et al., 2023; Durkheim, 1912/95).

At the same time, perceived emotional fit, the sense that one's emotional experiences are shared among fellow activists, protects against burnout and stress (Vandermeulen et al., 2023). In the Serbian protests, the shared experience of fear, anger, pride, and hope may have provided precisely this kind of affective scaffolding.

The Dual Consequences for Psychological Wellbeing

Research on mental health consequences of protest participation consistently finds mixed, pathway-dependent outcomes. Even nonviolent collective action can be associated with elevated symptoms of anxiety, depression, and post-traumatic stress, while simultaneously reducing isolation and strengthening community bonds (Ni et al., 2020). Even though the research shows both positive and negative effects within the same collective action, the direction of the effect depends on context, state response, duration, social support, and perceived efficacy. Ballard and Ozer (2016) systematize this complexity in a five-pathway model through which activism influences wellbeing: via stress and coping processes, via empowerment, via identity and purpose, via social connection, and via perceived systemic change. Each pathway can function as either protective or risk-enhancing, depending on contextual conditions. The model provides a dynamic account of how the same engagement, in the same movement, at the same moment, can simultaneously generate psychological resources through one pathway while producing strain through another. Within this framework, coping is the key mediating mechanism that determines whether activism leads to empowerment or strain, thereby shaping interpretations of political demands and long-term well-being (Ballard & Ozer, 2016; Klandermans, 2014).

Adaptive coping strategies operate through social support, meaning-making, and emotion regulation, reducing stress and supporting sustained involvement despite ongoing challenges.

Regulatory flexibility may further support resilience in unpredictable, phase-shifting protest contexts (Bonanno & Burton, 2013). Certain coping patterns, however, intensify strain: overcommitment, self-sacrificial norms, emotional suppression, and dependence on group validation for personal worth amplify the moral pressure in sustained activism. These patterns reflect collective norms rather than individual failures (Ballard & Ozer, 2016).

The negative strain pathway conceptualizes activism as a chronic rather than episodic stress context. Psychological burden arises less from single events than from accumulated demands over time: ongoing uncertainty about outcomes, continuous moral responsibility for collective goals, horizontal organizational pressures, and lack of clear endpoints for disengagement. Under these conditions, activism becomes an all-encompassing life frame that crowds out restoration and recovery. Activist burnout, defined as emotional exhaustion, cynicism, and a diminished sense of accomplishment due to chronic, unmanaged stress, represents the endpoint of this pathway. Chen and Gorski (2015) identified a culture of selflessness and martyrdom in which self-care is frequently framed as self-indulgence and movement norms become a source of strain.

Existing qualitative data on student activism documents simultaneous strain and meaning creation (Conner et al., 2023). While many student activists reported chronic stress, emotional exhaustion, and burnout, others described activism as psychologically sustaining, particularly when supported. Activists in Conner et al.'s (2023) study described persistent pressure to remain constantly available, emotionally resilient, and productive as a self-imposed moral imperative that made rest feel like abandonment and doubt like betrayal. This pattern of activist guilt (feeling of never doing enough and falling short of collective obligation) intensified psychological strain.

Research found that participation was associated with heightened negative emotional reactivity, reduced sensitivity to positive events, and greater emotional instability, patterns that predicted later depressive symptoms and reduced well-being at three-month follow-up (Hou & Bonanno, 2018).

These findings underline the importance of framing self-care as a resource rather than a retreat from the movement, as a way to overcome the moral resistance to rest in activist cultures (Gorski, 2015).

The Present Study

Despite the growing body of research on youth political engagement, several gaps remain that the present study seeks to address. Existing work has predominantly relied on quantitative, cross-sectional designs in established Western democracies (Kappus, 2023), leaving the lived experience and meaning-making of sustained collective action largely underexamined.

Qualitative, phenomenologically grounded accounts are especially scarce in post-socialist, semiauthoritarian contexts, where political repression compounds the psychological and relational demands placed on participants, and the psychosocial consequences of activism remain underexplored (Kwan, 2023). Furthermore, the ongoing Serbian student movement constitutes a distinctive case: sustained for more than six months through continuous faculty blockades, they are unusually prolonged compared with most student mobilizations documented in the literature. The movement is morally rather than ideologically (left-right) framed, structurally leaderless, nonpartisan, and unfolding under active government pressure and repression. It has also achieved exceptional reach and size, with blockades and protests occurring simultaneously across all state universities in Serbia. Broad societal support from citizens and academic staff has further contributed to its persistence and legitimacy. Together, these features make the movement a specific and valuable context for exploring the social and psychological consequences of sustained civic engagement.

The present study examines how students actively involved in the 2024/25 blockades in Serbia constructed meaning around their participation and its psychological and social implications. Adopting a Reflexive Thematic Analysis approach (Braun & Clarke, 2006 , 2019 , 2021), the study explores shared and divergent meanings in participants' accounts, recognizing their embeddedness in broader social, political, and cultural contexts. Particular attention was devoted to understanding how prolonged involvement in collective action transformed students' everyday worlds, social relationships, identities, and well-being. Specifically, the study aimed to explore how participation in the blockades: (a) reconfigured participants' lived experience (everyday routines, sense of self, political subjectivity, and future orientations); (b) reshaped social relationships, processes of belonging, and collective identity; and (c) shaped experiences of psychological demands and coping mechanisms in sustained activism. The study contributes to a nuanced understanding of student activism as transformative, relational, and psychologically consequential.

Method

Participants

The inclusion criteria were being undergraduate or graduate students and self-identifying as having an active role in the ongoing blockades. The sample was convenient and collected via a snowball method. A total of 20 students (11 women, M AGE = 22.15, SD AGE = 1.69) participated in the interviews. The sample size was considered sufficient to generate rich and diverse accounts of participation in the blockades and to analyze in depth how students made sense of their own participation.

Participants were mainly students at the University of Belgrade; two were from the University of Arts in Belgrade, one from the University of Novi Sad, and two studied at private universities in Belgrade. The interviewed students mainly studied social sciences (50%), 40% majored in natural sciences, and 10% studied the arts. A majority (85%) of the interviewed students were pursuing their bachelor's degrees, and three were pursuing their master's degrees. The sampled students were of relatively uniform socio-economic status (upper-middle class) who either still lived with their parents (70%) or, if they did not study in their hometown, in apartments they owned or rented (30%). None of our participants lived in student dorms.

Data collection

Data were collected through semi-structured interviews. Each of the first five authors conducted four interview sessions. Interviews were conducted online via the Google Meet platform (95%) or in person at the Faculty of Philosophy, University of Belgrade, and lasted 60-90 minutes.

The semi-structured interview guide covered the following topics: General information and sociodemographic data. Participants provided basic sociodemographic information and described their form and level of engagement in the ongoing blockades. Life before and during the blockades. Participants compared their daily routines, social lives, and sense of identity before and during the blockades, including their engagement with socially important issues. Family relations. Participants discussed their family's attitudes toward the blockades and their support for our participants' engagement, including how involvement affected family dynamics and relations: studies and education. Participants were asked about changes in their sense of student identity and academic satisfaction, as well as their fears and concerns regarding their academic future. The blockades and mental health. This section focused on stressful experiences during the blockades, participants' perceived mental health, and the extent to which media consumption contributed to emotional strain. Social support. In this section, participants described their main sources of social support during the blockades and reflected on how their peer relationships had changed over this period. Life after the blockades -future expectations. Participants shared their hopes and fears for the future, as well as their expectations about the effects of the blockades on their personal lives and on society as a whole.

Data analysis

The data analysis was based on reflexive thematic analysis (Braun & Clarke, 2019 , 2022).

Themes were understood as constructed through the researchers' active engagement with the data, rather than as categories that emerged independently from it. The coding scheme was constructed inductively, and the analysis was performed manually using MAXQDA 2022 software (VERBI Software, 2022). Codes were extracted from each interview separately, then interpreted in the broader context of all interviews and iteratively revised. After the coding process was completed, all codes were jointly discussed, considered in their entirety, and integrated into themes through a recursive, collaborative process of reflection and interpretation. The research team shared the same socio-political context as the participants, and support for the movement was seen as an analytic resource rather than a source of bias to eliminate. Reflexive notes and discussions were used to create interpretative conclusions. During analysis, we also relied on phenomenological principles, closely observing how participants described their lived experiences and the meanings they provided (Smith & Fieldsend, 2021; Willig, 2013).

Ethical considerations

This research was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Department of Psychology at the University of Belgrade before data collection. Before participation, all participants provided informed consent to participate and to audio recording. We practised continuous consent throughout the interview due to the potentially sensitive nature of some topics. Anonymity was guaranteed by using pseudonyms in both the interview transcript and all research reports. The audio recordings were deleted after the transcription was completed.

Results

The analysis identified three superordinate themes that capture students' experiences of participation in blockades. The first theme, The Blockades as a Transformative Lived Space, reflects changes in everyday life and personal development, as well as in how students came to see themselves as politically and civically engaged actors. The second theme, Reconfiguration of Social Relations through Collective Engagement, examines the formation of collective identity and moral commitment, and the negotiation of group boundaries within the student movement.

Finally, the third theme, Emotional Strain and Relational Buffering in the Collective Engagement, captures experiences of anxiety, uncertainty, burnout, and perceived threat in the collective engagement, as well as the resources that helped participants to manage their emotional distress and sustain long-term engagement in the blockades.

Before illustrating the themes, we will outline the context of students' everyday lives during the blockades. The faculty blockades fundamentally disrupted the structure of students' academic lives: classes were suspended, fixed schedules dissolved, and the inherent uncertainty about the protest duration further unsettled their routines. Nonetheless, the occupied faculties became a new kind of shared lived space where students spent their days alongside peers they would not otherwise encounter. These changes made students free from standard academic obligations, creating unscheduled time.

The concentration of student life around the occupied faculties also restructured their everyday social patterns: daily co-presence brought students into sustained contact with peers from different departments and year groups, extending beyond prior academic acquaintance and becoming more central than previously dominant friendship and peer groups. These changed conditions form the backdrop against which the experiences described in the following sub-themes unfolded. The blockades also extended into the online sphere: social media served as a key space for self-organization and information sharing.

Personal and Political Transformation Through Collective Engagement

The theme Personal and Political Transformation through Collective Engagement encompasses students' experiences of personal growth and of coming to see themselves as political actors during their participation in the blockades. Two sub-themes were identified. The first, Personal Growth Through the Experience of the Blockade, captures how the restructuring of everyday life created conditions for individual development, including expanded free time, new forms of socialization, and the acquisition of social competencies. The second, Emergence of Political Subjectivity and Civic Identity, traces how engagement in the blockades gave rise to a broader sense of civic responsibility, redirected participants' political attention from the global to the local, and contributed to a redefinition of the student role.

Personal Growth Through the Experience of the Blockade

The described changes in the structure of everyday life and new opportunities for socialization laid the foundation for students' personal growth. Participants recognize the blockade period as formative, suggesting that changes in the structure of everyday life and new opportunities for socialization helped students grow in ways they had not expected. Without classes and fixed schedules, students described a new sense of temporal freedom and flexibility, which they perceived as an opportunity for rest, self-care, socialization, and self-development. As one participant described: "I have more time to train, to dedicate to the extracurricular projects I am It is important to note that not all the students experienced this kind of relief and inspiration.

Some students experienced these modified routines as disorientation, loss of structure, confusion, and inability to maintain a coherent daily rhythm. Like one participant said: At first, it's all euphoria; it's interesting. However, over time, you realize you have less and less control over your life. In terms of some habits during the day and a simple routine.

And then that's it... It was inconvenient for me, let's say, I'm looking... I want to work, to get a job. To do something during the day. But I am not sure how long this will last.

Engagement in socialization, group discussions, and public speaking at plenums also emerged as significant aspects of the blockade experience. Several participants reported overcoming a fear of public speaking and social anxiety in general, gradually developing confidence and communication competencies. For example: I've become better at public speaking. I've relaxed when it comes to meeting new people. Students also reported that moving beyond their usual social circles led to exposure to diverse perspectives, which inspired them to learn and foster greater reflexivity and openness to diversity. As one participant noted: "I consider myself stubborn and firm in my views, but it's amazing to hear people say something that completely changes my opinion. That might be the most positive experience for me". These statements suggest that the blockade was not merely a disruptive event but an experience that helped students learn by doing, speak more confidently, tolerate different opinions, and become more open to people they would not otherwise have known.

Emergence of Political Subjectivity and Civic Identity

Students frequently described their involvement in the blockades as extending beyond a one-time reaction to specific socio-political circumstances. Participation was experienced as the beginning of a broader and more sustained commitment to collective action, civic engagement, and involvement in social issues. Blockades were described as a formative experience through which they increasingly perceived themselves as socially and politically engaged actors, while also developing a stronger sense of responsibility toward broader societal processes: I think the blockades will certainly come to an end, but our struggle definitely won't stop there. And… I believe we will continue… because essentially our goal is that, thanks to these blockades, even after they are over, students as such will keep this struggle and direction going.

In many ways, participants' accounts reflected a shift from observing broader political processes at a distance to a locally grounded form of political engagement that could be understood as a "think globally, act locally" orientation. While they described their previous engagement with political issues as relatively distant, abstract, or oriented toward global events beyond their reach, participation in protests and blockades redirected their attention and activity toward local forms of collective action in which they perceived themselves as capable of making tangible social and political change.

At the same time, although their actions were situated in specific local contexts (faculty, city, student community), participants increasingly interpreted these experiences within broader societal and political frameworks -democracy, justice, and the future of society. In this sense, participation contributed to the development of a perspective transcending individual or narrowly defined group interests, aligning local engagement with more general social and political principles: I used to read and follow things globally quite a lot -I was interested in all sorts of events happening around the world. But Serbia had always been a particularly difficult topic for me. I somehow felt that everything here was so bad, kind of absurd, and that nothing good was happening, and that there weren't really people you could try to do good things with.

At one point, I thought that this was simply too overwhelming for me to think about more deeply. But this situation really encouraged me -it kind of threw me into the arena and forced me to find my way. In some ways, I managed well, in others not so much, but I think it helped me a lot overall.

A recurring theme across interviews was a pronounced concern about the future at the macrosystemic level (Serbia). Participants frequently expressed a sense of responsibility toward broader societal outcomes, feeling it was their obligation to make Serbian society a better place, or that they would be to blame if things went downhill. This concern often served as a motivational force for engagement, reinforcing the perception that participation in the blockades was not only justified but necessary.

This growing sense of social responsibility was also reflected in participants' evolving understandings of what it means to be a student. Instead of perceiving this role as limited to learning and other academic responsibilities, participants increasingly framed it as encompassing social responsibility, civic engagement, and an obligation to respond to perceived injustices and contribute to broader social change. Thus, the role of the student was expanded to include an explicitly political and moral dimension:

Well, of course it's different. Now… now students… don't just study-they also take part in blockades, go out into the streets, help others, and travel to other cities. The whole notion of what it means to be a student is completely different now-it's been fully redefined. Now, a student… almost… It's not strictly necessary, but it's highly desirable to be fully socially engaged in whatever is going on.

Taken together, these findings suggest that participation in the blockades was closely linked to the emergence of a political subjectivity and a sense of civic identity among participants.

Through collective engagement, participants came to understand themselves as active members of a broader social and political community, oriented toward future engagement and social change.

Reconfiguration of Social Relations through Collective Engagement

The results suggest that participation in the student blockades led to pronounced changes in the shared sense of who they were as a group and ways of relating to others, both amongst the engaged students themselves and between the protesting students and their less-engaged peers.

First, the findings suggest that a novel collective identity emerged, one that could sustain their activist commitment beyond the blockades by fostering increased solidarity, mutual support, and a growing sense of belonging among protesting students. Additionally, participants described how this identity led to and sustained readiness for self-sacrifice and a moral commitment to the movement, often accompanied by collective hope -belief that their efforts would contribute to meaningful social and political change. Finally, the findings highlight that negotiating the boundaries and stability of the collective "we" was not always harmonious; students also described tensions, disagreements about who belonged, and unequal expectations about who was doing enough.

Formation of a New Collective Identity and Sense of Belonging

The majority of participants reported growing closer to other students who participated in the protests and blockades. The joint efforts and daily interactions related to working on mutual goals brought them closer together compared to the times when they were just studying together: …now we're more turned toward each other, we people from the faculty. I mean, that doesn't mean I socialize less with people outside the faculty, but somehow, at this moment, I just enjoy the company of people from the faculty more.

However, participants' deeper sense of connection with their colleagues and comrades was not driven solely by shared goals or time spent together. Ultimately, solidarity and mutual support were the key factors that deepened these relationships. The students experienced immense pressure, both internal and external, and dealt with stress, burnout, violence, and injustice. During those turbulent times, it was their comrades who truly understood what they were dealing with.

All interviewed students underscored the importance of the support system they have built: "We really became a community. I would single that out as the greatest value. The people around you believe in all of this; they believe in you, they make an effort, and contribute as much as they can." Moreover, it was not just the individuals that drew strength from the group; the group itself emerged strengthened and with a strong sense of collective identity. That is, struggles and solidarity joined together articulated the novel form of social identification -students-in-protest.

These narratives suggest that being in the blockades together, sleeping in faculty buildings, organizing protests, and arguing in plenums brought students closer than years of walking through the same hallways. What began as a socio-political action gradually evolved into a circle of people who knew, trusted, and relied on one another.

In addition, some participants expressed a growing sense of pride in themselves and their group as a whole. This manifested in feelings of joy and hope when witnessing students collaborating and building each other up, as well as when engaging positively with the wider community, which shared their goals and values: "...when I go to a blockade and see so many people, I feel a positive emotion; I feel proud of the citizens of Belgrade, and I feel proud of my friends who came there to support the blockade".

Such expressions indicate that pride further strengthened their sense of belonging to something larger, reinforcing the perception of participating in something meaningful and historically significant. The blockade was experienced not merely as a protest event, but as the formation of a community marked by shared values, emotional investment, and a sense of who they had become together.

Moral commitment, sacrifice, and future orientation

Building on the new collective identity, our participants typically displayed a pronounced readiness for self-sacrifice, articulated in narratives of "sacrifice for the greater good." As participants grow closer to peers who share their values and with whom they engage in the blockades, and as a novel form of collective identity emerges, these resources can be mobilized to sustain a struggle in which self-sacrifice becomes normalized. Thus, a strengthened sense of collective identity lent itself well to increased feelings of self-efficacy, pushing students to exert themselves and to contribute more to the movement and its success. Participants experience the deprivations and hardships they endure as worthwhile and meaningful, and as something they expect will ultimately yield positive outcomes: There will definitely be consequences -we've all lost something during the blockades, at the very least time and the academic year and so on. But I still think that, overall, we'll all come out of this much more positively compared to how we went in.", "I think I kind of accepted that, right at the beginning, we know why we're doing this, we know what our goal is, and I'm just looking in that direction. It's not that I'm particularly shaken… It all comes down to the reason why we're doing this.

In this way, personal losses (academic, emotional, professional) are framed as a necessary and legitimate sacrifice for a higher, collective goal. Sacrifice is experienced as a morally justified choice, often accompanied by minimizing personal consequences in comparison to the significance of the movement's success. Thus, collective engagement creates moral communities in which participants come to see themselves as "moral subjects", acting for the common good. In this sense, all of our participants share a deeply held hope that society will change for the better as a result of their collective struggle. Participants express their expectations that the ongoing protest movement would provide the impetus for further change, both on the plane of social consciousness, as well as concerning concrete material and political changes:

This has kind of woken people up more generally in society, so that we're moving out of this sort of deadlock. And I think it's really good that this has happened, and that the most profound changes are still to come, only if we continue to stay engaged and keep pushing in that direction. You can already see a certain shift for the better-I really don't see anything negative about it.

Regarding more concrete changes and expectations, the participants expressed their hopes that the current government would fall and that deep political restructuring would occur. In addition, these hopes were combined with others more closely related to their daily lives, such as a positive restructuring in how their universities operate, in the direction of increased participation and openness: (I wish) for the government to fall.

For us to get a system that actually functions. To get a university that will value our input, the input of students. (…) I would like it all to be resolved and for us to continue with our lives, while living in a better society. "I would change many more things. But still, this is, I'd say, a first step in that kind of… not exactly rebellion, but in that kind of opposition to the regime. And, I'd say, the first step toward change, toward something better in our community.

Our participants recognize and value what has been achieved to date, while emphasizing that even more profound change remains on the horizon. However, this hope was not always described as stable or taken for granted; rather, it often coexisted with uncertainty and ambivalence about the movement's future and the outcome of the protests, a theme we explore under the Anxiety, uncertainty and perceived threat in collective engagement. Despite this, the very experience of acting collectively appeared to generate and maintain a sense of possibility, even in the absence of clear or immediate results.

Negotiating the Boundaries and Stability of the Collective "We"

Alongside bonding with other protesting students, which strengthened social relationships and a sense of belonging, participation in the blockades also contributed to processes of relational differentiation. Across interviews, participants reported growing apart from friends and acquaintances who chose not to participate in the student movement or whose engagement they perceived as merely performative. The majority of participants highlighted the gradual weakening of those relationships, often attributed to a loss of shared everyday experiences and conversational ground as their routines increasingly revolved around the blockades: I was really in that position where I genuinely didn't know what to talk about anymore with people who weren't part of the blockade... like, they don't even know the abbreviations, and it was exhausting having to explain what all the abbreviations mean.

Others were more angered and hurt by the fact that some of their friends showed different political attitudes. Regardless of the specific emotional tone, the outcome was identical -collective action generated not only solidarity but also sharpened distinctions between those perceived as committed and those seen as passive, indifferent, or oppositional.

Despite the strong distinctions between out-group and in-group students, an important aspect of collective identity formation, the internal dynamics of the protesting group were far from homogeneous. Although students were united by a common political goal that set them apart from those not active in the movement, interviews also revealed internal tensions, disagreements, and social hierarchies that complicated their sense of unity. All interviewees described disagreements within the group, particularly regarding strategic decisions, the organization of activities, or the distribution of responsibilities. Moreover, significant ideological disagreements frequently arose. This meant that students not only had to fight for the chance to voice their opinions but also had to learn to navigate a sea of seemingly opposing ideas and act as a group, resulting in a proliferation of internal tensions. These accounts suggest that the blockades were not a space of seamless solidarity; students also had to manage disagreements about who counted as committed and whose voice carried weight. A strong sense of community and real internal friction existed side by side.

Emotional Strain and Coping in Collective Engagement

The theme Emotional strain and coping in collective engagement captures participants' emotional and psychological responses to prolonged engagement in the student blockades, as well as the strategies they used to regulate distress and sustain involvement over time. Participation was experienced as meaningful and empowering; however, it also entailed chronic exposure to uncertainty, social pressure, information overload, and emotional strain. The psychological challenges arising from these experiences, such as anxiety and burnout, were not viewed as individual shortcomings but rather as rooted in what the blockades themselves demanded of people: sustained presence, constant vigilance, and collective decision-making under pressure.

Additionally, participants did not manage emotions on their own; they understood when rest was permitted, whom they could turn to, and how to share and normalise distress within the group.

Rather than relying on individual strategies alone, participants drew on diverse and often overlapping mechanisms of managing their emotional state that both enabled resilience and, at times, reinforced patterns of overinvestment and vulnerability to burnout. This theme thus integrates both the experience of strain and the ways students supported and buffered each other, making sustained collective engagement possible, and comprises three subthemes: Overload, pressure, and burnout; Anxiety, uncertainty, and perceived threat; and Support systems and emotional regulation.

Living with uncertainty, vigilance, and threat

Participants' accounts consistently underlined uncertainty as a foundation of their psychological experience -uncertainty linked to the unpredictability of blockade duration, academic trajectories, and broader political developments. Anxiety and worry were not described as episodic but as something that lasted and became part of every life segment. Participants frequently expressed a sense of temporal suspension ("waiting"), accompanied by difficulties in planning and orienting toward the future. One student explained: "I can't even imagine what Saturday will look like, let alone how all of this will end. That honestly worries me the most." Some described a sense of confusion and disorientation while thinking about the future, and thus adopted a present-moment orientation: "We don't know what will happen, we're just going day by day, trying to figure things out as we go." Importantly, uncertainty encompassed individual, collective, and societal concerns. The individual concerns reflected worries about their academic trajectories, formal and informal consequences, and fear of repression. The concern for society reflected fears that if the movement fails, it would lead to apathy and long-term political disengagement of the entire society: "There is a fear that yet another political failure in the last three decades could lead to apathy dominating our society for the next 20 years, which would have catastrophic consequences." At the same time, narratives reflected hope and anxiety coexisted without resolving into either confidence or despair, representing a great psychological demand. Sustaining hope for change and a fear of failure over months, without a discernible endpoint, required ongoing emotional work. I don't know, while I'm reading all of it, my biggest worry is always that I can't see any end scenario, neither positive nor negative. Everything gets so mixed up that one moment you think, well, surely something good will come of it, and the next, we've only been in the blockade for three months, and we're asking for tectonic changes.

Across accounts, uncertainty became normalised in everyday functioning. Participants described postponing personal milestones (graduation, employment, relocation), making shorthorizon rather than long-term plans, and oscillating between bursts of motivation and periods of withdrawal as expectations about movement outcomes shifted. When participants tried to imagine what came next, they found themselves stuck between two possibilities: either the movement would succeed, and something fundamental would change, or it would fail, and nothing would. This informational vigilance was inseparable from anticipatory anxiety: each notification reopened the question of what might happen next, foreclosing the possibility of rest or psychological distance. Monitoring was not experienced as a voluntary choice but as a structural demand. The result was a low-level alertness that did not fully shut down even when nothing was immediately occurring. Keeping track of information did not resolve the uncertainty, keeping participants anchored in the immediate present while preventing them from using their resources.

The perceived continuous threat gave this hypervigilance an aim, so they were tracking something real, socially amplified, and constantly recurring through interpersonal communication and media. Participants were afraid of anticipated violence, repression, and surveillance almost on an everyday basis, producing a state of constant alertness and fear that was hard to shake. As one student described: "The worst part is the fear when we go out on the street… all those messages about riots, police, tear gas… You start thinking about your life." Since the fear was prolonged and continuous, individual attempts at coping were undermined by the omnipresent socially sustained triggers. Fear extended beyond immediate physical danger to also include reputational, institutional, and future-oriented risks, reflecting a feeling of exposure that extended beyond the immediate threat.

Experiencing overload, pressure, and burnout

Participants' accounts consistently positioned information overload, normative pressure, and emotional exhaustion as interrelated aspects of engagement that fed into one another. The continuous flow of updates across social media, messaging groups, and news outlets was experienced as both necessary for participation and cognitively overwhelming, contributing to making it hard to concentrate on anything else and wearing people down emotionally. As one participant noted: "We have a million WhatsApp groups, Twitter, Instagram, TV… my brain is overloaded, I can't take in any more information." Anxiety was also sustained by the volume of information participants felt obliged to track, resulting in a persistent state of vigilance carried in their bodies as much as their minds. This led to an unresolvable double bind: disengaging risked missing something that might matter, while persistent engagement produced exhaustion and a feeling of having simply taken in too much. One student said: "Whatever I'm doing, I'm checking updates. It's like I can't focus on anything else." Rather than self-regulating, this intensified engagement, as some described being "glued to the phone" and "obsessively following everything" for days at a time, creating a situation of compulsive tracking that did not restore calm but continued to generate more of the same anxiety.

Exceeding the capacity for information, participants also struggled to evaluate its credibility and construct their own understanding under rapidly shifting conditions. Across these accounts, the compulsive monitoring was driven by the impossibility of knowing which next message, post, or notification would change everything.

As a part of the same mechanism, participants described persistent self-evaluative pressure grounded in internalized group norms and social comparison. Engagement in the blockades was frequently assessed in terms of "doing enough," with how much you contributed becoming a measure of who you were. As one student stated: "I stress because I'm not doing anything… I want to do everything, but I can't finish anything." Another reflected: "It somehow gets measured how much you're there and how much you give -and that affects how you feel." Participants described feeling that absence from even a few plenums could erode their standing in the eyes of more consistently present peers: "if you miss two or three plenums, in their eyes you no longer have a clue, you're not competent or credible to say anything", so that stepping back, even briefly, carried anxiety about being left behind or judged. Besides this structural inability to disengage imposed by movement, there also existed a psychological unwillingness to disengage: "It is not an option for me to stop and say -enough," as one participant noted. External expectations from family and the broader social environment further intensified this sense of responsibility, as one student stated: "My father most often tells me that I should go out as much as possible, that I should be involved, that I should be... Umm... You know what I mean?" Within this context, emotional exhaustion and burnout emerged as the result of months of cognitive overload, emotional strain, and the relentless pressure to keep giving. Some of the most engaged participants reported feeling more drained, irritable ("After a few hours, I realize I'm arguing with my friend over nothing.") than usual, and, at times, disengaged from their organizational roles, particularly when efforts did not yield visible results ("It's sad to say, but right now I don't see my role anymore… in the organizational sense, I feel scattered,").

Rather than resulting in full withdrawal, exhaustion was often accompanied by a practical adjustment, pulling back without fully withdrawing, with some participants shifting toward more instrumental or limited forms of participation. One participant described this shift clearly: "That's when I realized I had completely burned out and that I simply could not function. And that, in that state, I was not contributing to others in any way -on the contrary, I was only making things harder for everyone, and I simply had to take a break."

Support systems and emotional regulation in collective engagement

Organized through the lens of collective norms, students used a range of strategies to manage emotional weight, often integrating behavioral, cognitive, and interpersonal resources.

Social support emerged as the most important coping resource. Relationships with family members, peers, partners, and professors helped students feel less alone, more certain in decisions they made, and able to keep going. One participant emphasized: "It means the most to me to have the support of those closest to me, to have peace at home," while another noted: "Here, we are sharing every joy and any concern." Support functioned not only as emotional comfort but also as a reinforcement of the sense that what they were doing was right and worth continuing. Sharing emotions within trusted networks within the movement itself was a crucial means of making sense of what they were going through. Group conversations enabled participants to normalize their experiences and reduce isolation: "When I hear others talk, I realize I'm not the only one who feels this way." At the same time, some participants described conflict avoidance and emotional suppression as strategies for maintaining group harmony. As one participant explained: "I do not talk about everything. Some things you just do not say." Physical activity was framed as an intentional "break" from political engagement, not a withdrawal, suggesting that participants negotiated their legitimacy in relation to implicit collective standards. It served as a means of distancing from the emotional intensity of ongoing engagement and restoring a sense of balance. As one participant noted: "Working out is my outlet.

Or going for a one-hour walk with music -that really helps me."

Participants also reported deliberate psychological distancing, including limiting media exposure, withdrawing from protest-related conversations, and seeking solitude. This distancing was framed as necessary to protect themselves emotionally rather than as disengagement. For example: "I increasingly find it helpful not to talk about it and to just be by myself." While mostly effective in reducing overload, such strategies were sometimes accompanied by feelings of guilt, revealing that detachment was not an unbiased individual choice but was evaluated against what the group expected of those truly committed.

While many strategies supported resilience through connection, finding meaning in what they were doing, and emotional regulation, others reflected pressures to manage themselves in ways the group required. Participants' accounts constructed coping and emotion management as not purely individual. They were shaped by the group, organized through shared norms about what was adequate, and inseparable from the same relationships that generated the strain.

Discussion

Participation in the 2024/2025 Serbian student blockades transformed how students understood themselves, their relationships, and their place in Serbian society. Reflexive thematic analysis (Braun & Clarke, 2019 , 2022) generated three interconnected emphases: personal development, relational changes, and collective emotion regulation. The study aimed to: (a) describe how students made sense of their participation; (b) identify the psychological, relational, and moral dimensions of sustained engagement under political repression; and (c) understand how they coped with its emotional demands. The discussion situates these themes within scholarship on emerging adulthood, collective action, and protest-related well-being. It develops an integrative interpretation in which solidarity is conceptualized as both a source of the psychological costs and a buffer against it.

Constructing the Blockades as a Psychosocial Phenomenon: A Reflexive Thematic Account

The analysis below was interpretative. All members of the research team lived in Serbia and were academically engaged in supporting student movement, facilitating rapport and interpretive sensitivity, while introducing potential bias noted when relevant in the analyses. The presented structure is one defensible reading of the corpus; alternative analytic positions may have produced different patterns and interpretations.

A first analytic emphasis concerns the co-emergence of personal development and political subjectivity under sustained collective participation. Alongside Arnett's (2000) account of emerging adulthood and experiential-learning scholarship (Gready & Jackson, 2025; Loader et al., 2015), the blockades functioned as an alternative developmental setting in which capacities such as public speaking, conflict navigation, and logistical coordination were acquired through situated practice rather than institutional curricula. The shift from student as an apolitical role to studentin-protest as a normatively loaded identity can be interpreted through theories of conscientization (Freire, 2005) and politicized collective identity (Simon & Klandermans, 2001; Tajfel & Turner, 1979), suggesting a transformation in how students understood themselves as political actors rather than merely an enactment of existing views. Understanding of inequality within society predicts increases in political engagement (Plummer et al., 2022), consistent with our accounts, which say that it was not a sudden modification but a gradual remodelling through sustained collective action. Freire's (2005) account of conscientization helps explain these findings because political subjectivity does not emerge solely from cognition but requires practical action through which individuals test their beliefs, assess the situation critically, and act to change it. Watts and Flanagan (2007) conceptualize sociopolitical development, distinguishing interconnected dimensions of critical reflection, critical motivation, and critical action. Rekker et al. (2017) further define that during emerging adulthood, young people consolidate political identity consistent with their prior attitudes, a pattern most pronounced among the highly educated.

Sustained engagement in an aggressive political setting during this life phase is therefore likely to produce a lasting political identity rather than a temporary response to circumstance. This interpretation may also reflect our attentiveness to growth narratives, as researchers who lived through the period, shaping how we constructed this analytic emphasis.

We hold this in deliberate tension with a counter-current. Some participants described the blockades as destabilizing, marked by disorientation, loss of routine, and difficulty deriving meaning from the disruption. Viewed through Ballard and Ozer's (2016) dual-pathway framework, the same structural conditions that opened developmental possibilities for some participants were experienced by others as sources of confusion, anxiety, and stress. Rather than resolving this tension, the blockades simultaneously created opportunities for self-development for some students while exposing others to psychological strain and difficulties in everyday functioning.

The psychosocial consequences are unlikely to be limited to the protest period itself. Vestergren A second analytic emphasis traces the web of relationships through which students developed a shared sense of identity. This interpretation requires caution, as concepts such as collective identity or moral community risk being valorized in-group cohesion while obscuring its costs and exclusions. Nevertheless, for many participants, the student-in-protest identity became central to self-concept rather than merely a social role. This process can be understood through social identity theory (Tajfel & Turner, 1979; Turner et al., 1987) and, in more intensive forms, identity fusion (Swann, as cited in Wibisono et al., 2022). However, we understood the collective identity as a processual act that, as Melucci (1995) describes, is a network of active relationships continually built through interaction, emotional investment, and the co-creation of shared meanings. This perspective complements social identity theory by highlighting internal tensions, group dynamics, and constant negotiations. For some students, the movement's goals felt indistinguishable from their own, and withdrawal felt like a betrayal rather than simple disengagement. Movement-relevant obligations were experienced as personally meaningful.

Participants described readiness for sacrifice, meaningful interpretations of loss, and emotionally intense intra-group bonds, aligning with Jasper's (2006 Jasper's ( , 2011) ) work on moral emotions and Durkheimian (1912 Durkheimian ( /1995) ) register on solidarity formed through shared suffering.

Relational differentiation, including growing distance from non-participating peers, internal disagreements, and ideologically marked subgroups, was treated neither as a failure of solidarity nor as a positive feature of identity formation. Boundary setting is structurally important to collective identity (Tajfel & Turner, 1979) but can also position those outside the boundary as morally distinct; however, our data do not allow firm conclusions regarding these consequences.

Our coding decisions on this point were repeatedly revised within the team because, as participants in the broader Serbian context, we were susceptible to reading boundary work more sympathetically than warranted. Einwohner et al. (2008) caution that likeness and disparity operate simultaneously: participants must construct themselves as similar to and different from multiple audiences at once, and the more overlap there is, the more identity work there will be. Participants' accounts of navigating disagreements reflect this kind of ongoing identity labor. Internal tensions were similarly informative. Although the plenum structure encouraged democratic participation, participants also described role ambiguity, unequal expectations regarding endurance and contribution, and decision-making strain. We could see epistemic authority within, highly connected to physical presence, so how respected someone's opinion depended not only on what participants knew, but also on being seen to be there consistently. These dynamics align with Ballard and Ozer's (2016) strain pathway and Gamson's (1992) account of normative regulation, how group norms come to govern individual behavior from within.

Participants' expressions of hope and future orientation partially align with the Social Identity Model of Collective Action (SIMCA; van Zomeren et al., 2008), in which shared identity and injustice jointly sustain participation even in the absence of immediate political outcomes, where perceived efficacy was not the factor that made contribution as expected. Several participants described continuing to participate despite not expecting success, and some, despite expecting failure. It should be noted that SIMCA is not a purely efficacy-based model; it explicitly foregrounds shared identity and perceived injustice as coequal motivational pathways.

Nevertheless, even accounting for these additional components, these accounts suggest that continued participation may also be sustained by moral commitment, habit, or solidarity independent of expected success. In future research, it should be more thoroughly examined what our respondents consider as success, because it might be defined differently than our understanding so far, so changing the society and mobilizing the nation could be a form of efficacy.

A third analytic emphasis concerns the emotional and coping dimensions, which we see as inseparable from the web of relationships described above. Evidence from Soucie et al. (2025) is particularly relevant, showing that among emerging adults, the dense relational bonds formed amid genuine uncertainty may constitute a social resource that protects against later isolation, even if those same conditions entail the psychological strain documented in this study. Emotional strain during the blockades was not confined to individual participants but was produced and managed collectively. Anxiety, burnout, and perceived threat emerged from structural features of the mobilization, including pervasive uncertainty, information overload, moral pressure, the absence of clear leadership, and the difficulty of making decisions by consensus. Organizational dynamics within the movement amplify these individual pressures. The horizontal, leaderless plenum model, while offering democratic participation and resilience, also produces stressors: prolonged considerations, role ambiguity, and difficulty voicing dissent without risking ostracism. At the same time, the group's unspoken expectations shaped participants' responses to these conditions.

An alternative framing that treats strain as individual vulnerability would obscure the analytically generative reading in which the same social fabric that sustained participation also generated the pressures from which participants needed to recover.

Accounts of anxiety and uncertainty are consistent with Hou and Bonanno's (2018) work on reduced predictability as a predictor of distress in collective action, operating simultaneously across academic futures, political outcomes, and personal safety. Anxiety and worry were not lived as periodic but as an ongoing and integral part of the movement, aligning with evidence that reduced perceived predictability in social movements is associated with later psychological distress (Hou & Bonanno, 2018). The normalization of uncertainty described by participants should not be interpreted simply as adaptation. Rather, it represents a substantive reorganization of how the future is imagined and planned for. Therefore, we avoid evaluating that shift as either healthy or unhealthy. The impulse to frame it positively, as resilience, would protect a movement we are broadly sympathetic with.

Burnout and overload resonate with, and can be approached through, Conner et al. (2023) and the broader activist-guilt literature, though we are cautious about its diagnostic register.

Participants described measuring personal worth through ongoing contribution and experiencing disengagement as a moral failure, as group norms they had taken on as their own rather than rules imposed from outside, consistent with Gamson's (1992) account of normative regulation. Ballard and Ozer (2016) identify moral obligation as a primary stressor because it is self-imposed and unresponsive to external reassurance: no amount of recognition from co-participants can resolve the internal sense that one should be doing more. We keep open the tension this raises: the more deeply students feel part of the movement, the more it functions simultaneously as a source of meaning and as a mechanism through which disengagement is experienced as a betrayal of the self. We do not resolve this by claiming that strong identification is straightforwardly costly, nor that the costs are simply the price of meaningful participation. Both readings would underestimate what participants gained and what they lost from the same process of identification.

These experiences align with findings that long-term emotional involvement in collective action impairs recovery processes and reduces responsiveness to positive cues (Bonanno & Burton, 2013; Hou & Bonanno, 2018). Irritability, which participants described as spilling over into their closest relationships, through arguing with friends over nothing and becoming short-tempered with people who were their primary source of support, is taken as evidence for the aforementioned. The costs of burnout were not seen as personal deficit but as relational damage.

The accounts of coping pointed toward one of the analysis's most theoretically generative insights. Participants' multi-layered coping strategies -including physical activity, deliberate distancing, emotional sharing within trusted networks, and selective avoidance of conflict -were recognizable through Park's (2010) meaning-based framework and Ballard and Ozer's (2016) social-connection pathway. The importance of emotional disclosure for psychological regulation and relational cohesion was previously described by Conner et al. (2023) and was evident in the present research, as group conversations allowed participants to normalize distress and reduce isolation. The data further suggested that coping strategies were themselves morally evaluated within the collective: rest could evoke guilt, distancing could feel like betrayal, and emotional suppression functioned less as a symptom of dysfunction than as a socially rational response to a group culture that rewarded those who kept going and made stopping feel shameful. The framing of physical activity as an intentional 'break' rather than a withdrawal is particularly revealing in this regard: it suggests that participants negotiated the legitimacy of rest against implicit collective standards, rather than simply choosing to step back when they needed to. This was one of the most prominent representations of the protection/exhaustion paradox that runs through the results. The same collective norms that made participants feel held, legitimate, and purposeful also set the terms under which rest, distancing, and emotional openness are not welcome. Coping was not a neutral individual act but a morally evaluated one, and that evaluation was guided by the relationships it was meant to sustain. Students appeared in media as a movement, not by individual names, which strengthened social identification and reduced vulnerability to repression, aligning with findings that depersonalized collective identities enhance movement resilience and cohesion (Dahlum & Wig, 2020; Reicher, 2011). This perspective highlights how coping strategies were embedded within the collective structure of the protest movement rather than located only at the individual level.

Alongside burnout, participants also described shared emotional intensity, pride, solidarity, and the feeling of being part of something that would be remembered, experiences that can be interpreted through Pizarro et al.'s (2022) account of collective effervescence. These accounts also converge with Altaras et al.'s (2003) documentation of the 1996/1997 Serbian student protests, in which initial fear and anger transformed, through collective action, into sustained pride and resilience. This convergence should be interpreted cautiously rather than taken as evidence of a stable cultural pattern, especially given the continuing symbolic significance of the 1996/1997 mobilization in Serbian public memory and the possibility that both researchers and participants interpreted current experience through an inherited sociocultural script. The counter-current registered in Theme 1, of participants describing the period as disorienting, may represent experiences that can not completely fit in previous narratives. Across the three emphases, we have constructed not a description of what the blockades were but an account reading of what they did: to participants, to their social relations, and to the categories through which they made sense of themselves. Throughout, we have sought to remain explicit about the analytic choices shaping this reading, rather than presenting it as the only possible interpretation of the data. The following section develops this analysis into a more integrative theoretical interpretation.

Integrative interpretation

We have presented the three analytic emphases as integrated, rather than parallel findings.

Personal transformations and social relations mutually influence one another, sustaining collective action and increasing emotional demands. Consequently, coping operated relationally. The social bonds functioned both as sources of psychological pressure and as buffers against it. These connections represent an analytic construction, grounded in the data and shaped by our perspective as researchers, rather than a structural property inherent to the phenomenon. This circularity extends Ballard and Ozer's (2016) dual-pathway model by suggesting that stress and social connection were more tightly entangled than empirically separable. Rather than simply buffering stress, the group also constituted the context in which stress was both produced and mitigated. The plenum structure helps specify this dynamic. By eliminating formal leadership and distributing responsibility horizontally, the movement fostered civic agency and democratic participation, while generating role ambiguity, pressure to do more, and cumulative exhaustion.

Because institutional role definitions were absent, what counted as "enough" remained personally and collectively undefined. Students described frustration with organizational inefficiency, as criticizing the process felt like undermining the movement. Combined with external threats, safety concerns, stigmatization by state-aligned media, and surveillance, these dynamics produced cumulative emotional depletion. Relationships within the movement provided emotional containment, legitimacy, and resilience, while simultaneously generating implicit expectations and self-imposed pressure.

Two further integrative observations follow. First, political subjectivity in this context was constructed through embodied practice rather than adopted as a cognitive position, including organizing logistics, sleeping in faculty buildings, arguing in plenums, and navigating interpersonal tensions. This is consistent with Bourdieu's (1990) account of habitus and phenomenological approaches in which identity is sedimented through practice rather than declared cognitively. Porter (2013) similarly identified political identity as a distinctive developmental construct that predicts a level of later social involvement. The shift from academic obligation to civic responsibility may therefore have consequences beyond the protest period, shaping how they understand their role in society after the blockades end. These frameworks remain relatively underrepresented in the collective action literature (Freire, 2005; Simon & Klandermans, 2001; van Zomeren et al., 2008). This may help explain why short-term protest exposure tends not to produce durable civic identity, whereas prolonged blockade-style engagement appears more likely to do so.

Second, we interpret the moral embedding of coping as structurally produced rather than individually chosen, while recognizing this as an analytic choice. Existing coping frameworks (Ballard & Ozer, 2016; Conner et al., 2023; Park, 2010) treat coping as an individual repertoire of strategies, while present data suggest that coping itself was subject to collective moral evaluation.

What might be coded clinically as maladaptive coping -overcommitment, suppression, and inability to disengage -can instead be understood as a response to the movement's moral culture required, rather than a personal failure to manage oneself. As a reflexive caveat, we recognize that a research team's sympathy toward the movement's values may have inclined us to frame these dynamics as sacrifice rather than as structurally induced vulnerability, and we treated this as a potential source of bias throughout the analysis.

Finally, we interpret the Serbian context as a structural condition that amplifies both the resources and the costs identified across the three emphases. Political polarization, repressive state responses, and the movement's protracted, unresolved nature shaped the emotional tone of participants' accounts. The convergence with Altaras et al.'s (2003) documentation of the 1996/1997 student protests suggests a culturally specific trajectory from fear and anger to solidarity and pride, through which Serbian students may interpret collective action amid perceived institutional failure. Those participants similarly described their participation as a formative experience with consequences for identity and self-understanding. Whether this trajectory is genuinely emergent from participants' lived experience or shaped by a pre-existing cultural inheritance associated with earlier student mobilizations (e.g., protests in 1996/1997) is a question that warrants reflexive scrutiny and further empirical investigation.

Limitations and Future Directions

Several limitations should temper the interpretation of these findings. Convenience and snowball sampling, although appropriate for a qualitative, saturation-oriented design, limit transferability. Participants were predominantly from upper-middle-class backgrounds and major urban universities, while students from smaller institutions, private universities, or with different socioeconomic profiles are underrepresented. This socioeconomic structure may have influenced several processes identified in the analysis. Political efficacy, willingness to engage in collective action, educational security, the capacity to tolerate prolonged academic disruption, and the ability to sustain long-term activist involvement are all shaped by access to economic, social, and cultural resources. Consequently, participants may have had greater opportunities to absorb the personal costs of engagement and to frame uncertainty, sacrifice, and collective action in more agentic or future-oriented ways than students facing greater material constraints.

Reflexivity constituted both a strength and a limitation. The team's embeddedness in the same socio-political and academic context as participants enabled rapport and interpretive sensitivity, but also introduced the possibility that narratives of growth, solidarity, empowerment, and meaning-making would appear particularly salient within the interpretive process. Although reflexive procedures were used to mitigate this risk, they cannot fully eliminate it.

The design was cross-sectional, with interviews conducted at a specific point in an ongoing mobilization. The psychological experiences described may therefore shift substantively as the movement evolves, concludes, or otherwise resolves. Future research should pursue longitudinal designs that track psychological outcomes beyond the conclusion of the movement. Since this research focused only on active students, future research should incorporate other student groups (activists with varying levels of engagement), which would help assess the generalizability of these findings. Quantitative studies could test the relationships identified qualitatively here, including the link between collective identity strength and vulnerability to burnout. Finally, dedicated research with marginalized student groups within the movement is needed, as their experiences may diverge from the dominant narratives identified in this sample.

Conclusion

The 2024/2025 Serbian student blockades constitute not only a political event but also an experience that shaped who students were, how they related to others, and what they believed themselves capable of. In the absence of an adequate institutional response to perceived injustice, protesting students developed their own sense of civic identity and community bound by shared values and mutual support. The present analysis suggests that sustained collective action reshapes self-concept, social relations, and emotional functioning, while the bonds that enable this transformation may also generate psychological strain. We offer this reading as one defensible interpretation shaped by our position as a research team embedded in the same socio-political context as our participants. These findings carry implications for universities, mental health practitioners, and civil society organizations concerned with supporting young people who engage in collective action, and they invite further scholarship to take protest seriously as a site of psychological development rather than to treat it solely as political behavior.
