Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download information from other preregistration sites, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 685 tests with 34 instances of inconsistent reporting, Statcheck correctly detected 34 of them, and incorrectly identified 26. Therefore 0% of true instances were missed, and 43% of detections were false positives. See https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/ for more details of the validation.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Of 1,783 non-significant p-values in the ground truth, version 0.4.4.3 found 1,719 (3.6% missed), and 94.8% of its 1,814 detections were in the ground truth. How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. A TRO uses them for each trov:TransparentResearchPerformance. * run_results$exit_status: the exit status of the process that ran the script (0 = ended normally); NA when the process did not report one (a time limit, or Docker failing to start the container). * artifacts: one row per file, with stage (deposit: as downloaded from the repository, hashed from the downloaded file; before_run: the run folder just before the first script ran, after metacheck’s own changes, such as rewritten paths and unpacked archives; after_run: only the files the scripts made (status new), changed (changed) or removed (removed), as an unchanged file is the one listed before the run), path (relative to the run folder, as valid UTF-8, a byte of another encoding written as <xx>), size (bytes), sha256, mime_type (by extension, NA when unknown), source (the file’s address in the repository, for deposit). The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). Files metacheck itself copied or made between passes (see modifications, output_copied, file_placed) show in after_run too. A TRO uses the rows as trov:ResearchArtifacts and each stage as a trov:ArtifactArrangement. * modifications$sha256_before, modifications$sha256_after: the hash of the changed script as downloaded and as it ran; NA when the script is not at the same path in both (a script made from an R Markdown file). * environment: the system that ran the code (trov:TransparentResearchSystem in a TRO): metacheck_version, started_at, ended_at, host (R version, platform, operating system), sandbox, network_isolated (TRUE only when every Docker run had --network none; the process sandbox does not stop the network), internet, timeout_per_script, total_timeout, python_local (the Python run outside Docker and its version), and docker: runs (for each kind of run, r_run, r_install, python_run, python_install, the image and the docker run arguments, such as --network none, --read-only, --cap-drop ALL, no-new-privileges, the process limit and the memory and processor limits) and images (for each image, its ID and digests from docker image inspect, its creation time and the R and Python versions its environment declares).

run_results$checked says what the run of each script shows about the code, in five groups, so that a script that does not reproduce is kept apart from one the check could not test: ran (it ran to the end); code_error (it did not reproduce: it stopped with an error of the code or the deposit, could not be read, or needs a file the deposit does not hold); dependency (a package or program was not available: a package that could not be installed or was not loaded, a program outside R and Python, a system library, or R crashing in a package’s compiled code); time (it did not finish within the time limit for one script, or was not started because the time for all scripts had run out); and not_checkable (it could not be checked automatically: it needs a person, the internet, a key or another setting of the authors’ computer, one platform such as Google Colab, Windows or OpenSAFELY, more memory than the run allows, a file that was not downloaded, a screen, or a program that runs it, or it is a file of a package of the code that Python runs only as a module of that package). It is NA for files run inside another script, files that are not an analysis, files not read, and a Docker failure. summary_table counts each group (repro_ran_n, repro_code_error_n, repro_dependency_n, repro_time_n, repro_not_checkable_n), and the report’s first sentence gives them. The traffic light reflects the static readiness signals by default; a script in code_error that stopped with an error makes it red, as a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure, and a script in one of the other three groups keeps it from being green. Calls that need a screen, which the run does not have (dev.print(), dev.copy2pdf(), dev.copy2eps(), savePlot(), windows(), win.graph(), x11(), quartz(), View()), are replaced before the run by calls that need none, which modifications records as screen_call_replaced. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

Background Growing evidence has explored how emotional intelligence (EI) and humour styles interact in shaping adaptive outcomes. However, systematic evidence on this relationship remains limited. This study aims to systematically review and narratively synthesise the literature that has investigated the relationship between EI and humour styles.

Methods A search of five databases -MEDLINE, PsycINFO, Web of Science, PubMed, and Psychology and Behavioural Sciences Collection -was performed. Studies were included if they: (1) were empirical and quantitative; (2) involved adult participants recruited from nonclinical settings; (3) used variables specifically referred to as humour styles or EI tests; and (4) were published on or before July 1, 2024.

Of the initial 489 articles identified, nine were included in the qualitative synthesis. EI was consistently and positively associated with adaptive humour styles (self-enhancing and affiliative humour) and, in some studies, negatively associated with maladaptive humour styles (self-defeating and aggressive humour), with small-to-moderate correlations. Trait EI generally demonstrated closer associations with all humour styles compared to ability EI. Self-enhancing humour showed the most consistent correlations with both ability and trait EI across all adopted EI measures. Few and weak correlations were noted concerning aggressive humour.

The present review highlighted the relationship between emotional competencies and trait humour. A next step in research is to examine ways of fostering appropriate humour behaviours adapted to specific contexts, and enhancing emotional and social functioning. Additional effects on low mood, anxiety and social function also could be recorded over time to identify whether humour enhancement approaches reduce the risks or impact of low mood or anxiety.

1. Introduction

An increasing number of studies have explored the interaction between emotional intelligence (EI) and humour styles due to growing interest in how the two constructs influence the achievement of adaptive life outcomes, such as psychological well-being, in adults within nonclinical settings [1] [2] [3]. Generally, EI refers to a set of competencies in perceiving, understanding, processing, and managing our own and others' emotions [4, 5]. High-EI individuals are better at perceiving and managing emotions, integrating emotional experience with cognitive processes, and adapting to various contexts. Consequently, EI is closely associated with improved psychological and physical health [6, 7], enhanced relationship satisfaction [8, 9], more effective coping strategies [10], and heightened self-esteem and optimism [6]. Similarly, various aspects of humour have been shown to beneficially influence mental health [11, 12] and physical health [13] [14] [15]. This systematic review examines the empirical evidence on the proposed relationship between EI and humour.

1.1. Emotional intelligence (EI)

The concept of EI, introduced in 1990 [16], has been comprehensively studied, with two main theoretical and measurement approaches: ability EI [4] and trait EI [5, 17]. Ability EI refers to the capacity to reason about emotions and to use emotions and emotional knowledge to enhance thought [18], encompassing four branches: perceiving emotions, using emotions to facilitate thought, understanding emotions, and managing one's emotions and those of others [19]. Two maximal performance assessments, the Multi-Factor Emotional Intelligence Scale (MEIS) [20] and Mayer-Salovey-Caruso Emotional Intelligence Test (MSCEIT) [19], were developed to measure the four-branch ability EI model.

Trait EI focuses on the subjective perception of emotions and self-evaluated competency in managing emotional information, which is more closely related to personality traits [5, 17].

Trait EI is usually assessed with self-report tests (e.g. EIS -Emotional Intelligence Scale; TEIQue -Trait Emotional Intelligence Questionnaire). A comprehensive model defines trait EI as a unique, multifaceted trait within the lower levels of personality hierarchies, reflecting the emotional dimensions of personality [5]. The TEIQue was developed to assess the four factors of this model: Emotionality, Self-Control, Sociability, and Well-Being [21]. The EIS assesses EI unidimensionally based on its original conceptualisation [22]. However, some researchers argued that EIS was better represented by four factors, including Appraisal of Emotions (self and others), Expression of Emotions, Regulation of Emotions (self and others), and Utilization of Emotions (for problem-solving) [23]. Recent studies found that the EIS captures broader content, including self-efficacy [24], and shows moderate-to-strong correlations with personality traits [25], supporting it as a trait EI measure [24]. The Genos Emotional Intelligence Inventory (i.e. Genos EI), designed for workplace settings, is a self-and raterreport assessment of the frequency of emotionally intelligent behaviours [26]. It comprises seven dimensions, such as Emotional Self-Awareness, Emotional Expression, and Emotional Self-Control. Different EI subscales may relate to humour in distinct ways, offering deeper insight into emotional competencies.

The correlations between ability EI and trait EI are small [27, 28], indicating that the two types of measurement assess distinct constructs. Assessing the possession of emotional knowledge and skills is different from evaluating confidence in emotional ability. Ability and trait models both include intrapersonal and interpersonal EI aspects, allowing theoretical connections with other intra-and inter-personal processes.

1.2. Humour styles

The concept of humour originated in the 19th century, with an aesthetic connotation referring to the capacity to appreciate humour, as well as a narrower definition describing it as a sympathetic form of amusement [29, 30]. However, over two centuries, there remains no consensus definition of this complex and elusive phenomenon. From a psychological perspective, humour is a form of social play that encompasses social, cognitive, emotional, and behavioural components [31] [32] [33] [34] [35] [36]. Eysenck (1972) [36] conceptualised humour in three distinct dimensions: quantitative (being easily and frequently amused), qualitative (sharing similar humour preferences with others), and productive (amusing others). Hehl and Ruch [37] expanded Eysenck's framework by linking sense of humour to individual differences and conceptualised it as a stable personality trait [38]. Sense of humour is increasingly seen as a multidimensional and relatively stable personality trait [29], where cognitive, emotional, and social abilities allow us to understand, respond to, and produce humour in daily life [39].

However, systematic categories of humour remain elusive. The Humorous Behaviour Q-sort Deck (HBQD) [40] identified ten styles (five bipolar) of humorous behaviour that are socially constructive and interpersonally competent, capturing how individuals use humour in everyday interactions. The Comic Style Markers (CSM) [41] identified eight comic styles focused on humour content, representing distinct types or qualities of humour. The Humour Styles Questionnaire (HSQ) [42] identified four types of humour based on individuals' preferences for engaging in humour in daily life. Designed to examine links between everyday humour and psychological well-being, the HSQ is currently the most widely used measure in humour research [43, 44]. It is the focus of this study.

In the HSQ, humour has been conceptualised as either adaptive or maladaptive based on its psychological function, being benign or detrimental, and categorised as intrapersonal or interpersonal, depending on whether it enhances the self or improves relationships [42]. Four trait-based humour styles were distinguished. Self-enhancing humour reflects a humorous and positive perspective on life even in stressful and adverse situations, linked to perspective-taking humour [45] and coping efforts [46]. Affiliative humour involves benign, respectful jokes that harmoniously amuse others, foster social bonds, and alleviate tension [42]. Self-defeating humour involves joking at one's own expense to entertain others and gain approval, potentially functioning as a form of defensive denial to mask negative emotions [42]. Aggressive humour, involving disparaging jokes that may hurt or alienate others, is often associated with hostility and low relationship satisfaction [42]. Although these styles are distinguishable, they often overlap, as the beneficial and detrimental functions of humour exist along a continuum rather than forming a strict dichotomy [42]. For example, gentle teasing may serve an affiliative function by fostering group cohesion, despite containing subtle aggression [47].

1.3. The connection between EI and humour styles

EI and humour styles have each been independently linked to outcomes such as health and wellbeing [6, 7, 11, 12, 48], and emerging evidence suggests possible associations between them.

Although a theoretical framework connecting EI and humour is currently lacking, the following theories may help explain the associations.

A relationship between EI and humour styles might be explained by the Differential Emotions Theory (DET), which considers emotion motivation as a crucial component of EI and focuses on the adaptive, motivational, and cue-producing characteristics of emotions [49].

Emotions guide thoughts and actions, motivate or direct behaviours, and may drive others' behaviours through emotional expressions [50]. Effective use of emotions could facilitate thought-action patterns and enhance life outcomes [51]. Greater emotional competence, such as better awareness and regulation of emotions, seems closely linked to adaptive humour styles [52]. Difficulties in emotional competence, such as deficiency in perception and management of emotions, seem to be linked to maladaptive humour. Individuals deploying self-defeating humour are less likely to understand and regulate emotions. They use humour in a selfdisparaging way to gain approval and mask negative feelings [42]. Those who engage in aggressive humour may struggle to understand others' emotions and manage their own, causing offence and alienation. These associations may also arise from the close relationship between EI and empathy.

Empathy refers to the ability to perceive others' emotions (affective empathy) [53] and rationally take others' perspectives (cognitive empathy) [54]. Individuals engaging in affiliative humour better perceive the emotional information of others (affective empathy), and facilitate relationships through amusement. This humour style is linked to emotional sensitivity and appropriate social timing [52]. Individuals who engage in self-enhancing humour are more adept at managing emotional information to cope with stress and maintain positive perspectives in adversity. The positive correlation between self-enhancing humour and EI may be explained by their shared basis in cognitive empathy.

The pathway connecting EI and humour styles might be explained by the broaden-andbuild theory of positive emotions, which proposes that positive emotions broaden individuals' short-term perceptions, thoughts, and actions, and help build long-term personal resources [55, 56]. The positive associations between EI and emotional well-being suggest that higher EI may elicit more positive emotions, which promote flexible and prosocial response tendencies [55] and a wider range of coping strategies [57]. This connection extends to humour styles, as humour is considered a coping strategy and a desirable response to the demands of everyday life [42].

EI is likely to be positively associated with adaptive humour and negatively associated with maladaptive humour. Trait EI may have a closer relationship with humour styles than ability EI. Meta-analyses indicate that trait EI has stronger associations with health outcomes than ability EI [6, 7], and is therefore likely to be more closely associated with humour styles than ability EI. Both trait EI measures and the HSQ assess trait-based constructs that reflect real-life behavioural tendencies (i.e. typical-performance), and may therefore be closely associated. By contrast, ability EI measures assess EI as a cognitive ability, reflecting knowledge of optimal emotion management strategies (i.e. maximum-performance) but not necessarily real-life behaviour [58].

To date, there has not been a systematic review investigating the relationship between EI and humour styles, which could enhance the understanding of different theoretical models (i.e. ability and trait EI) and EI components, and their relationships with each humour style.

Although trait EI theoretically may have closer associations with humour styles than ability EI, systematic evidence is lacking. A systematic review of the relationship between EI and humour styles was, therefore, conducted to provide a fuller picture of this relationship, test whether trait EI has closer associations with humour styles than ability EI, and to identify literature gaps.

This review may inform emotion, personality, and behaviour research, with practical implications for enhancing emotional and social well-being and mitigating risks of low mood and anxiety. The research questions and hypotheses follow: RQ1 What has been found in previous studies regarding the relationship between ability/trait EI and humour styles among adults in non-clinical settings? RQ2 How do previous studies describe the relationship between ability/trait EI and humour styles among adults in non-clinical settings? H1 EI is expected to be positively associated with adaptive humour styles and negatively associated with maladaptive humour styles.

H2 Trait EI shows a stronger association with humour styles than ability EI.

2. Method

The Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-analysis (PRISMA) guidelines [59] were followed to ensure the reliability and transparency of the review. The current review was registered in PROSPERO (ID: CRD42018089334).

2.1. Search strategy

Electronic databases including MEDLINE, PsyclNFO, Web of Science, PubMed and Psychology and Behavioural Sciences Collection were searched. Keywords were combinations of the following: emotional intelligence, emotional competence, emotional self-efficacy, humour, humour styles, humour styles questionnaire, HSQ, wit and humour, joke, and cartoons.

The Boolean operators (OR, AND) and truncations were used. The full search strategy can be found in the supplementary file 1.

Searches were not limited for start date and ended on July 1, 2024. These searches were supplemented by hand-searching the reference lists of eligible studies and performing forward citation searches of the included studies using Web of Science. No language restrictions were applied to the search, but only English language articles were included at the screening level.

The process of developing the search strategy for this review was done in collaboration with the subject librarian of the University of Glasgow.

2.2. Eligibility criteria

>Eligibility criteria 2.2.1.

Study characteristics

Studies were included if they met the following criteria: (1) Empirical studies that provided precise statistical tests (e.g., Pearson correlation, regression) of the correlation between EI and humour styles. (2) Studies that used adult (i.e., age 18 years and over) and participants recruited from non-clinical settings. (3) Studies that used variables specifically referred to as humour styles or EI, excluding studies that used other although related variables (e.g., coping humour, social intelligence). (4) Studies that used a psychometrically validated measure of at least one EI component. (5) Studies that used at least one of the four humour styles measured with the HSQ [42]. (6) Studies that have been published in peer reviewed journals. We adopted a cautious and quality-sensitive inclusion strategy to ensure conceptual and methodological clarity. Sense of humour is a complex and multifaceted construct, and any single instrument captures certain aspects while overlooking others [60]. Although related concepts such as coping humour (measured by the Coping Humour Scale, CHS) [61] are associated with psychological well-being, such measures were developed for use in specific contexts, such as stressful situations, and lack the capacity to distinguish between adaptive and maladaptive humour styles. Similarly, social intelligence refers to the ability to understand people and effectively relate to them [62], whereas EI focuses more specifically on perceiving, understanding, and regulating emotions. To maintain conceptual precision, studies based on these related but distinct constructs were excluded.

There were no restrictions on participant demographics such as gender, socio-economic status, and year of publication.

2.6. Data synthesis and analysis

Compared with the multifaceted and complex concept of humour, EI is more widely recognised as a consensual conceptualisation, which provides a theoretical basis for narratively synthesising studies that use different EI instruments. However, substantial heterogeneity in measurement tools (e.g. MSCEIT, TEIQue, EIS, Genos EI), subscale structures, and reporting formats (e.g. standardised β coefficients, Pearson's r, a single EI-humour style pairing), alongside the limited number of studies per instrument (none exceeding three) and the small overall number of studies included, made a quantitative synthesis statistically unfeasible and methodologically problematic. Additionally, the included studies varied in sample characteristics and research contexts (e.g. twins, psychology students, Western/Eastern samples), which would further compromise the validity of a meta-analysis and risk masking important differences in how EI and its dimensions are related to humour styles. Considering these considerations, and in accordance with recommendations from the Cochrane Handbook [64] and the Synthesis Without Meta-analysis guideline [65], meta-analysis was deemed inappropriate given the conceptual and methodological heterogeneity. Therefore, a narrative synthesis was adopted. This approach allowed us to better account for variation in conceptual frameworks, contextual settings, and methodological designs, and to offer a more meaningful and theoretically grounded interpretation of the relationship between EI and humour styles. The studies were synthesised based on the reported correlations between EI/its subscales and each humour style, and are presented according to the distinction between ability and trait EI. We also systematically presented the range of reported effect sizes, and examined patterns of association between humour styles and different dimensions of EI across studies and measurements. This approach offers transparent and theoretically informed account of the current evidence base.

As the TEIQue demonstrated the most consistent associations with the HSQ, and was used in studies with the largest sample sizes, we conducted a supplementary calculation of the effect size for the correlation between the TEIQue and the HSQ. The results are reported in the supplementary file 2.

3.3. Ability EI and humour styles

Yip & Martin (2006) adopted the MSCEIT to investigate the relationship between EI and humour styles [67]. A total of 111 undergraduate students (45 men and 66 women) participated.

Their ages ranged from 18 to 24 years (M = 18.8, SD = 0.95). Men scored significantly higher than women on aggressive humour whereas they scored lower on total EI and all branches of EI except Emotional Perception. Partial correlations were conducted due to these gender differences. Three weak correlations between humour styles and ability EI were obtained. They reported that self-enhancing humour was positively related to Emotional Management (r = 0.24, p < 0.05); aggressive and self-defeating humour were negatively related to Emotional Perception (r = -0.20, p < 0.05; r = -0.28, p < 0.01).

3.4. Trait EI and humour styles

Eight articles used self-report EI measures and included 8978 participants (2226 men and 6745 women) aged 17 to 90 years. Gender differences were investigated in two studies. Ogurlu (2015) and Wang et al. (2019) found that men scored higher than women in aggressive humour and self-defeating humour [1, 95] but scored lower than females on overall EI and all subscales in Ogurlu's (2015) study [95]. Among the eight articles, five reported associations between overall versions [89, 97, 98]. These studies reported that adaptive humour styles were found to be positively associated with all EI components and overall EI (r ranging from 0.18 to 0.59, all p < 0.05 or 0.001), whereas maladaptive humour styles were negatively associated in some studies with EI (r ranging from -0.09 to -0.41, all p < 0.05 or 0.001). Karahan et al. (2019) obtained all negative correlations between maladaptive humour and components of/overall EI with small-to-moderate magnitude [68]. However, Wang et al. (2019) reported negative correlations only between maladaptive humour and Utilization of Emotions, Regulation of Emotions, and overall EI, all of small magnitude; no correlation was found between maladaptive humour and Appraisal of Emotions [1]. Additionally, Ogurlu (2015) found no correlation between maladaptive humour and trait EI [95]. In both articles, adaptive humour styles were found to be positively correlated with Global TEI and its four facets (r ranging from 0.10 to 0.49, all p < 0.004 or 0.001), except for the correlation between affiliative humour and Self-control. Maladaptive humour styles were negatively associated with Global TEI (r ranging from -0.14 to -0.35, all p < 0.004 or 0.001) and Emotionality (r ranging from -0.13 to -0.28, all p < 0.004 or 0.001). Self-defeating humour was negatively associated with Well-being (r ranging from -0.14 to -0.33, all p < 0.004 or 0.001). No correlation was obtained between aggressive humour and Sociability. Aggressive humour showed fewer associations than the other three humour styles.

Gignac et al. (2014) tested the correlation between the general version of Genos EI and

humour styles [99], focusing on the four subscales most relevant to humour: Emotional Awareness of Others, Emotional Expression, Emotional Self-management, and Emotional Selfcontrol. All four subscales were associated positively with adaptive humour styles (r ranging from 0.12 to 0.44, all p < 0.05) and negatively with maladaptive humour styles (r ranging from -0.14 to -0.30, all p < 0.05).

In general, trait EI was regularly and positively correlated with adaptive humour styles and negatively correlated in some studies with maladaptive humour styles, with correlations of small-to-moderate magnitude. Self-enhancing humour showed the most consistent moderate correlations with trait EI across all self-report measures (r ranging from 0.27 to 0.59, all p < 0.05). Few and weak correlations were reported between aggressive humour and trait EI (r ranging from -0.13 to -0.41, all p < 0.05).

4. Discussion

>Discussion

4.1. Summary

This systematic review used a narrative synthesis to explore the relationship between EI and humour styles from ten relevant studies reported in nine articles. These studies investigated this relationship across five different EI measures belonging to ability EI and trait EI models.

Included articles were generally deemed to be of a good quality based on the criteria of the AXIS tool, albeit with some important limitations. This is the first systematic review to synthesise the evidence for the relationship between EI and humour styles.

Overall, EI was regularly and positively associated with adaptive humour styles and negatively associated in some studies with maladaptive humour styles, with weak-to-moderate magnitude. Although trait EI generally showed closer associations with all humour styles than ability EI, this should be concluded with caution because there was only one study focused on ability EI. Self-enhancing humour showed the most consistent relationship with both ability and trait EI across all measures. Few and weak correlations were found between aggressive humour and EI.

4.2. The relationship between ability EI and humour styles

Three correlations were identified between ability EI and humour styles. Emotional Perception involves identifying and interpreting emotional information from others, with a focus on emotional self-awareness [100]. Its negative correlation with two maladaptive humour styles suggests that negative humour may be linked to difficulties in emotion recognition and interpretation. In line with the DET, individuals with more maladaptive humour are poorer at accurately perceiving emotional cues [49]. This lack of adaptive emotional motivation and response can lead to disparagement of others or oneself and suppression of feelings. Selfenhancing humour was positively correlated with Emotional Management; the ability to regulate one's own and others' emotions, including maintaining positive affect, managing negative emotions, and generating context-appropriate responses [100]. This branch requires advanced emotional problem-solving ability through processing emotional information, using emotion knowledge to drive adaptive responses (e.g. maintaining positivity in adversity).

Empathy, a significant EI subcomponent underlying this process [101], is often seen as central to self-enhancing humour. The associations between two intrapersonal humour styles and ability EI suggest that the interaction between emotional problem-solving and humour is relevant to intrapersonal processes. These findings show that high-ability EI people tend to systematically use intrapersonal humour, suggesting a mechanism for facilitating humour behaviours through emotional skills. This mechanism may link ability EI to health and wellbeing.

Affiliative and self-enhancing humour styles were usually deemed related to EI. However, in this review, self-enhancing humour was only related to Emotional Management, and affiliative humour was not correlated with either branches or with total ability EI. This might be explained by the different approaches used to assess ability EI (MSCEIT) and humour styles (HSQ), and the theoretical models underpinning these two constructs. The focus of MSCEIT is on emotional problem-solving skills, whereas the HSQ assesses trait-like humour rather than a cognitive ability such as humour production. As women had higher EI and lower aggressive humour levels, partial correlation analysis was conducted to adjust for the influence of gender, potentially providing a clearer picture of the direct relationship between ability EI and humour styles.

Most of the correlations between ability EI and humour styles were non-significant, suggesting that having better emotion knowledge and problem-solving skills may not be sufficient to convert EI into adaptive thoughts and actions in everyday life. This corresponds to health meta-analyses, where ability EI showed weaker associations with overall mental and physical health versus trait EI [6, 102, 103].

4.3. The relationship between trait EI and humour styles

Across the eight studies using self-report measures, overall trait EI was consistently and positively associated with adaptive humour, and negatively with maladaptive humour.

In terms of intrapersonal humour, overall trait EI was positively associated with selfenhancing humour and sometimes negatively correlated with self-defeating humour. These results suggest that being confident in one's EI abilities was more likely to translate strong emotional skills into prosocial and health-promoting behaviours, as well as a humorous outlook towards adversity. The inconsistent correlations for self-defeating humour may reflect the multifaceted nature of trait EI, as its distinct facets could relate to self-defeating humour in divergent ways.

Reasonably consistent evidence linking EI components to intrapersonal humour styles was found only for self-enhancing humour, which was positively associated with all trait EI components. Thus, empathy may play a key role in the link between trait EI and humour styles.

Self-defeating humour sometimes showed no association with Sociability (TEIQue) [52], Self-control (TEIQue) [2], or Appraisal of Emotions [1]. Sociability involves adapting to new conditions, excellent social skills, and enhanced social relationships, which requires advanced EI skills and interpersonal capabilities. In contrast, self-defeating humour primarily serves intrapersonal functions and may reflect defensive denial, which is less likely to align with high Sociability. Self-control involves advanced competencies such as regulating external stress and controlling emotions to achieve long-term goals and maintain psychological well-being. High Self-control may mitigate extreme negative behaviours but does not necessarily prevent selfdefeating humour when used as a defence mechanism. Appraisal of Emotions reflects individuals' accuracy and sensitivity to emotional information. Wang et al. (2019) reported that accurate emotion perception was associated with adaptive humour but not clearly with reduced self-defeating humour [1]. However, Karahan et al. (2019) found a negative association between emotional appraisal and self-defeating humour [68]. Studies using TEIQue also found negative associations between self-defeating humour and EI components related to perception and understanding [2, 52]. These inconsistent results regarding the role of Appraisal of Emotions and the engagement of self-defeating humour might be due to the different settings (e.g., sampling in Asia or Europe, enrolment of university students or the general population) or variance in measures (e.g.

EIS or TEIQue). The different self-report measures showed commonalities but also some unique variance [28, 104], so their relationship with humour styles might vary. The mixed results regarding the EIS corresponded to the disagreement around the interpretation and representation of its four subscales. It was argued that the EIS is a singleconstruct measure [22] and that the factor structure is unstable [105]; thus, the results of subscales may differ in using the EIS. Furthermore, the inconsistent results regarding selfdefeating humour may be attributed to this HSQ subscale not fully capturing the concept, as outlined by Martin et al. (2003) [42]. For example, positive relationships were found between self-defeating humour and well-being indicators [106, 107], implying inconsistent relationships between EI and self-defeating humour.

For interpersonal humour, overall trait EI was positively correlated with affiliative humour and sometimes negatively associated with aggressive humour, indicating that individuals with higher trait EI tend to foster harmonious relationships [108] and avoid sarcasm or disparaging humour [109]. In line with the DET, better emotion knowledge and emotional skills encourages adaptive and flexible behaviours, such as humour [49]. Concerning the correlations between trait EI components and the two interpersonal humour styles, reasonable evidence was found between affiliative humour and trait EI components, although inconsistent results were found regarding aggressive humour.

Affiliative humour was correlated with all components of EI except for Self-control in Vernon et al.'s (2009) study using the TEIQue [52]. Affiliative humour is motivated by the desire to enhance interpersonal cohesiveness and foster relationships, thus pertaining more to social connectivity (interpersonal focus) than to internal emotion regulation and stress control (intrapersonal focus). Therefore, being capable of controlling one's desires and regulating stress may not enhance affiliative humour as a means of strengthening social connections.

Aggressive humour showed few and weak correlations with trait EI components. In the also found associations with Emotionality using the TEIQue [2, 52]. These inconsistent associations might be due to the heterogeneous correlations between empathy and aggressive behaviours. Meta-analyses found inconsistent relationships between affective empathy and aggression among children and adolescents [110], and little variances between empathy and aggression among adults [111]. The correlations between cognitive/affective empathy and reactive/proactive aggression possibly explain these mixed relationships. Reactive aggression, the spontaneous reaction to perceived threats, is commonly related to high emotional arousal and inadequate emotion regulation; proactive aggression refers to intentional and organised aggressive behaviours motivated by expected reward [112] [113] [114]. A recent empirical study among adolescents found negative correlations between cognitive/affective empathy and proactive aggression rather than reactive aggression, suggesting a weak correlation between reactive aggression and empathy [114]. Therefore, aggressive humour might involve more reactive than proactive aggressive behaviours, so is marginally associated with EI. However, some EI measures may be less likely to appropriately separate between cognitive and affective empathy, such that the associations between aggressive humour and EI components were inconsistent across different EI measures. It is unclear whether these adolescent findings generalise to wider populations, but they may help explain inconsistencies in aggressive humour and suggest further research in adults.

The closer associations with humour styles for trait EI relative to ability EI imply that, although advanced emotion knowledge and skills are important, high-ability EI may not be sufficient for conversion of such emotional abilities into emotionally intelligent (humorous) responses; individuals must have confidence in their emotional abilities and the tendency to use them in daily life, consistent with the health literature [102, 103].

4.4. The inconsistent relationship between EI and maladaptive humour styles

The correlations between EI components and maladaptive humour are less consistent than those with adaptive humour, although maladaptive styles are generally associated with lower overall EI. This may reflect the inconsistent correlations between maladaptive humour and indicators of psychological well-being and adjustment. Self-defeating humour is not always detrimental to well-being and may only partially reflect the characteristics outlined by Martin et al. (2003) [42], which could explain its inconsistent associations with EI. Aggressive humour shows limited associations with social adjustment [67] and psychological well-being in meta-analyses [11], making its weak correlation with EI unsurprising. In addition, individuals may use combinations of humour styles such that simple correlations tend to be misleading [115] [116] [117].

For example, a longitudinal study in children indicated that self-defeating humour was less detrimental to well-being when used in combination with other humour styles [117]. Aggressive humour, when used in combination with affiliative humour, may yield positive effects [117].

The inconsistent findings on maladaptive humour align with a laboratory study suggesting that positive, rather than negative, humour was a more effective form of emotion regulation [118]. Emotion regulation is a putative mechanism by which EI produces adaptive outcomes [103], which may underlie the closer association between EI and adaptive versus maladaptive humour. This might be explained by the effect of positive emotions elicited by adaptive humour tending to "undo" negative emotions [119] and link to effective emotional skills. Accordingly, the interaction between adaptive humour and emotional skills might be more effective than that involving maladaptive humour. This suggests that positive emotions may play a role in the relationship between EI and humour styles.

4.5. Underlying study biases

Trait EI shows a closer relationship with humour styles than ability EI, but as well as the theoretical differences between trait and ability EI, some underlying methodological biases should be considered. First, the results might be contaminated by common method biases [120].

Trait EI measures and the HSQ were used to assess individuals' typical performance in everyday situations, whereas the MSCEIT assesses individuals' maximal performance under certain circumstances, making it difficult to confirm the extent to which the correlations are due to a true relationship between EI and humour styles or the common method of measurement.

Furthermore, as only one of the studies focused on ability EI, conclusions should be made with caution.

Second, the psychometric properties of the measurements used should be considered. Both trait EI and humour styles show overlap with personality traits [42, 76]. A systematic study of the incremental validity of trait EI revealed that the TEIQue consistently accounts for additional variance in various criteria beyond that explained by personality traits and other related variables [81]. For ability EI, individual studies have demonstrated its incremental validity over intelligence and personality in predicting criteria related to positive relationships and adolescent mental health [121, 122]. However, inconsistent evidence suggests that the MSCEIT scores may not reflect the four-branch structure: the model without Emotional Facilitation fitted the data better [71]. Psychometric evidence for the HSQ is mixed. Its cross-cultural validity is supported by translations into over 30 languages and consistent replication of the four-factor structure across diverse cultural contexts [123] [124] [125] [126]. Criterion validity with respect to psychological well-being has been demonstrated in numerous studies [127, 128], and incremental validity beyond personality traits, well-being, and mood has also been reported [128, 129].

However, concerns remain regarding its construct validity. Heintz and Ruch [130] found that only self-defeating humour demonstrated satisfactory convergent validity. Although the divergent validity of aggressive and self-defeating styles was generally acceptable, affiliative and self-enhancing styles overlapped, raising concerns regarding their distinctiveness. A subsequent study using self-other agreement reported sufficient divergent validity for all styles, though convergent validity remained inadequate for self-defeating humour [131]. These findings suggest that the consequences of self-defeating humour may depend on contexts, and that the scale may not fully capture the construct. Similar results indicated that the variance in the self-enhancing and aggressive styles reflected both humour and non-humorous context, while the variance in self-defeating style was mainly driven by contextual factors [132]. This suggests that the associations between EI and the HSQ, particularly self-defeating humour, may have been confounded by item context. Heintz [133] found that the criterion validity with respect to daily humour behaviour was medium to large for affiliative and self-enhancing styles, but limited for aggressive and self-defeating styles. The weaker validity of maladaptive styles suggests that they may be less consistently expressed in everyday behaviour, possibly explaining their inconsistent associations with EI. A study using Item Response Theory identified several areas for improvement, including rating scale optimisation, subscale balance, item readability, and the refinement of poorly performing items [134].

Research on humour profiles suggests that the HSQ items may not adequately capture the motivational diversity underlying daily humour [135]. For example, the same humour style may serve interpersonal purposes for some, but self-focused aims for others [136]. A recent study combining the HSQ and CSM Questionnaire distinguished nine styles of humour [137], suggesting that personality may encompass additional styles of humour which are not identified by the HSQ.

4.6. Strengths and limitations

The current study is the first systematic review to synthesise the evidence for a relationship between EI and humour styles. This review was conducted in a rigorous way with screening, data extraction, and quality appraisal conducted independently by two authors. The findings contribute to our understanding of how ability EI and trait EI relate to humour styles by synthesising existing evidence, which may help to improve psychological well-being.

Furthermore, this study contributes to our understanding of the associations between specific EI components and humour styles, which may benefit fundamental research on emotion and personality psychology.

However, several limitations should be noted. First, all reviewed studies were crosssectional, limiting causal inference and failing to capture changes over time. Second, all studies used convenience samples, including psychology students, whose academic background may have influenced their questionnaire responses. Third, as community rather than clinical samples were used, it remains unclear how EI and humour styles relate to clinical conditions such as depression or anxiety. In addition, none of the studies included nationally representative samples, limiting cultural heterogeneity.

A further limitation is that a systematic psychometric review was not conducted, as this was beyond the scope of the present work and would require a separate protocol.

Furthermore,due to substantial heterogeneity in measurement tools, subscale structures, and reporting formats, along with the limited number of studies per instrument and overall, a quantitative synthesis was considered statistically unfeasible and methodologically problematic.

Additional variation in sample characteristics (e.g., twin samples, psychology students) and research contexts (e.g., Western vs. Eastern populations) may further obscure meaningful differences in how EI relates to humour styles.

5. Conclusions and implications

This systematic review has demonstrated a relative paucity of research on the association between EI and humour styles, particularly regarding ability EI and the indirect relationships between these constructs. Further research is needed to enable future meta-analyses and to strengthen the empirical base for understanding both the interaction between EI and humour styles and the individual constructs. Additionally, EI and humour may be linked through positive emotions, which future research could explore as a potential mediator.
