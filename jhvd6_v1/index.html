Reporting p values imprecisely (e.g., p < .05) reduces transparency, reproducibility, and re-use (e.g., in p value meta-analyses). Best practice is to report exact p-values with three decimal places (e.g., p = .032) unless p values are smaller than 0.001, in which case you can use p < .001.

What we found

We found 4 non-significant p values that should be checked for appropriate interpretation.

Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

Metacheck does not yet analyze automatically whether sentences which include non-significant p-values are correct, but we recommend manually checking the sentences below for possible misinterpreted non-significant p values.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. Abstract

Previous studies have found that the engagement of domain-general cognitive control facilitates the solution of sentence ambiguities. This web-based behavioral study investigated the interplay between general cognitive control and linguistic processing. We examined the interactions between proactive control and processing of English wh-dependencies. We reasoned that if processing English wh-dependencies engages proactive control, we should observe differences in reaction times and/or accuracy during processing when proactive control is exercised to different extents.

To this end, we manipulated participants' proactive control mode by asking them to perform one of the two variants (less vs. more proactive versions) of the AX-Continuous Performance Task (AX-CPT), right after which they read English wh-dependency sentences in a self-paced manner. We also manipulated the gap type (filled vs. control) of the English wh-dependency sentences: filled gaps induce parsing disruptions while control gaps do not.

Our AX-CPT results revealed that participants were in distinct proactive control modes under our manipulation; however, such differences did not modulate the subsequent sentence reading task. We did observe a filled gap effect at the subject position, in contrast to previous studies. This suggests that our participants were more proactive than those in prior studies, possibly due to the proactivity manipulation task.

Proactive Control and the Processing of English Wh-Dependencies: A Web-Based

Behavioral Study

Language processing has been hypothesized to interact with domain-general cognitive control processes, such as memory, attention, and inhibitory control involved in conflict resolution. Evidence can be found in clinical research of aphasic patients (Brownsett et al., 2014; Schumacher et al., 2019; Stefaniak et al., 2022) and in experimental studies in lab settings (Goral et al., 2011; Gordon et al., 2001; Hsu et al., 2021; Hussey et al., 2017; Novick et al., 2014; Traxler et al., 2005). These studies suggest that the engagement of domaingeneral cognitive control can benefit recovery from aphasia or facilitate the process of resolving sentence ambiguities. For instance, Novick et al. (2014) and follow-up studies from their team (Hsu et al., 2021; Hsu & Novick, 2016; Hussey et al., 2017) have consistently found that participants' performance on correctly re-interpreting ambiguous sentences improves after engaging in domain-general cognitive control.

Prior studies focused on how domain-general cognitive control, specifically, inhibition involved in conflict resolution, can facilitate sentence disambiguation and re-interpretation. In the current study, we examined the issue from a different perspective by looking at the dynamic interaction between domain-general proactive control and the processing of English wh-dependencies. In particular, we looked into whether and how exercising domain-general proactive control to different extents can modulate the processing of English whdependencies. We were specifically interested in knowing whether participants would employ different strategies to process English wh-dependencies when they are biased to be in different proactivity modes. A preview of the results suggested that we successfully manipulated participants' proactive control mode, but did not find differences between the more and less proactive groups in a subsequent reading task. In contrast to prior studies on wh-dependencies, we observed a prominent filled gap effect at the subject rather than the object position. We speculatively ascribed this to our participants being more proactive after being in the proactivity manipulation task.

English Wh-Dependency

Wh-dependencies are among the most commonly investigated structures in psycholinguistics and have been extensively investigated across different languages, such as English (Kaan et al., 2000; Phillips et al., 2005; Stowe, 1986; Stowe et al., 1991; Wagers & Phillips, 2014), German (Felser et al., 2003; Fiebach et al., 2001 Fiebach et al., , 2002)), Japanese (Aoshima et al., 2004; Ueno & Kluender, 2009), and Mandarin (Hsiao & Gibson, 2003; Xiang et al., 2014 Xiang et al., , 2015)). A wh-dependency sentence typically contains a wh-word that must be associated with another word or syntactic position (a.k.a. argument assigner) to obtain the full interpretation. In English wh-dependencies, the wh-word usually appears in a clause-initial position and the wh-word and its argument assigner can be adjacent or span multiple words.

For instance, to obtain the full interpretation of the sentence in (1), the language processor must associate the wh-word (who) with the following verb (met/meet) that the wh-word can be an argument of. Such association can be represented as a filler-gap relation (i.e., the whfiller is filling the empty subject position in (1a), and the object position in (1b)).

The Processing of English Wh-Dependencies

A robust finding with respect to the processing of wh-dependencies in English relative clauses is that subject-extracted relative clauses (SRCs) are easier to process than objectextracted relative clauses (ORCs); see (2) for examples. This is reflected by, for instance, faster reading times in SRCs compared to ORCs (e.g., Gibson et al., 2005; Gordon et al., 2001; Staub, 2010; Traxler et al., 2002). This difference between the processing of SRCs and ORCs has evinced great interest in psycholinguistics and many theories have been proposed to account for the phenomenon (for a review, see Gordon and Lowder (2012)). These theories can be generally categorized into (but not limited to) three types: (i) frequency/exposurebased models, (ii) semantics/pragmatics-based models, and (iii) memory/resource-based models.

(2) a. subject-extracted relative clause (SRC)

The senator [whoi __i bothered the reporter] caused a big scandal. b. object-extracted relative clause (ORC)

The senator [whoj the reporter bothered __j] caused a big scandal.

The frequency/exposure-based models propose that frequency of exposure plays a critical role in language processing (e.g., Diessel & Hilpert, 2016). The essence of this approach is that higher frequency of exposure leads to easier processing, and vice versa. Corpus studies have found that SRCs outnumber ORCs in English, which can account for the easier processing of SRCs than ORCs in English (Gordon & Hendrick, 2005; Roland et al., 2007).

Fine-grained distributional information of the nouns in the relative clause of SRCs and ORCs has also been investigated and further supports the crucial role of frequency of exposure in language processing (Reali & Christiansen, 2007b , 2007a).

The semantics/pragmatics-based models claim that semantic/pragmatic information can modulate language processing. In processing SRCs and ORCs, SRCs are claimed to be more straightforward in meaning than their ORC counterparts, thus resulting in easier processing of SRCs than ORCs (e.g., Gordon & Lowder, 2012). Interestingly, the processing difficulty in ORCs can be reduced if pragmatic cues are provided, suggesting pragmatic/semantic cues play an important role in language processing (e.g., King & Just, 1991). Besides, perspective shifting at the discourse level has also been argued to support the semantics/pragmatics-based approach, as processing difficulties can be modulated in response to perspective shifting during parsing (MacWhinney, 1977 (MacWhinney, , 1982;; MacWhinney & Pléh, 1988).

Memory/resource-based approaches, on which we based the present study, propose that memory systems and cognitive resources play a critical role in language processing (e.g., Ness & Meltzer-Asscher, 2017; Van Dyke, 2007; Van Dyke & Lewis, 2003; Van Dyke & McElree, 2011). In the case of English wh-dependencies, it is argued that when our language parser encounters the wh-word, the wh-word will stay active in working memory until it can be integrated with its argument assigner downstream. Proposals differ as to whether the cost of processing wh-dependencies is due to costs associated with the storage of the wh-word, and/or to interference when the wh-word needs to be retrieved (e.g., Ness & Meltzer-Asscher, 2017 , 2019; Van Dyke, 2007; Vasishth et al., 2019). As for storage, the longer the wh-word stays in working memory, the more cognitive costs our language parser incurs for the maintenance (e.g., Chen et al., 2005; Ness & Meltzer-Asscher, 2017 , 2019; Qian & Packard, 2022). In terms of retrieval, when more information is introduced before the wh-word can be integrated with its argument assigner, the information will interfere with the wh-word in working memory, resulting in a greater retrieval difficulty of the wh-word for integration (e.g., Van Dyke, 2007; Van Dyke & Lewis, 2003; Van Dyke & McElree, 2006; Vasishth et al., 2019). Memory/resource-based models can therefore account for the relatively easier processing of SRCs than ORCs, in that the distance between the wh-word and its argument assigner is shorter in SRCs than in ORCs, thus less cognitively demanding for wh-word retrieval and storage. Less information will thus be introduced and interfere with the wh-word in SRCs as compared with ORCs.

According to the Active Filler Strategy (AFS) (Frazier & Flores d'Arcais, 1989) and Syntactic Prediction Locality Theory (SPLT) (Gibson, 1998), both examples of memory/resource-based approaches, our language parser postulates potential gaps once it encounters a wh-word in wh-dependencies but always favors the closest gap among all the potential gaps in order to minimize cognitive costs of maintaining the wh-word. Based on the proposal of AFS and SPLT, an interesting question can be asked as to what will happen if the closest gap is already occupied. Stowe (1986) conducted a study that probed into this issue and found that parsing was temporarily disrupted at the closest gap when it was unexpectedly filled, which is known as the filled gap effect (at the object position). When the parsing disruption occurs, the parser needs to undo the initial anticipation of the closest gap and search for another gap that the wh-word can fill, thus resulting in longer processing time; see Thus far we have mainly discussed what happens at the object gap. In principle, the whword can also fill the subject gap of the embedded clause, as is occupied by the embedded subject (Ruth) in (3a) and (3b). Some might thus argue that this subject gap is preferred over the object gap and predict a parsing disruption at the subject gap (e.g., Ruth in (3a) and (3b) vs. Ruth in (3c)) based on the memory/resource-based models, as the integration distance is shorter for the subject gap than the object gap (e.g., De Vincenzi, 1991; Frazier & Flores d'Arcais, 1989; Kwon et al., 2006; Stowe, 1986). While Stowe (1986) did not observe parsing disruptions at the subject gap, some studies have observed parsing disruptions at the subject gap (e.g., Juffs & Harrington, 1995; Lee, 2004; Tollan & Palaz, 2021; Wagers & Pendleton, 2016). Interestingly, in these studies, parsing disruptions at the subject gap were observed only when the wh-word and the embedded subject were not adjacent to each other and were separated by multiple words (e.g., Alice asked whoi, after the party was over, Kate could drive grandad home to __i.). The finding of a filled subject gap effect with multipleword interventions between the wh-word and the subject gap can have intriguing theoretical implications for the processing of English wh-dependencies. Some suggest that the processing of subject vs. object gaps is inherently different (Stowe, 1986). Others suggest that postulating a gap upon encountering a wh-word takes time to process but undergoes the same processes between subject and object gaps and that the preference of the closest gap (i.e., the subject gap) in processing English wh-dependencies can hold true when given sufficient parsing time (e.g., Tollan & Palaz, 2021; Wagers & Pendleton, 2016).

In addition to time, other factors may determine a reader's sensitivity to filled subject and object gaps. For instance, would individuals who tend to be more pro-active and anticipate more actively be more likely to show filled gap effects than readers who are anticipating less actively? The present study aims to bias people to be in different proactivity modes, and investigate the effect on processing wh-dependencies, looking both at filled gap effect at the object position, and at subject position.

Domain-General Cognitive Control: Proactive Control and Reactive Control

Cognitive control mediates how one manages thoughts and behaviors to achieve the internal goals. A widely held assumption is that cognitive control is domain-general and encompasses different underlying mechanisms, such as memory systems, attention, and inhibitory control. One influential approach is the dual mechanisms of control (DMC) framework, which divides cognitive control into two main mechanisms: proactive control and reactive control (Braver, 2012). Proactive control refers to the sustained maintenance of encountered information in working memory. It is cognitively more demanding as information stays active throughout. Reactive control, on the other hand, refers to the transient reactivation of previous information in memory upon encountering certain cues. It is cognitively less demanding as information is activated only when cues are encountered.

These two types of control are not mutually exclusive (Mäki-Marttunen et al., 2019), and individuals can employ them, and shift between them, to different extents in different contexts (Gonthier et al., 2016).

To measure the use and shift between proactive and reactive control, the AX-continuous performance task (AX-CPT) has often been utilized (e.g., Gonthier et al., 2016; Morales et al., 2013 Morales et al., , 2015)). A standard AX-CPT is composed of four types of cue-probe pairs: A-X, A-Y, B-X, and B-Y, where A and X are constant letters while B and Y are variables that can be any letter except for A and X. The cue-probe pairs are often separated by 3 intervening letters in a standard AX-CPT. Participants are instructed to respond to the probe as quickly and accurately as possible via button press. When cue A is followed by probe X, participants have to press one of the two buttons upon seeing probe X. In other cue-probe pairs (A-Y, B-X, B-Y), participants have to press the other button upon seeing the probe. The A-X pair usually accounts for most of the trials (e.g., 70% of the total trials) while the rest cue-probe pairs take up and share a relatively small percentage of the trials (e.g., 10% for A-Y, B-X, and B-Y, respectively). This disproportionate distribution of the A-X pair vs. other cue-probe pairs aims to bias participants to be proactive, as they are likely to anticipate probe X upon seeing cue A under the more frequent occurrence of the AX condition. Depending on the experimental goal, different variants of the AX-CPT have been used (e.g., changing the distributional proportion of the four conditions, or not having distractor letters between the cue and target) (e.g., Gonthier et al., 2016).

Among the four conditions in the AX-CPT, the AY and BX conditions are particularly important to examine the proactivity and reactivity bias. If one is proactive, one will be likely to make more errors and/or have longer reaction times (RTs) in the AY condition and fewer errors and/or have shorter RTs in the BX condition. This is because upon seeing cue A in the AY condition, proactive people will anticipate an upcoming probe X and get ready to respond. When the probe turns out to be the unexpected Y, proactive people will likely make mistakes and/or show longer reaction times. Upon seeing cue B in the BX condition, proactive people will already be prepared to make the correct response, resulting in fewer errors and/or shorter RTs in the BX condition. A reversed pattern is expected if people are reactive (i.e., fewer error and/or shorter RTs in the AY condition and more errors and/or longer RTs in the BX condition). That is, upon seeing cue A in the AY condition, reactive people will less likely expect the upcoming probe. They will respond to probe Y when they encounter the probe, thus resulting in fewer mistakes and/or shorter RTs than their proactive counterparts. However, when reactive people encounter probe X in the BX condition, this probe X will reactivate the cues in both AX and BX conditions, thus causing interference and resulting in more errors and/or longer RTs. Therefore, in an AX-CPT, performance on the AY and BX conditions is usually examined to evaluate one's proactivity and/or reactivity. The proactive behavioral index (PBI) is often utilized to quantify one's tendency of being proactive or reactive and is obtained with the following equation, using RTs and/or error rates as inputs: (AY-BX)/(AY+BX) (Gonthier et al., 2016). A positive PBI indicates more influence (e.g., more errors, longer reaction times) from the AY condition, thus (more) proactive; a negative PBI, on the other hand, means more influence from the BX condition, thus reactive or less proactive. The present study followed Gonthier et al. (2016)'s design of the AX-CPT, which is a variant of a standard AX-CPT; see Methods for details.

Connecting English Wh-Dependency and Cognitive Control

An interesting but rarely explored question, which we address in this study, is whether and how proactivity/reactivity affects the processing of wh-dependencies. According to the DMC framework, proactive and reactive control operations rely on memory systems to maintain or reactivate encountered information (Braver, 2012). These two types of control can also work independently and can be flexibly exercised to different extents in response to different contextual demands (Gonthier et al., 2016; Mäki-Marttunen et al., 2019). Based on the prediction-based approach to the processing of English wh-dependencies (e.g., Gibson, 1998), if maintaining a wh-filler and postulating wh-gaps engages domain-general proactive control, then changes in proactive control can potentially modulate such parsing. In the present study, we specifically examine such modulation, if any, from domain-general proactive control to the processing of English wh-dependencies.

Our research question is: can exercising proactive control to different extent modulate the processing of the filled gap in English wh-dependencies? If so, how? We will first examine the filled gap effect at the object position. We will also look at the filled gap at the subject position to further investigate if our proactivity manipulation might play a role. Our predictions are as follows. First, concerning the filled object gap effect, when people are biased to be more proactive (by the AX-CPT in the current study), thus employing proactive control to a greater extent, they will be more likely to expect an object gap in processing English wh-dependencies. When the closest object gap is unexpectedly filled, it will take longer for more proactive people to undo the initial anticipation of this gap and reanalyze the sentence. On the other hand, when people are biased to be less proactive under experimental manipulation, the expectation of the closest object gap will be reduced. The unexpectedly filled object gap at the closest gap site will thus be not as much surprising to less proactive people. They will therefore need less time to recover from the unexpectedness, as compared with their more proactive counterparts. As for the gap at the subject position, we predict that people in a more proactive mode will elicit a larger filled subject gap effect, compared to their less proactive counterparts. The same reasoning of the filled object gap applies to the processing of the subject gap. As the filled subject gap effect is relatively inconsistent in previous studies, if any modulation can be observed, it would add to the literature the potential interactions between parsing strategies and language-specific processing.

Methods

Participants

The experiment was conducted over the internet. Two hundred and twelve participants were recruited on Prolific (http://www.prolific.co/) and performed the experimental tasks using FindingFive platform (FindingFive Team, 2019). Our pre-screening criteria on Prolific selected monolingually-raised English speakers of 18 to 35 years of age without any language-related and vision-related disorder. They were also required to reside in America in the pre-selection criteria and use desktop computers with physical keyboards for the study.

Seventy-four participants were excluded for further analyses as they (1) self-reported being proficient in other language(s) in addition to English, per a brief language background survey, (2) self-reported having (a) language-related disorder(s), (3) obtained low accuracy rates (<80%) of true/false statement verification questions, and (4) obtained low accuracy rates (<60%) per condition (AX, AY, BX, BY) in the AX-CPT, following the criteria in Gonthier et al. (2016). The other 138 participants whose data sets were included in the analyses selfreported being functionally monolingual native English speakers with no or rather limited knowledge of any other language(s). The data analyzed contained 72 datasets in the more proactive group (49 females, 20 males, 3 non-binary, age=18-35 years, mean age=25.2), and 66 data sets in the less proactive group (51 females, 13 males, 2 non-binary, age=18-35 years, mean age=25.3). Educational background was also comparable between the more proactive group (6 with below the college degree, 66 with the college or above degree) and the less proactive group (7 with below college degree, 59 with the college or above degree). All participants gave informed consent prior to the experiment (University of Florida Institutional Review Board Protocol IRB202102007) and received monetary compensation ($12 USD) upon completion.

Materials and Data Analysis

The study consisted of two consecutive tasks (i.e., the AX-CPT and sentence reading task) to investigate whether and how domain-general proactive control modulates the processing of English wh-dependencies. Each participant participated in only one version of the experiment and could do the tasks only once. Participants were randomly assigned to either the more proactive or the less proactive version of the AX-CPT. After that, all participants did the same sentence reading task (with list assignments counterbalanced).

Prior to the experiment, participants first read the introduction of the study and gave informed consent on the webpage to agree to participate in the study. Then, they were asked to read and follow the instructions carefully for the AX-CPT and sentence reading task, which they would perform consecutively in the experiment. After that, they completed some practice trials (8 practice trials for the AX-CPT, 6 practice trials for the sentence reading task) to familiarize themselves with both tasks before the formal experiment. During the formal experiment, participants first completed the AX-CPT, right after which they did the sentence reading task. We did not give instruction reminders between these two tasks so as to facilitate and maximize the intended spillover of the proactivity mode from the AX-CPT onto the reading task. Upon completing the sentence reading task, participants filled out a brief demographic survey and a language background questionnaire, adapted and simplified from LEAP-Q (Marian et al., 2007). The study was about an hour long, including breaks. Our experimental materials, analysis scripts, data, and supplementary materials can be found in https://u.pcloud.link/publink/show?code=kZ9vyfJZskzEJ2qD2TBbI51cxcqGl89bqkc7.

The AX-CPT Materials. For the AX-CPT, we followed Gonthier et al. (2016)'s design and created two versions (more proactive, less proactive) that aimed to bias participants to be in different proactivity modes. A total of 200 cue-probe pairs were created and distributed across the A-X, A-Y, B-X and B-Y conditions for both versions of the AX-CPT (80 for A-X, 20 for A-Y, 20 for B-X, 80 for B-Y). Note again that A and X were constant letters while B and Y were variable letters that could be any letter except for A and X 1 . Participants had to respond to the probe as quickly and accurately as possible during the experiment. When cue A was followed by probe X, participants had to press button J upon seeing the probe. In other cue-probe combinations (i.e., A-Y, B-X, B-Y), participants had to press button F. The more proactive and less proactive versions of the AX-CPT differed in the following aspects. In the more proactive version, participants were additionally instructed that cue A was very likely to be followed by probe X, which aimed to bias them to exercise proactive control to a greater extent. In the less proactive version, additional cue-digit pairs (e.g., A-3, B-6) were introduced in the experiment (20 pairs for A-digit, 20 pairs for B-digit), with the digit randomly selected from 1 to 9. Participants had to withhold their responses when the probe was a digit and they were not given the additional instructions as in the more proactive version. The less proactive version was therefore expected to bias participants to exercise proactive control to a less extent. Analysis. Analysis of the AX-CPT and reading data was carried out in R version 4.4.3 (R Core Team, 2025) and RStudio (Posit team, 2024). For the AX-CPT analysis, reaction times and responses to the probes in the AX, AY, BX, and BY conditions were analyzed. Only trials with correct responses entered the RT analysis. Unusually short RTs below 100 ms were removed, which accounted for 0.2% of the datapoints. Since there was a 1000 ms time-out at the probe frame, we did not further cut off slow responses at the probe 2 . Since the raw RTs had a right-skewed distribution, we carried out a box-cox transformation to better meet the normal distribution assumption. Specifically, we used the BoxCox() function in the forecast package (Hyndman & Khandakar, 2008) for box-cox transformation with the "loglik" argument. As for the error analysis, all responses to the probes entered the analysis with correct responses coded 0 and incorrect responses coded 1. In addition to the RT and error analyses, we calculated the proactive behavioral index (PBI) for both RTs and errors to further examine the participants' tendency of proactivity, following (Gonthier et al., 2016).

Specifically, we utilized the averaged RTs and error rates of the AY and BX conditions for each individual to calculate the PBIs under the equation of (AY-BX)/(AY+BX) 3 . A positive PBI reflects more difficulty in AY trials, thus a more proactive mode; a negative PBI, on the other hand, reflects more difficulty in BX trials, thus a reactive and/or less proactive mode.

Reaction times were examined using lmer with the box-cox transformed RTs as the dependent variable; the errors were examined using logistic glmer with the (in)correct responses as the dependent variable (correct responses coded 0, incorrect responses coded 1). The Sentence Reading Task Materials. As for the sentence materials, we manipulated the gap type (filled gap, control gap) in English wh-dependencies. A total of 40 experimental item sets (average length=14.1 words) were created of two conditions each as illustrated in (4); for a complete set of materials, see https://u.pcloud.link/publink/show?code=kZ9vyfJZskzEJ2qD2TBbI51cxcqGl89bqkc7. Each sentence contained an embedded relative clause in which the relative pronoun (which) was followed by a 2-word subject (the teenager), a predominantly transitive verb (hit), and a 2word noun phrase (the window) as the object of the verb. In the filled gap condition (4a), the relative clause start with which, which is the object of the preposition (with) downstream; in the control gap condition (4b), the wh-phrase is a prepositional phrase (with which). The predominantly transitive verbs were selected from Lapata et al. (2001) and confirmed by native English speakers in the lab. Each item-pair had a different verb. The selection of the predominantly transitive verbs aimed to prompt the parser to associate the wh-word with the verb, thus maximizing the filled gap effect (at the object position). There were at least 4 words (was soft and relatively light) before the end of the sentence in the main clause; see (4a-b). (4) a. filled gap condition: The ball [which]1 filler [the teenager]2 [hit]3 [the window]4 with ___ gap was soft and relatively light. b. control gap condition: The ball [with which]1 filler [the teenager]2 [hit]3 [the window]4 ___ gap was soft and relatively light.

Conditions (4a) and ( 4b) contained identical words but differed in the ambiguity of gap postulation. In (4a), two potential gaps (serving as the object argument) could be postulated upon encountering the wh-word (which): a gap after the verb (hit) vs. the preposition (with).

The closest gap right after the verb was favored to minimize the processing cost. As for (4b), the displaced wh-phrase (with which) could disambiguate the gap, thus preventing the parser from postulating a gap after the verb (hit). Therefore, a difference in RTs was expected at the object position (the window) right after the verb (hit) between ( 4a) and ( 4b).

Twenty distractor sentences (average length=14.1 words) were created with a comparable length to the experimental sentences. These items served to distract participants from the wh-dependency structure and therefore did not contain the wh-dependency relation as in the experimental sentences (e.g., The couple applied several layers of sunscreen before heading to the sandy beach.). Thirty true/false statement verification questions were added and served to check whether participants were attentive during the experiment (e.g., The teenager hit the window with a soft ball; 10 true/false statement verification questions each for filled gap, control gap, and distractor sentences).

The 40 experimental sentence pairs were counterbalanced across participants such that each item appeared only in one of the two conditions (i.e., filled vs. control gap condition) within a list and appeared in the other condition in the other list. Each participant read each item only once during the experiment. The distractor sentences were identical across the participants. All the sentences were presented in a randomized order during an experiment.

Procedure. In the sentence reading task, the self-paced reading paradigm was implemented, in which participants read a sentence word-by-word at a self-paced rate by pressing the space bar. A sentence contour was presented and a former word would be masked as the sentence was disclosed incrementally word-by-word (non-cumulative moving window display). Some sentences were followed by a true/false statement verification question that was presented in a full sentence as attention check. Participants had to press button J (true) or F (false) in response to the questions. RTs of each word in a sentence and (in)correct responses to the true/false statement verification questions were recorded in the sentence reading task. Two 40-second breaks were evenly distributed in the sentence reading task and participants could choose to proceed before the 40-second time-out by buttonpressing.

Analysis.

For the analysis of the self-paced reading times, we first removed unusually short and long responses (below 100 ms or above 2000 ms) (Fine & Jaeger, 2016; Prasad & Linzen, 2021). After that, for each participant, response time (RT) outliers were removed based on the criterion of their mean +/-2.5 standard deviations. This affected 4.9% of the data points. The raw RT distribution of the sentence reading task was right skewed. Therefore, we applied the box-cox transformation to the raw RTs to better meet the normal distribution requirement in the same way as the RT transformation in the AX-CPT.

For the statistical analysis of the sentence reading data, we used a linear mixed-effects model to examine the dependent variable of box-cox transformed RTs. Our independent variables were the within-participant factor of gap type (control gap, filled gap) and the between-participant factor of proactivity (less proactive, more proactive), both of which were sum-coded (control gap: -0.5, filled gap: 0.5; less proactive: -0.5, more proactive: 0.5). Our word frames of interest were shown in the subscripts in (4) above, which included the (i) wh-word1/wh-phrase1, (ii) embedded subject2, (iii) target verb3, (iv) critical noun phrase4foot_0 . The RTs of the words of interest were averaged for the wh-phrase in (i), the embedded subject in (ii), and the critical noun phrase in (iv). Our models started out with the maximal randomeffects structure that included by-participant and by-item intercepts and gap type as the byparticipant and by-item random slopes. The bobyqa optimizer was applied. Model reduction was carried out in the same way as in the AX-CPT until the model converged. The p-values were obtained by the lmerTest package. Effects were considered statistically significant if the p-value was smaller than 0.05.

Discussion

The present web-based study investigated whether and how exercising domain-general proactive control to different extents could modulate the processing of English whdependencies, focusing on the filled gap site. While domain-general cognitive control operations have been found to be engaged in language-specific processing in previous studies, we hoped to further contribute to the literature the dynamic modulations between these two distinct domains. To this end, we created two versions (less proactive vs. more proactive) of the AX-CPT in order to bias participants to exercise proactive control to different extents. Participants read English wh-dependency sentences right after they performed the AX-CPT. If the participant's proactivity mode carries over to the sentence reading task, and if processing wh-dependencies shares processes with the AX-CPT, we expected the processing of the wh-dependency, especially with respect to filled gap effects, to be modulated by the version of the prior AX-CPT. Our expectations were not fully born out based on the results. First, even though our AX-CPT manipulation was successful in inducing differences in the proactivity mode between the two groups, we failed to find differences between the groups in the sentence reading task. Second, we did not replicate the filled gap effect at the object position (the window4), but did find an effect at the subject position (the teenager2) following the wh-word/wh-phrase (which1/with which1). Below, we first discuss the AX-CPT. Next, we discuss the findings and implications of the sentence reading task results.

The AX-CPT

In the AX-CPT, a group difference (less proactive vs. more proactive) that reached statistical significance was observed in both RT and error analyses. The group exposed to the proactive version of the task had shorter RTs and fewer errors than the group exposed to the less proactive version. This could not be a speed-accuracy tradeoff as shorter RTs did not lead to more error responses, and vice versa. Besides, the more proactive group had a larger PBI in both RT and error response analyses. The results suggested that these two groups indeed behaved differently under our experimental manipulation of two versions of the AX-CPT.

Across the groups, most errors were made in the AY condition, which suggests an overall proactive tendency of the participants.

To recap, our AX-CPT results showed that we successfully biased participants to be in different proactivity modes (less proactive vs. more proactive) under our experimental manipulation, replicating similar patterns in Gonthier et al. (2016). The results also supported that participants could flexibly employ domain-general proactive control to different extents in response to different contextual demands, confirming cognitive fluidity observed in Gonthier et al. (2016) and Mäki-Marttunen et al. (2019). Besides, the observed pattern between the AX-CPT condition and proactivity group suggested that the more proactive group indeed exercised domain-general proactive control to a greater extent, as reflected by longer RTs and more errors in the AY (vs. BX) condition. Interestingly, though distinct behavioral patterns were observed between the less and more proactive groups, the less proactive group seemed to still reveal a tendency of being proactive (but to a significantly less extent than the more proactive counterpart), as reflected by longer RTs in the AY (vs. BX) condition. This may be due to the age of the participants. Previous studies report that young adults tend to be proactive, as compared with young children or older adults (Bugg, 2014; Karayanidis et al., 2011; Kopp et al., 2014; Van Gerven et al., 2016). Van Gerven et al. (2016) observed a U-shape pattern, which reflects proactivity shifting over the lifespan. In particular, people generally tend to be proactive from early adulthood (18-25 years of age) to late adulthood (~60 years of age); they tend to be reactive outside these periods. The participants in our study (18-35 years old) fell into this proactive age range. Hence, their overall proactive tendency is not surprising.

The Sentence Reading Task

In the sentence reading task, we did not observe any difference in RTs at the target verb (hit3) as a function of gap type, proactivity group or the interaction between them. We also did not observe any differences at the filled object gap site (the window4), thus not replicating the filled (object) gap effect (Stowe, 1986). However, we did observe a filled subject gap effect that appeared at the earlier word frames of interests. Specifically, this filled subject gap effect appeared at the embedded subject (the teenager2). The observed filled subject gap supports the AFS (Frazier & Flores d'Arcais, 1989) and SPLT (Gibson, 1998) which claim that the closest gap (subject gap vs. object gap) is favored to minimize the processing costs.

We proposed three potential explanations that can account for the observed filled subject gap effect in the present study as well as some possibilities of why no modulations were observed between proactive control operations and the processing of English wh-dependencies. First, the observed filled subject gap in the present study might result from a generational shift in participants' overall proactivity tendency (less proactive or more proactive), as the filled subject gap effect seems to be observed more often in later time (e.g., Tollan & Palaz, 2021; Wagers & Pendleton, 2016; Yan & Liu, 2021). Contemporary young adults may have different sentence processing and/or cognitive control strategies than young adults 20 years ago, perhaps driven by changes in means of communication and communication style, such as brief messages on social media. Technology has been found to affect cognition in various ways (e.g., Cardoso-Leite et al., 2016; Cudo et al., 2019; Zhang & Xiang, 2023). To what extent and in what way technology modulates cognitive control and potentially language processing are beyond the scope of present study but is worth exploring for future studies.

Second, it might also be possible that participants' proactivity was boosted after performing the AX-CPT in the present study, regardless of the version of the AX-CPT task they received. If so, they may have been more likely to anticipate a subject gap during parsing, giving rise to the filled subject gap effect. This explanation could be tested by conducting a similar study as the current one, but directly comparing the reading results from participants who received the AX-CPT before the reading task with results from those who instead performed a task that does not involve proactive control.

Third, we speculated that the filled subject gap effect might stem from the difference between the argument-related wh-word (which1) and the adjunct-related wh-phrase (with which1). Specifically, the argument-related which1 can function as the subject of the embedded clause. Since the subject gap is the closest potential gap in the sentence, encountering an unexpectedly filled subject gap likely disrupted parsing, resulting in the observed filled subject gap effect at the critical noun phrase (the teenager2). In contrast, the adjunct-related wh-phrase (with which1) can never serve as the subject of the embedded clause. As a result, no processing disruption would occur when the parser encountered the embedded subject, which is obligatory for grammatical structure formation. Thus, the filled subject gap effect can be attributed to the distinction between the argument-related wh-word and the adjunct-related wh-phrase. Furthermore, we speculated that the absence of a filled object gap effect might be influenced by the parsing disruption caused by the filled subject gap. The filled subject gap (the teenager2) and the filled object gap (the window4) were in close proximity, separated only by the target verb (hit3). More time might have been required for the parser to detect the filled object gap, especially after recovering from the parsing disruption at the subject gap. This recovery was reflected in the absence of a spillover effect at the target verb. Given the short distance between the filled subject and object gaps, the parser may have been unable to register the effect at the object gap, leading to the absence of the filled object gap effect.

Future studies could explore this further by manipulating the distance between the filled subject and object gaps.

As to our failure to observe the modulation of the AX-CPT manipulation on sentence reading, it might be that such modulation effect was too subtle to be easily captured in a behavioral experiment. An electroencephalography (EEG) study may provide deeper insights into the differences at the neural level (Lin & Kaan, 2025). In fact, neural-level modulation has already been observed (Lin & Kaan, 2025). In addition, the effect of our proactivity manipulation may have been short-lasting and may not have carried over to the sentence reading task at the behavioral level. An interleaved or trial-by-trial experiment design (e.g., Ness et al., 2021) of mixing the AX-CPT and sentence reading trials within a block might be more suitable to test carry-over effects of the cognitive control task onto the reading task. Furthermore, as individuals in early adulthood (18-25 years of age) to late adulthood (~60 years of age) have been found to be inherently proactive (Van Gerven et al., 2016), it might be relatively difficult to suppress their tendency of being proactive (i.e., turning them into being less proactive) and maintain such suppression. Even though our proactivity manipulation worked between the two groups in the present study, its effect may not have persisted until the sentence reading task. Therefore, recruiting participants around the proactivity shifting boundaries (e.g., 14-16 years of age) may help capture such changes and obtain the modulation effects in the behavioral results, if these exist. Further investigations are thus required in future studies.

Conclusions

To briefly summarize the present study, our goal was to explore whether and how domain-general proactive control could interact with and modulate the language-specific processing of English wh-dependencies, focusing on the filled gap effect at the object position and the filled gap effect at the subject site. We employed two versions (less proactive, more proactive) of the AX-CPT and successfully biased people to exercise proactive control to different extents. However, we failed to observe our predicted modulations between proactive control and the processing of English wh-dependencies but the modulations have been observed at the neural level (Lin & Kaan, 2025). We also failed to find a filled gap effect at the object position, but did observe the filled gap effect at the subject site. We proposed three potential explanations and suggested possible improvements for the current study design. Though our predictions were not born out, our study could still contribute to the investigation of the dynamic interaction between domain-general proactive control and language-specific processing of English wh-dependencies. The observed patterns of the AX-CPT in the present study confirmed that domain-general cognitive control can be fluid and dynamic in response to the external contextual demands. The filled gap effect at the subject site could particularly serve as motivation for future studies to further explore the interaction between domain-general cognitive control and language-specific processing.
