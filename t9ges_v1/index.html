Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine, Daniel Lakens and Cristian Mesquida

Overall, among all instances flagged as power statements, 90.6% were correct (positive predictive value).

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download information from other preregistration sites, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). Statcheck might still miss one-sided tests that are described in another sentence, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 685 tests with 34 instances of inconsistent reporting, Statcheck correctly detected 34 of them, and incorrectly identified 26. Therefore 0% of true instances were missed, and 43% of detections were false positives. See https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/ for more details of the validation.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on three hand-coded sets of papers (scienceverse/validation: 249 Psychological Science, 250 Economics and 250 MDPI papers). Of 1,783 non-significant p-values in the ground truth, version 0.4.4.3 found 1,719 (3.6% missed), and 94.8% of its 1,814 detections were in the ground truth. Some differences come from the ground truth: it lacks p-values with a correction name (“p Bonf = .061”), and counts partial eta squared written with its subscript after it (“η 2 p = .05”), “p = .05”, and some table notes and figure legends (“† p < .10, *p < .05”), which this module leaves out, as non-significant p-values. How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

If you want to extend the package to be able to download files from additional data repositories reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Full text

Abstract

In the past two decades, research has indicated that variation in the course of mental disorders may be partially explained by interpersonal heterogeneity. Patients with interpersonal different styles can have variable outcomes even when they do not differ in pre-treatment symptom presentation or psychiatric diagnosis. This phenomenon is known as interpersonal pathoplasticity. In this paper we present the results of a systematic review of 19 studies conducted in adult patients with DSM-IV Axis I mental disorders that (a) used the Inventory of Interpersonal Problems to identify subgroups of patients with similar interpersonal styles, and (b) compared these interpersonal subgroups in terms of demographic and clinical characteristics (e.g., age, symptom severity) and course over time (e.g., therapeutic alliance development). Across diagnostic categories, we found few differences between subgroups in demographics and in clinical presentation. At the same time, there were subgroup differences in the course of mental disorder. Specifically, having a warm-submissive interpersonal style tended to be more favourable for treatment than having a cold-submissive or cold-dominant style, in support of interpersonal pathoplasticity. This phenomenon has transdiagnostic potential in clinical practice, since between-person variation in interpersonal style provides opportunities for improving and tailoring treatment. However, our review primarily included studies on patients diagnosed with anxiety and mood disorders. To find additional evidence for interpersonal pathoplasticity, future studies should assess interpersonal heterogeneity in patients with other mental disorders and examine its relation to the course of these disorders.

review

Interactions between patients and clinicians are shaped by each person's interpersonal style, that is, the behavioural pattern the person generally uses to interact with others. Interpersonal style can be defined by a blend of communion, representing motivations to connect and get along with others, and agency, representing motivations to separate from and get ahead of others (Wright et al., 2023). This agency-communion framework, also known as the Rose of Leary (1957) or interpersonal circumplex model (Fournier et al., 2010), has been used within the clinical sciences and beyond (Eriksson, 2018; Girard et al., 2017; Kurt and Frimer, 2015; Markey et al., 2005). This review focused on between-person variation in interpersonal style among patients diagnosed with a mental disorder and aimed to show how this may relate to the presentation and course of their symptoms.

The interpersonal circumplex model is central to Contemporary Integrative Interpersonal Theory (CIIT; Wright et al., 2023). Rooted in classic interpersonal theory (Sullivan, 1953) and building on the work of Leary (1957) and various others (e.g., Horowitz, 2004; Kiesler, 1996), CIIT holds that a person's interpersonal style is shaped by their social development, is expressed more in some social contexts than in others, and may cause interpersonal problems if it does not match the present social environment for a longer period of time. These problems can become a source of distress and may thereby contribute to a deterioration of mental health. Indeed, currently symptomatic patients diagnosed with a mental disorder generally report high levels of interpersonal distress (e.g., Gómez Penedo et al., 2025a; Harrison et al., 2022; McEvoy et al., 2013).

However, the association between a diagnosis and an interpersonal style is complex. Two patients with different diagnoses may have the same interpersonal style (Girard et al., 2017). This is consistent with the multifinality principle of developmental psychopathology, which holds that the same risk factor can have different outcomes (Cicchetti and Rogosch, 1996). Conversely, in line with the equifinality principle, which holds that different risk factors can have the same outcome, two patients with the same mental disorder may have different interpersonal styles. For example, while some patients with social phobia (social anxiety disorder) tend to be low in agency and low in communion, others may tend to be low in agency in combination with high communion (Cain et al., 2010). While the former group will primarily report being socially avoidant (i.e., problems with cold submissiveness) the latter group will primarily report being overly accommodating (i.e., problems with warm submissiveness).

The Inventory of Interpersonal Problems (IIP) was developed by Horowitz et al. (1988) and can be used to gain insight into levels of interpersonal distress and into respondents' interpersonal style.

The IIP includes typical interpersonal behaviours and asks how hard respondents find it to do these behaviours (e.g., "say no to other people") or to what extent respondents do them too much (e.g., "please other people"). A higher score across all items reflects more general interpersonal distress. Alden et al. (1990) derived a 64-item version containing 8 (octant) subscales that give insight into respondents' difficulties with being domineering, vindictive, cold, socially inhibited, non-assertive, overly accommodating, self-sacrificing, or intrusive. In line with the interpersonal circumplex model, these subscales form a circular correlation pattern, with conceptually more similar subscales correlating more positively and dissimilar subscales correlating negatively. Consequently, subscale scores are often reduced to two orthogonal factors. In line with the aforementioned interpersonal circumplex model, these factors are labelled Communion and Agency (Wright et al., 2009). The blend of these two factors makes up respondents' interpersonal style and predicts their main interpersonal problem theme (e.g., low agency and either low or high communion; Cain et al., 2010). This 64-item version is known as the IIP-Circumplex (IIP-C; Alden et al., 1990) or IIP-64 (Horowitz et al., 2000). Several 32-item versions have been derived from this one 64-item version, including both the IIP-Short Circumplex (IIP-SC; Soldz et al., 1995) and the IIP-32 (Horowitz et al., 2000).

Multiple IIP studies conducted in patients with mental disorders have suggested the existence of subgroups of patients with the same psychiatric diagnosis but with different interpersonal styles (e.g., Cain et al., 2010; Grosse Holtforth et al., 2014; Przeworski et al., 2011). Results from these studies have been used to argue that while these interpersonal subgroups are largely comparable in terms of clinical presentation (e.g., anxiety or depression symptom severity, diagnostic comorbidity) before treatment, they may show different trajectories in the course of their disorder and vary substantially in treatment outcome (in terms of reduction in both symptoms and interpersonal distress; Cain et al., 2010; Salzer et al., 2011). Thus, interpersonal heterogeneity might contribute to between-patient differences in clinical outcomes. This notion, that interpersonal style accounts for variation in the course of mental disorder, is known as interpersonal pathoplasticity (Wright et al., 2023).

The fact that two patients diagnosed with the same mental disorder can vary substantially in their clinical outcome remains a major problem in clinical practice. While this may be explained by between-patient heterogeneity in symptom severity and presentation (e.g., Wienicke et al., 2023), it might also be explained by between-patient differences in interpersonal style (even among patients with similar symptom severity and presentation). In line with this argument, several studies have shown that interpersonal style can predict treatment process (in terms of therapeutic alliance development; Zilcha-Mano et al., 2015), which in turn has been shown to predict treatment success. Indeed, interpersonal style is considered particularly relevant during patient-therapist interaction and can thus be targeted within the therapeutic context (e.g., Kiesler, 1996). Consequently, pretreatment assessment of interpersonal style could be highly informative for clinical practice.

There have been systematic reviews of IIP studies conducted in patients with mental disorders (Gómez Penedo and Flückiger, 2023; Gómez Penedo et al., 2025b; McFarquhar et al., 2018). These reviews mostly focused on IIP-derived general interpersonal distress. One review included a metaanalysis that showed a moderate to large decrease in interpersonal distress with treatment (specifically, psychotherapy for depression; McFarquhar et al., 2018). Another review included a meta-analysis showing that patients with an anxiety or depressive disorder who have more interpersonal distress at the start of psychotherapy generally have worse treatment outcomes (Gómez Penedo and Flückiger, 2023). Most recently, Gómez Penedo et al. (2025b) conducted a mega-analysis of 10 treatment studies conducted in 1349 depressed patients. Higher pre-treatment interpersonal distress was again predictive of a worse treatment outcome, irrespective of the type of treatment (e.g., psychotherapy vs. medication). While this review also found that pre-treatment communion and agency scores did not significantly predict treatment outcome, these scores were considered separately, rather than as a blend representing interpersonal style. Overall, while the aforementioned reviews of IIP studies are valuable and provide clinically relevant insights, none of these reviews considered how patients with the same mental disorder but different interpersonal styles may vary in the presentation and course of their disorder.

The aim of the present study was to evaluate the currently available clinical data on interpersonal heterogeneity in mental disorders. Accordingly, we present the results of a systematic review of studies that included individuals with a primary diagnosis of a mental disorder according to Axis I of the fourth edition of the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders (DSM; American Psychiatric Association, 1994) or comparable (e.g., DSM-5, International Classification of Diseases; American Psychiatric Association, 2013; World Health Organization, 2010) and that used a version of the IIP (for a scoping review, see Hughes and Barkham, 2005) to derive subgroups characterized by different interpersonal styles.

Our first research question was: What interpersonal subgroups can be identified among individuals diagnosed with a mental disorder? We expected to find subgroups across all quadrants of the interpersonal circumplex model, regardless of diagnoses. This would serve to show that there is no one-to-one relation between a specific interpersonal style and a specific mental disorder, thus supporting the transdiagnostic utility of CIIT. The existence of subgroups also provides necessary evidence for interpersonal pathoplasticity (Cain et al., 2010).

Our second research question was: How do interpersonal styles relate to the presentation and course of mental disorder? In support of interpersonal pathoplasticity, interpersonal subgroups were not expected to differ on demographic variables, nor on clinical presentation at the time of diagnosis (e.g., pre-treatment symptom levels and general interpersonal distress; Cain et al., 2010).

At the same time, interpersonal subgroups were expected to differ on longitudinal outcomes. This would also be consistent with the criteria for interpersonal pathoplasticity (Wright et al., 2023).

Overall, the rationale for our review was that the interpersonal style of patients with a mental disorder may moderate their clinical outcome (e.g., Cain et al., 2010). Evidence for this could be used to inform and improve the process and content of treatment across psychiatric diagnoses.

While a transdiagnostic approach to the treatment of mental disorders has been developing in recent years (Dalgleish et al., 2020; Farchione et al., 2024), to date this approach has mostly targeted behaviours, cognitions, and emotion regulation without an explicit focus on interpersonal situations.

Method

Our review method followed the updated PRISMA guideline (Page et al., 2021). The review was preregistered on Prospero (#56229). Two deviations from the preregistration should be noted. Firstly, we also included studies in which less than 100%, but still more than 50%, of participants had a primary diagnosis of a DSM-IV Axis I mental disorder or comparable. Second, we included studies in which patients had a variety of diagnoses on Axis I and sometimes on Axis II. This was considered acceptable provided most patients had an Axis I disorder.

Search strategy

We first searched PsycInfo and Medline on 16 October 2024, using EBSCOhost. Our specific search string is available in the Supplementary Material. We preregistered the following selection criteria: (a) Studies were empirical, cross-sectional or longitudinal, peer reviewed, and published as an academic journal article, with no restrictions to the date or language of publication.

Studies in books or dissertations, case studies, and unpublished studies were excluded.

(b) Study samples consisted of adults with a primary diagnosis of a DSM-IV Axis I mental disorder or comparable, excluding (neuro)cognitive disorders, sexual or gender identity disorders, sleep disorders, somatoform disorders, adjustment disorders, and disorders usually first diagnosed in infancy, childhood, or adolescence. Samples (primarily) consisting of adults with a primary diagnosis of a DSM-IV Axis II personality disorder or comparable were also excluded. If patients <18 years of age were included, then the mean age of the sample was at least 18 years.

(c) Patients completed an established version of the IIP (Hughes and Barkham, 2005).

(d) IIP scores were used to derive interpersonal subgroups, using cluster analysis or comparable.

Articles identified in EBSCOhost were imported into EndNote 20. Duplicates were identified automatically using the Find Duplicates option and manually by sorting by title and author. After removing all duplicates, the EndNote file containing the identified articles was imported into Rayyan (Ouzzani et al., 2016). Two independent raters (A2 and A3 -BLINDED FOR REVIEW) screened all articles using the title and abstract. Due to moderate agreement (Cohen's kappa = .54), articles on which the two raters disagreed were generally retained, to reduce the risk of inadvertently excluding relevant articles before reading the full text. The same raters then independently assessed the full text of the remaining articles for eligibility, with substantial agreement, Cohen's kappa = .74. In case of disagreement, articles were discussed between the two raters and, if needed, the first author was consulted. Reasons for exclusion were noted according to the aforementioned selection criteria.

After selecting relevant articles, we used Scopus to identify earlier studies cited by these articles as well as more recent studies that cited them. This was done on 28 January 2025, after which A2 and A3 first screened titles and abstracts, and then A1 and A2 included additional articles based on the full text. After completing our initial searches, we repeated the search in EBSCOhost on 6 March 2026.

Analytic procedure

Most included studies used the structural summary method (Wright et al., 2009) to analyse the IIP data and derive participants' levels of general interpersonal distress, interpersonal style, and the specificity of this style. These variables are represented by the structural summary parameters "elevation", "angular displacement", and "amplitude", respectively. These parameters can be averaged to sample or subgroup levels, provided the model fit (R² ) is adequate (at least .70; Wright et al., 2009).

To answer research question 1 (on the existence of interpersonal subgroups), whenever possible, we extracted structural summary parameters for each subgroup that was identified by a study. To locate each identified subgroup in the circular space of the interpersonal circumplex model, the displacement parameter was used. This parameter is expressed as an angle (range 0-360°) and represents each subgroup's interpersonal style, defined as a blend of communion and agency (see Introduction). If the model fit at sample level was inadequate (R² < .70), then this was an indicator that interpersonal subgroups exist within the sample. However, even if R² > .70, then interpersonal subgroups may exist (Wright et al., 2009), hence samples with adequate model fit were retained at this point. In contrast, if the model fit at subgroup level was inadequate, then this subgroup was excluded from further analysis. All subgroups with adequate model fit were sorted into quadrants (Q1-Q4) corresponding to an interpersonal style characterized by higher levels of both communion and agency (Q1 at 0-90˚: warm-dominant), lower levels of communion and higher levels of agency (Q2 at 90-180˚: cold-dominant), lower levels of both communion and agency (Q3 at 180-270˚: cold-submissive), or higher levels of communion and lower levels of agency (Q4 at 270-

360˚: warm-submissive).

To answer research question 2 (on the correlates of interpersonal heterogeneity), we first checked for subgroup differences in demographic variables, to gain insight into whether these variables might act as confounders (Cain et al., 2010). We then proceeded to examine subgroup differences in pre-treatment clinical variables, such as symptom levels and general interpersonal distress. Finally, we examined subgroup differences in longitudinal outcomes, particularly in the context of treatment. This served to gain insight into the occurrence of interpersonal pathoplasticity, that is, whether interpersonal styles (that defined subgroups) relate to the course of mental disorder (Wright et al., 2023).

Quality assessment

The risk of bias for both the cross-sectional and longitudinal data included in the systematic review was assessed using quality assessment criteria derived from checklists available from the Joanna Briggs Institute (Moola et al., 2020) and adapted for the purposes of this review, see Table S3.

Specifically, we adopted JBI's critical appraisal tools for analytical cross-sectional and cohort studies, respectively. One rater (A4) reviewed all included articles multiple times (at least twice for studies with both cross-sectional and longitudinal data) and discussed issues with one or two colleagues (A1, A5) whenever necessary.

Results

Summary of searches

Figure S1 in the Supplementary Material presents a flow diagram of the study selection process. We initially identified 171 articles in EBSCOhost of which 36 articles were duplicates. When screening the remaining articles, 53 were excluded on the basis of title or abstract. When assessing the remaining articles for eligibility using their full text, 66 more articles were excluded in light of the selection criteria. One additional article was excluded (Schauenburg et al., 1995) after we contacted the authors and learnt they could not provide the data required for our synthesis. This left 14 articles, containing 15 studies. Four studies were then added to our review by using Scopus, making a total of 19 studies. A final search on 6 March 2026 did not result in inclusion of additional studies.

Table 1 lists all 19 included studies. Ten studies focused on anxiety disorders, mostly generalized anxiety disorder (GAD) and social phobia. Five studies focused on mood disorders, or more specifically depressive disorders. Three studies included patients with a mix of anxiety and mood disorders, and one study focused on a substance-related disorder. To be able to contextualize the results, Table S1 provides additional background information on each study. Whenever possible, structural summary data are provided at sample level. As mentioned in the Method section, these data give insight into average levels of general interpersonal distress ("elevation"), the average interpersonal style ("angular displacement") and the specificity of this style ("amplitude") within the sample (Wright et al., 2009). For 13 studies, Table 1 also includes the model fit (R² ) at sample level. Interpersonal subgroups are likely when R² < .70, which was the case for 4 studies, but may also exist when the R² is higher, so all studies were retained at this point.

Subgrouping was done on 31-543 participants per sample (mean 206) using mostly the IIP-64 (Alden et al., 1990; Horowitz et al., 2000). Two samples completed the IIP-32 (Horowitz et al., 2000) and one sample the IIP-SC (Soldz et al., 1995). The number of subgroups per sample was most often derived using a form of cluster analysis on the orthogonal IIP factors of Communion and Agency, and reported to range from two to eight.

Research question 1: Location of subgroups in the interpersonal space?

Table S2 in the Supplementary Material lists all subgroups identified across samples. Q4 subgroups were the most prevalent, followed by Q3. Structural summary data were available for 90% of 69 identified subgroups. All except two of these subgroups showed at least adequate model fit (R² > .70), suggesting they represented prototypical subgroups. The two exceptions both included patients with GAD; these subgroups (both in Q1) were excluded from further analysis.

In total 67 subgroups were included in the analyses. Figure 1 shows their location in the circular space. Across disorder categories, we generally found subgroups across all quadrants of the interpersonal circumplex model. All samples had at least one subgroup in Q4, 15 samples had at least one subgroup in Q3, 11 samples had a subgroup in Q2, and 11 samples in Q1. As most subgroups were located in Q4, followed by Q3, interpersonal styles characterized by low agency were most common.

Research question 2: Interpersonal subgroups and the presentation and course of mental disorder?

Demographic variables. Eleven studies compared subgroups on demographic variables, mostly gender and age, see Table 2. None of these studies found significant age differences between subgroups. Most also found no significant differences in gender, except Q4 subgroups had a higher proportion of female participants than Q3 subgroups in some studies (Clapp et al., 2014; Grosse Holtforth et al., 2014; Locke et al., 2018; Simon et al., 2015), suggesting that women were somewhat more likely than men to have a warm-submissive interpersonal style. None of the studies that examined other demographic variables (i.e., education, employment, ethnicity, marital status) found significant subgroup differences on these variables. Overall, gender appeared the most likely to confound subgroup differences in clinical variables, but at the same time only a few studies considered or formally analysed subgroup differences in demographic variables.

Pre-treatment clinical variables. All studies except one (Puschner et al., 2005) compared interpersonal subgroups on severity of mental disorder symptoms, diagnostic comorbidity, and/or pre-treatment general interpersonal distress, see Table 2. Symptom severity varied only slightly across subgroups. Two studies found differences between subgroups within the same quadrant but not between subgroups in different quadrants (Clapp et al., 2014; Frandsen et al., 2020). One study found higher symptom severity specifically in one of the Q4 subgroups, but not in the other two subgroups within the same quadrant (Grosse Holtforth et al., 2014). Overall, there was little evidence that subgroups differed on pre-treatment clinical variables.

In terms of diagnostic comorbidity, there were no significant subgroup differences on comorbid mental disorder (on DSM-IV Axis I). Observed subgroup differences in the prevalence of comorbid personality disorders (on Axis II) were limited to avoidant personality disorder (AvPD).

While one study reported more AvPD comorbidity in subgroup Q4 than in subgroup Q2 (Zilcha-Mano et al., 2015), another study found AvPD comorbidity to be highest in subgroup Q3 (Przeworski et al., 2011; Study 2). In terms of general interpersonal distress, we found no consistent differences. While in one study general interpersonal distress was higher in Q4 and Q3 subgroups than in the Q1 subgroup (Minges et al., 2020), in another study subgroup Q3 differed from subgroup Q4a but not from subgroup Q4b (Frandsen et al., 2020). Overall, while consistent subgroup differences in diagnostic comorbidity were rare, often these differences were also not assessed systematically.

Longitudinal outcomes.

All 11 studies included in our review that followed patients over time focused on treatment process (range 4-21 sessions) or outcome (range 6 months -1.5 years), see Table 3. Across these studies, we found both similarities and differences between subgroups. With respect to the treatment process, we found that Q4 subgroups tended to show better therapeutic alliance development over the course of 5-8 sessions than Q3 or Q2 subgroups (Grosse Holtforth et al., 2014; Puschner et al., 2005; Zilcha-Mano et al., 2015). In another study, with a mean follow-up of 21 sessions, the Q4 subgroup completed fewer psychotherapy sessions and were more likely to stop treatment than the Q2 subgroup (Pitman and Hilsenroth, 2016), which seems inconsistent but could also indicate reduced need for treatment over the course of time. In line with this idea, there were no subgroup differences in post-treatment anxiety levels in this study (i.e., after 21 sessions). At the same time, studies that found subgroup differences in treatment outcome (e.g., post-treatment symptom severity and general interpersonal distress) were largely consistent, with Q4 subgroups generally having the most favourable outcome (Cain et al., 2010; Gómez Penedo et al., 2019; Minges et al., 2020; Salzer et al., 2011). The follow-up period in the study by Gómez Penedo et al. (2019) was 4 sessions, and in the remaining three studies it was 6 months. Overall, 11 out of the 19 reviewed studies included findings on subgroup differences in treatment process and/or outcome. Three studies that included treatment process measures found evidence for better therapeutic alliance development among Q4 subgroups. Four studies that included treatment outcome measures found larger decreases in interpersonal distress or symptom severity among Q4 subgroups. Subgroup Q4 thus compared favourably to the other subgroups. While this was not found in all longitudinal studies, no study found better outcomes in other subgroups.

Quality assessment

Table S3 in the Supplementary Material includes the results of the quality assessment. Most studies had clear selection criteria, adequately described sample characteristics and setting, included psychometric data on the IIP, and specified how interpersonal subgroups were identified. Discussion

In this systematic review, we answered two research questions aiming to evaluate the currently available clinical data on interpersonal heterogeneity among patients with mental disorders. Our first question was on the existence of different interpersonal subgroups. To answer this question, we reviewed studies of individuals with a DSM-IV Axis I mental disorder (or comparable) that identified interpersonal subgroups using the IIP. As expected, we found subgroups in all four quadrants of the interpersonal space, indicating that interpersonal style can vary substantially, both within psychiatric diagnoses and across. This supports more frequent inclusion of the IIP during pre-treatment clinical assessment. The IIP has mostly been used to assess patients' overall level of interpersonal distress, but it can also give insight into which specific behaviours are likely to contribute to the distress. As such, even though the IIP says little about the overall quality of a patient's social relationships, it can help explain what types of problems patients may experience in personally relevant interpersonal situations, including in daily life (e.g., with romantic partners) but also in therapy (with clinicians).

While the finding of interpersonal heterogeneity in mental disorders is not new, our review highlights its occurrence across diagnoses and thereby the transdiagnostic value of the IIP.

While we found interpersonal subgroups across all four quadrants of the interpersonal circumplex model, subgroups in the two submissive quadrants (Q3 and Q4 in Figure 1) were more common than subgroups in the dominant quadrants (Q1 and Q2). This is not surprising given that, except for Minges et al. (2020), all studies focused on patients with anxiety or depressive disorders.

These are considered internalising mental disorders (Krueger et al., 1998), and internalising has primarily been associated with low agency (i.e., submissiveness). In comparison, the association with communion is more variable, ranging from low to high (i.e., from coldness to warmth; Girard et al., 2017). At the same time, submissive subgroups were also found in the study on cocaine dependence (Minges et al., 2020), which is generally considered an externalising disorder and thus associated with high agency (i.e., dominance; Girard et al., 2017). Overall, across diagnostic categories, we found evidence in line with the broader differentiation between internalising and externalising disorders, but also evidence indicating that not all patients with an internalising disorder (e.g., anxiety, depression) versus an externalising disorder (e.g., cocaine dependence) fit this type of differentiation, or can be differentiated along the dimensions of agency alone. Overall, interpersonal heterogeneity was clearly present.

In relation to our second research question, we were primarily expecting to find differences in the course of mental disorder, regardless of demographic variables and clinical presentation. The 11 studies that had a longitudinal component all focused on treatment (Table 3). Three studies examined a treatment process measure, namely, therapeutic alliance during the initial sessions or weeks. In all three studies, warm-submissive subgroups had a more favourable treatment process than other subgroups. These findings align with other studies showing better alliance development among patients with higher levels of communion (Altenstein-Yamanaka et al., 2017; Dinger et al., 2013; Kushner et al., 2016), lower levels of agency (Gómez Penedo et al., 2020; Renner et al., 2012), or both (i.e., more friendly submissiveness; Constantino and Smith-Hansen, 2008).

Seven of the longitudinal studies examined decreases in symptom severity or general interpersonal distress as treatment outcome measures. In four of these studies, warm-submissive subgroups tended to have better treatment outcomes than cold-submissive or cold-dominant subgroups, most notably larger decreases in general interpersonal distress. The follow-up period was 6 months in the three of the four studies. The studies that considered the same outcome measures, but did not find subgroup differences, were comparable in length.

The observed subgroup differences in treatment process and outcome were found in nearabsence of differences in demographics and pre-treatment clinical presentation (Table 2). Thus, subgroup differences in treatment success could not readily be explained by these variables, at least not at the group level and within one diagnostic category. Nonetheless, it may be premature to rule out their confounding influence. Subgroup differences in demographic and pre-treatment clinical variables, including general interpersonal distress, were often not evaluated systematically, or perhaps not found due to sample size or limited sample variability (e.g., in ethnicity or education). It remains possible that demographic variables such as gender, and clinical variables such as pretreatment interpersonal distress, are significant contributors to observed subgroup differences in longitudinal outcomes.

Implications

If warm-submissive patients have a higher rate of treatment success than cold-submissive patients (who formed the second most common subgroup across the reviewed studies), and if developing an alliance with cold-submissive patients is more difficult, then perhaps an increased focus on alliance development in cold-submissive patients could be beneficial. This could be achieved by assigning cold-submissive patients to a more intensive treatment (Zevalkink and Berghout, 2008) and perhaps also by being a more empathetic therapist (Cain et al., 2010). Additionally, the treatment of coldsubmissive patients could be tailored such that they learn to become more communal. For example, a focus on fear of negative evaluation, aiming to reduce social avoidance, has been proposed for cold-submissive patients with social phobia (Cain et al., 2010) but may also benefit cold-submissive patients with another primary diagnosis, such as persistent depressive disorder (where coldsubmissiveness is relatively common; Ley et al., 2011).

At a broader level, our results suggest that distinct mental disorders, as diagnosed using the DSM or comparable, can have comparable interpersonal correlates. This highlights the need for transdiagnostic research on how interpersonal style relates to the core emotional and cognitive symptoms of mental disorders (e.g., feeling tense or sad, negative thinking patterns) rather than to the diagnoses per se (Girard et al., 2017). Moreover, while our results do not provide direct evidence for interpersonal pathoplasticity, they align with the concept.

Potential limitations

We distinguish between limitations of the studies included in our review, and of the review itself.

Regarding the former, while we conducted an extensive quality assessment and found study quality to be generally acceptable, few studies clearly described relevant demographic and pre-treatment clinical variables in detail. Moreover, even when such variables were available, few studies formally compared subgroups on these variables, or fully reported these comparisons, as mentioned earlier.

Most reviewed studies used cluster analysis to derive subgroups. In general, the value of such a categorical approach to characterize interpersonal problems has been criticised (Wendt et al., 2019). More specifically, reviewed studies that used cluster analysis (or latent class analysis or latent profile analysis) generally assumed at least two subgroups and varied in the maximum number of subgroups assumed. Such methodological variations reduced the comparability of the results across studies. Besides, results derived from cluster analysis may depend on the characteristics and size of the sample being studied (König et al., 2016).

Reassuringly, we found a reasonably clear pattern favouring warm-submissive subgroups in terms of treatment process and outcome, using a procedure that involved comparing subgroups by quadrant of the interpersonal circumplex model. Nonetheless, this quadrant-based approach was neither preregistered nor without drawbacks. Several studies had multiple subgroups in Q3 or Q4, see Table S2, and sometimes there were differences between subgroups in the same quadrant, see Tables 2 and 3. At the same time, our quadrant-based approach was in line with recommendations (Gurtman, 1996) and employed by two of the reviewed studies (König et al., 2016; Puschner et al., 2005). While we also considered dividing subgroups by octants, in line with the subscales of the IIP reflecting eight types of interpersonal problems in the circular space (Alden et al., 1990), none of the reviewed studies used this approach. Also, an octant-based split would have decreased the number of subgroups per octant, and increased the number of comparisons, which would have limited our options for synthesis.

Our review focused on studies conducted in patients with DSM-IV Axis I mental disorders (or comparable) that used the IIP to divide samples into interpersonal subgroups. This facilitated our synthesis and subsequent interpretation of the studies' results, but it means we excluded studies that primarily focused on (DSM-IV Axis II) personality disorders (e.g., Solomonov et al., 2020). While this might be considered a limitation of our review, we excluded these types of studies because CIIT conceptualizes personality disorders as interpersonal disorders (Wright et al., 2022), each characterized by a specific interpersonal problem theme (Girard et al., 2017; Wilson et al., 2017). This conceptualization is also in line with the 11 th revision of the International Classification of Diseases (World Health Organization, 2022) which specifies personality disorder in terms of severity (depending on the level of distress) and pattern (depending on which aspect of personality is the most problematic), and no longer differentiates between discrete personality disorders.

We also excluded studies that used non-clinical samples or that used questionnaires other than the IIP to derive interpersonal subgroups. This means that while we included Cain et al. (2010), who examined interpersonal subgroups among patients with social phobia, we excluded Cooper and Anderson (2019), who did the same among students with a high score on a social phobia scale, and Cain et al. (2012) who used the NEO Personality Inventory to examine subgroups among patients with major depressive disorder. While not considering these types of studies may be considered a limitation of our review, it is worth noting that comparable to Cain et al. (2010), Cooper and Anderson (2019) also found two subgroups in Q3 and Q4, and Cain et al. (2012) found their warmsubmissive subgroup to have a less chronic course of disorder than other subgroups, despite no significant differences in baseline symptom severity and comorbidity. As these findings are in line with those of our review, we believe that including studies that used non-clinical samples or questionnaires other than the IIP would not have altered our results and conclusions. Nonetheless, questionnaires in general are limited by being self-evaluative measures. Additional research on interpersonal styles defined by other types of measures (e.g., observational or implicit measures) would be expected to generate additional insights.

Future directions

All reviewed studies except one (Minges et al., 2020) focused on anxiety and depressive disorders.

Our review may promote future research on interpersonal subgroups in other types of mental disorders, such as substance-related disorders (Minges et al., 2020) and eating disorders. In support of the latter category, Ambwani and Hopwood (2009) found interpersonal subgroups and evidence for interpersonal pathoplasticity among students with a high score on a bulimia scale.

More broadly, we support a transdiagnostic approach to examine if interpersonal heterogeneity has value in understanding the presentation and course of psychopathology. Most individual studies included in this review focused on one diagnosis. On the one hand, meaningful subgroup differences in clinical variables such as pre-treatment symptom severity and general interpersonal distress may emerge when multiple mental disorders are considered simultaneously.

Such cross-sectional subgroup differences would confound evidence of subgroup differences in longitudinal outcomes and thereby challenge the concept of interpersonal pathoplasticity. On the other hand, if interpersonal heterogeneity were found to have explanatory and prognostic value beyond psychiatric diagnoses, this could have a significant impact on research and clinical practice.

If future research continues to find that interpersonal style relates to the course of mental disorder, then the practical use of measures such as the IIP becomes even more clinically relevant.

To date, the IIP has mostly been used to track changes in general interpersonal distress in the context of treatment (e.g., McFarquhar et al., 2018). Further, higher pre-treatment interpersonal distress has been shown to predict a worse treatment outcome (Gómez Penedo et al., 2025b). Furthermore, pre-treatment assessment of an individual patient's interpersonal style may inform therapists not only on what to expect during their interactions with the patient (e.g., Kiesler, 1996) but also on how to tailor treatment to the patient, for example, by offering assertiveness training to Q3/Q4 but not Q2/Q1 patients. This more personalized approach could be expected to contribute to a change in the patient's style, which in turn could help prevent the re-emergence of interpersonal distress and mental disorder. This idea deserves further study but is broadly in line with other recent literature on the relevance of CIIT for psychotherapy (Cain et al., 2024; Pincus and Hopwood, 2025).

Clinically, we believe the quadrant-based approach to be both intuitive and practical. Each patient can easily be placed in the quadrant corresponding to their IIP-derived angular displacement score. This provides immediate insight into the nature of their interpersonal problems, along the dimensions of communion and agency, and may even have predictive value for treatment success.

Conclusions

This systematic review found that interpersonal subgroups characterized by warm-dominance, colddominance, cold-submissiveness, and warm-submissiveness can be found across diagnostic categories. More importantly, we synthesized evidence in support of the notion that patients with anxiety, mood, and substance-related disorders may have a more favourable treatment process and outcome if they have a warm-submissive interpersonal style. Appendix 1) Selection criteria: This criterion assesses the clarity with which inclusion criteria are described. "Yes" is assigned when the selection criteria are clear and explicitly stated, "Unclear" is assigned when the selection criteria are vaguely defined, and "No" is assigned when selection criteria are absent.

2) Sample characteristics and setting: This criterion evaluates a) the clarity with which the clinical characteristics of participants and the clinical setting are described, and b) whether demographic information (gender/sex and age) is reported. "Yes" is assigned if both clinical characteristics and setting, as well as demographic characteristics, are clearly described, "Unclear" is assigned if one of the two components is missing, and "No" is assigned when both components are absent.

3) IIP psychometrics: This criterion assesses whether the psychometric properties of the IIP are adequately described, regarding a) reliability, such that Cronbach's alpha (or an equivalent statistic) is reported for the current sample; and b) validity, such that either structural or construct validity is examined. "Yes" is assigned when both reliability and validity are clearly reported, "Unclear" is assigned when one of the two components is rated "no" or when most information is unclear, and "No" is assigned when both components are rated "no". 4) Identifying interpersonal subgroups: This criterion evaluates the clarity with which interpersonal subgroups are derived, specifically: a) statistical analyses and software used, b) number of solutions explored and chosen, and c) clarity of the basis for analysis (e.g., agency and communion dimensions, ipsatized octant scores). "Yes" is assigned when all three components are clearly described, "Unclear" is assigned when one subcriterion is rated "no" or one or more components are unclear, and "No" is assigned when two or more subcriteria are rated "no". 5) Demographic differences between interpersonal subgroups: This criterion assesses whether demographic factors, namely age and/or gender/sex, are considered as potential confounders, and whether subgroup differences relative to these demographics are examined. "Yes" is assigned when at least one demographic variable (age or gender/sex) is addressed and examined, and "No" is assigned when neither demographic variable is addressed and examined 6) Strategies to address demographic differences: If demographic differences exist between interpersonal subtypes, this criterion evaluates whether a) strategies to account for these differences are implemented in statistical analyses, or b) considerations or recommendations related to these differences are assessed in the discussion section. "Yes" is assigned when strategies are implemented or discussed, only when demographic differences are identified. "NA" is assigned when demographic differences were examined under Criterion 5 but none were found. "No" is assigned when demographic differences were not examined, and thus not addressed. 7) Methodological clarity and reliability of cross-sectional outcomes: This criterion assesses cross-sectional outcomes in terms of a) clarity of constructs and measurement methods, b) reporting of reliability (Cronbach's alpha or equivalent) for all crosssectional measures in the current sample, and c) specification of the clinical interview, when applicable (i.e., when outcomes are based on a clinical interview). "Yes" is assigned when all cross-sectional measures are clearly described, reliability is adequately assessed, and the clinical interview is specified. "Mixed" is assigned when reliability and validity are described for some, but not all cross-sectional measures. "Unclear" is assigned when all cross-sectional measures lack either reliability or methodological clarity.

8) Statistical analyses:

This criterion evaluates whether the statistical analyses linking interpersonal subtypes to cross-sectional outcomes are a) clearly described (including specific tests and follow-up comparisons), and b) accompanied by a description of how multiple comparisons are handled, when applicable. "Yes" is assigned when both components are rated "yes", "Unclear" is assigned when one component is "no" or when some or most of the information is unclear, and "No" is assigned when both components are rated "no", or one is "uncertain" while the other is "no". 9) Confounders for longitudinal outcomes: This criterion assesses whether potential confounding factors were considered as such and whether subgroup differences relative to these factors were examined. "Yes" is assigned when at least one potential confounding variable is addressed and examined, and "No" otherwise. 10) Strategies to deal with confounders of longitudinal outcomes: If differences due to a confounder were found between interpersonal subtypes, this criterion evaluates whether a) strategies to account for these differences are implemented in statistical analyses, or b) considerations or recommendations related to these differences are assessed in the discussion section. "Yes" is assigned when strategies are implemented or discussed, only when differences due to a confounder are found between interpersonal subtypes. "NA" is assigned when such differences were examined under Criterion 9, but none were found. "No" is assigned when such differences were not examined, and thus not addressed.

Follow-up clarity: This criterion assesses the clarity of the follow-up, that is, whether the time since the baseline assessment is clearly specified. "Yes" is assigned when follow-up is clearly and explicitly stated, "Unclear" is assigned when the follow-up is vaguely defined (e.g., pre-and post-treatment), and "No" is assigned when this information is absent.

Incomplete follow-up: This criterion assesses whether reasons for loss-to-follow-up were described. "Yes" is assigned when the reasons underlying incomplete follow-up are clearly and explicitly stated, "Unclear" is assigned when the reasons underlying incomplete follow-up are vaguely defined, and "No" is assigned when this information is absent.

Strategies for incomplete follow-up: This criterion assesses whether strategies to address incomplete follow-up were reported and utilized.

Statistical analyses for longitudinal outcomes: This criterion evaluates whether the statistical analyses linking interpersonal subtypes to longitudinal outcomes were a) clearly described (including specific tests and follow-up comparisons), and b) accompanied by a description of how multiple comparisons are handled, when applicable.
