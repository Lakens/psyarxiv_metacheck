Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard), also Internal Review Board - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term); not IEC followed by a number, which is a technical standard - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee/Commission, in the singular or plural - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee, Bioethics Committee/Commission, Ethikkommission - Ethical Review Authority/Unit/Office/Agency - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Human Research Protection Office/Program, Office for Human Subjects Protection - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee(s) on/for (Medical and Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt - Ethics review that was not needed: Ethics review was not required, did not require further ethical review, exempt from formal human research ethics review, no new institutional ethics review

A template line the authors have not filled in (“[Add ethics approval … here.]”) is not a statement.

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

When a statement is expected

The module sorts each paper by where its data came from (the internal .data_source(); the signs and how the rule was tested are described in R/data-source.R): - own: the authors collected data from people or animals, for this paper, in an earlier study of their own, or (a protocol) will collect them; - existing_records: records about individual people that the authors obtained for this study, such as hospital records or a cohort; - existing_unclear: existing data about people from a source the module could not identify; - open_resource: an open resource any researcher can use, such as UK Biobank or NHANES; - none: no data about people or animals (a review, simulation, commentary, theory, tutorial, coding of published papers, or figures about groups); - uncertain: the signs were missing or pointed both ways; - no_text: the paper has no text.

A statement is expected for own, existing_records and existing_unclear. A paper without a statement in these classes, or in uncertain, is flagged. The module reads the text, and the results of power, stat_p_exact, stat_check, repo_check, data_check and prereg_check when they ran before it (run it after them); without stat_p_exact, it finds the p-values in the text.

On 199 PsyArXiv and medRxiv preprints labelled after the rule was fixed, the module decided right whether a statement is needed for 86.9% (the earlier live-data search: 83.4%). Its main errors: other people’s studies described in the words of a new study, records and specimens described only as counts, and open resources the paper does not call open.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 685 tests with 34 instances of inconsistent reporting, Statcheck correctly detected 34 of them, and incorrectly identified 26. Therefore 0% of true instances were missed, and 43% of detections were false positives. See https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/ for more details of the validation.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on 226 Psychological Science papers with 5,019 hand-coded p-values, 438 of them imprecise (scienceverse/validation, branch stat_p_exact). Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). Of the misses, 13 are starred table notes (“*p < .05”) that the module leaves out on purpose and 11 are p-values it did not find; of the wrong reports, 20 are p-values the ground truth does not contain (such as “ps < .05”) and 6 use ≤ or ≥. These figures measure agreement with the hand-coding, which has known errors in both directions (scienceverse/validation#48).

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on three hand-coded sets of papers (scienceverse/validation: 249 Psychological Science, 250 Economics and 250 MDPI papers). Of 1,783 non-significant p-values in the ground truth, version 0.4.4.3 found 1,719 (3.6% missed), and 94.8% of its 1,814 detections were in the ground truth. How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. Full text

Abstract

Ensuring equitable language learning support for everyone has become an urgent necessity, considering increasingly linguistically diverse classrooms. Understanding how linguistic backgrounds affect language learning outcomes is crucial, beyond the binary categories of nonnative vs. native. Linguistic distance (LD) measures present more nuanced instruments to investigate the effect of the speaker's mother tongue. This systematic review examines research incorporating LD as a predictor of second or foreign language learning outcomes. Based on the search criteria, 35 articles were identified. The selected studies exhibit high variability in the tested languages, data samples, and methodological approaches. Among various LD measures, lexical distance and the index method were the most common approximations.

Findings predominantly report a significant effect of LD on all language skills: speaking, writing, listening, and reading, regardless of tested language, age groups, data sample, and other nonlinguistic variables. Furthermore, the findings emphasize the importance of selecting LD measures following a skill-based approach.

Introduction

Linguistic diversity, or multilingualism, is the norm in many regions around the world. Speaking multiple languages is a daily necessity for functioning in the community or the workplace. In traditionally monolingual Western societies, increasing global migration is leading to a rise in linguistic diversity. Research across disciplines consistently underscores the essential role of language skills in facilitating both individual and societal integration (Capstick, 2021; Esser, 2006). Beyond social integration, English proficiency has become a gatekeeper for access to education and professional careers (Ghio et al., 2023; OECD, 2012). Language proficiency in the majority and/or foreign language (FL) plays a pivotal role in educational outcomes for students of all ages. Students with an immigration background, particularly first-generation immigrants (Rodríguez et al., 2020), often demonstrate lower academic performance than their nativespeaking peers (OECD, 2006 (OECD, , 2019)). This non-native language status can negatively affect their educational progress and attainment not only in language learning but also across different subjects such as literacy, science, and math (Borgonovi & Ferrara, 2020; Flisi Sara et al., 2016; OECD, 2019). It is important to note that language status or proficiency is only one variable out of many that impact educational and professional trajectories. Parental education (Fan & Chen, 2001), family income (Duncan & Brooks-Gunn, 1997), or cultural capital (Sullivan, 2001), among other variables, have the potential to create a compounding adverse effect on academic outcomes. In all subjects, language learners face the challenge of processing both linguistic and non-linguistic information in parallel. Comprehending instruction in a non-native language is cognitively more demanding and activates thinking processes, which can negatively affect the processing of non-linguistic information (Borgonovi & Ferrara, 2020).

Research on second language acquisition (SLA) and FL learning has demonstrated that the heritage language (HL) can have a significant influence on the development and proficiency in the target language (TL). The interaction between two languages within an individual speaker has been extensively studied in cross-linguistic influence (CLI) research (for a review, see Jarvis & Pavlenko, 2010). However, this influence is not a simple fusion or mixing of languages rather, it involves a more complex and nuanced interaction between the systems. This interaction can manifest in various ways, leading to facilitation or acceleration, delay, or transfer from one language to the other (Genesee et al., 1995).

The Language Transfer Hypothesis maintains that when the first language (L1) and second language (L2) share similar grammatical features and linguistic structures, such as article systems, verb inflection, and word order, L2 learners can transfer knowledge and familiarity with these structures from their L1 to an L2 instead of learning them (e.g., Forbes & Fisher, 2018; Westergaard et al., 2017). Researchers widely agree that this transfer is multifaceted, involving cognitive processes shaped by linguistic, psychological, and societal factors rather than a straightforward habit-forming mechanism (Jarvis & Pavlenko, 2010).

In CLI and language transfer research, most studies compare one language's influence on another by analyzing shared and distinct features. Comparatively, fewer studies examine the influence of multiple languages simultaneously. This limitation makes it challenging to compare results across different studies and to fully understand how the HL influences the TL. While CLI provides rich, detailed insights into the relationship among a select few languages and features, it does not offer broader insights beyond this scope. Furthermore, educational and linguistic research involving multilingual populations frequently employs a binary classification of language status, labeling individuals as either native or nonnative speakers, or as speakers of the majority language. This binary approach assumes homogeneity among non-native speakers, which oversimplifies the complex, gradient nature of linguistic processes and does not account for important differences that impact language learning and proficiency outcomes (Jaekel et al., 2024).

Research employing LD measures can provide a more nuanced perspective. The concept of LD was introduced early in linguistic research. According to Crystal (1987), the structural similarity of languages has been considered important in foreign language acquisition. The assumption is that learning a new language becomes easier when it shares structural similarities with one's native language, rather than when the languages are vastly different. However, Crystal emphasizes that establishing a direct correlation between linguistic differences and learning challenges is not straightforward. Even the fundamental task of measuring linguistic differences proves extremely complex due to the numerous variables involved.

While Crystal (1987) suggested that interlingual distance cannot account for variation in language learners with various linguistic backgrounds. Recent linguistic and educational research has recognized and developed new LD measures and their potential to advance the understanding of the interaction of two languages (Jaekel, et al., 2024; Muñoz et al., 2018; Schepens et al., 2016). LD measures allow a broader analysis of CLI using large-scale multilingual datasets.

Employing these measures enables us to shed light on the complex relationships between diverse HLs and the TL. Various methods and approaches exist for operationalizing the distance between languages at lexical, phonological, and syntactic levels, and will be described in detail in the current systematic review. While research has provided substantial evidence that LD is a powerful predictor of L2 proficiency and an individual-difference factor in language acquisition, it has been notably underexplored in both SLA (Muñoz et al., 2018) and educational research (Borgonovi & Ferrara, 2020).

Current study

The current systematic review aims to provide a comprehensive overview of research on LD, exploring how it has been theoretically defined, measured, and applied in empirical studies, particularly in relation to second and foreign language learning outcomes. The review aims to bring together current methodological approaches and provide insights into future studies and teaching practices. The main guiding research questions are: 1.

How has linguistic distance been measured? 2. How is linguistic distance associated with second and foreign language acquisition outcomes?

For consistency, the current article uses the terms HL and TL to refer to the languages between which LD was measured. HL refers to a speaker's first language or mother tongue; however, in multilingual communities, it may also be the second language. TL refers to a foreign or second language that learners are studying in a formal setting.

Methods

To provide a comprehensive overview of how LD has been measured and its impact on language learning outcomes, we conducted a systematic review. The systematic review was conducted following established protocols (e.g., Moher et al., 2015; Pickering & Byrne, 2014; Polanin et al., 2017), based on predetermined search parameters and inclusion criteria to ensure methodological rigor and reproducibility. To maximize coverage of the emerging and interdisciplinary literature on LD and to reduce potential publication bias, the search was expanded to include grey literature alongside peer-reviewed publications.

Data collection

After developing the search protocol, based on current literature on LD, the systematic review was registered with the International Database of Education Systematic Reviews (IDESR, 000109). Next, a thorough literature search was conducted, drawing on data from Web of Science, ProQuest, Scopus, and Google Scholar. These databases were selected based on alignment with systematic reviews previously published in education, linguistics, and psychology. Additionally, forward and backward citation tracking was conducted on the selected articles to identify further relevant studies.

Findings

In the following section, we discuss the findings from the data presented in Table 3.

Involved countries and tested languages

The data indicates that 28 studies were conducted on data from a single country, while seven studies present cross-country investigations.

In most studies, the samples represent a wide linguistic diversity. LD measures were used to compare between two (Muñoz et al., 2018) to languages from 127 countries (Van Der Slik & Van Hout, 2026) and the country's majority language. Studies generally compared the distance of two languages; only two studies also computed LD between participants' L2 and the third language (L3) (Schepens et al., 2016; Van Der Slik, 2010).

Data samples

The data samples analyzed in the reviewed articles show significant variation. Sample sizes range from 50 (Crossley et al., 2019) to larger samples of 15 million (Van Der Slik & Van Hout, 2026). While some studies collected their own data (Crossley et al., 2019; Lindgren & Muñoz, 2013), Others used secondary large-scale data (e.g., Van Der Slik & Van Hout, 2026).

Age groups.

The studies exhibit significant variation in participants' ages. Some studies focus on specific age groups, such as toddlers (Floccia et al., 2018; Sussman et al., 2023), children between six and eleven (Blom et al., 2020; Jaekel et al., 2023; Jaekel, et al., 2024; Jaekel et al., 2025; Lindgren & Muñoz, 2013; Muñoz et al., 2018; Vogel & Stang-Rabrig, 2025), teenagers (Berthele & Vanhove, 2020; Borgonovi & Ferrara, 2020; Elder & Davies, 1998; Kuperman, 2025), or adults (Oksuz et al., 2025; Schepens et al., 2013b Schepens et al., , 2013a Schepens et al., , 2020 Schepens et al., , 2023;; Van der Slik et al., 2019; Van Der Slik & Van Hout, 2026) while in other investigations, the samples included both child/ teenager and adult data (Chai & Bao, 2023; Chai & Ma, 2022; Chiswick & Miller, 2005; Crossley et al., 2019; Isphording & Otten, 2011 , 2014). Among the 35 reviewed studies, a wide range of approaches to operationalize LD between HLs and TLs were used (Tables 3 and 4). One key distinction between these measures is that some represent continuous distance metrics (e.g., phonological, lexical, morphological, typological, complexity-based, and learnability measures), whereas others rely on categorical groupings of linguistic background information. A further distinction concerns their theoretical basis: some lexical and grammatical measures are derived from direct linguistic similarities and differences between languages (e.g., ASJP, cognate distance, typological distance), whereas others are based on genetic or phylogenetic relationships between languages (e.g., phylogenetic lexical distance, genetically informed syntactic distance, or language family membership).

The analyzed studies differ further in the number of LD measures they employed. Across 21 studies, a single LD measure was computed, with 14 using continuous measures and 9 employing categorical classifications.

The remaining 14 studies compared or combined multiple LD measures within the same study. These combinations ranged from comparisons between two conceptually similar measures, such as alternative lexical distance indices (e.g., Schepens et al., 2013b; Van Der Slik, 2010), to broader multimethod approaches combining up to five or six measures from different linguistic domains, including both categorical and continuous measures (Isphording & Otten, 2014; Oksuz et al., 2025).

Lexical distance

Lexical distance is the most prevalent LD measure and was measured in 24 studies. Several different lexical distance measures were employed, differing in the type of lexical input data (wordlist vs. cognates) and computational approach (automated vs. nonautomated):

A prominent tool is the Automated Similarity Judgment Program (ASJP), which calculates the lexical distance between languages and was developed by Wichmann et al. (2025). Lexical distance, based on the ASJP, computes the Levenshtein distance between a modified 40-word Swadesh list (Swadesh, 1952), argued to be universal and culturally independent. To account for word length variations, the ASJP algorithm uses a normalization process by dividing the Levenshtein distance by the character count of the longer word, resulting in the Levenshtein Distance Normalized (LDN) score (Wichmann et al., 2010). Further refinement through an additional normalization process, designated as Levenshtein Distance Normalized Divided (LDND), adjusts for random similarities between lexical items (Wichmann et al., 2010).

To quantify LD as a single measure in seven studies, the ASJP was used (Chai & Ma, 2022; Isphording & Otten, 2011; Jaekel et al., 2023 Jaekel et al., , 2024 Jaekel et al., , 2025;; Mavrou & Chao, 2023; Vogel & Stang-Rabrig, 2025), and in combination with other measures in five studies (Berthelé et al., 2022; Borgonovi & Ferrara, 2020; Isphording & Otten, 2014; Oksuz et al., 2025; Schepens et al., 2013b).

In three studies, alternative lexical distance measures based on wordlists were used (Floccia et al., 2018; Kuperman, 2025; Oksuz et al., 2025). Kuperman (2025) computed lexical distance, focusing on the similarity of consonant-structure similarity, and applied stochastic modeling proposed by Beaufils & Tomin (2020), which is based on the comparison of 18 words from a non-standardized word list. In contrast, Floccia et al. (2018) adapted a non-automated approach by manually selecting age-specific vocabulary and computing the lexical distance based on the phono-lexical. Beyond standardized word-list approaches, lexical similarity has also been computed using scores derived from lexical databases that capture the proportion of similar words between language pairs based on cognate relationships (Bella et al., 2021). The resulting score ranges from 0 to 100 and reflects surface-level lexical similarity distance; this measure was used in Öksüz et al. ( 2025).

An alternative approach to computing the lexical distance relies not on the comparison of words but rather on shared cognates by counting shared cognates between languages: One of the cognate distance measures is based on comparing lexical items from a modified version of the Swadesh list (1952) and the percentage of shared cognates (Dyen et al., 1992; McMahon & McMahon, 2005) and was employed by two studies (Lindgren & Muñoz, 2013; Van Der Slik, 2010). The other presents a cognate recognition index that quantifies an individual's ability to identify cognates by calculating the ratio of correctly recognized cognate words to the total number of cognate words presented in auditory form and was used in one study (Muñoz et al., 2018). This metric indicates one's ability to discern lexical similarities across languages when exposed to spoken cognates.

Phonological and Morphological Distance: Feature-based Index Method

Moving beyond lexical distance, several studies have computed phonological and/or morphological distances based on typological characteristics using an index method. These approaches are similar in that they compare a selected set of phonological, morphological, or syntactic features, typically drawn from language databases such as the WALS (Dryer & Haspelmath, 2013) for various language features (e.g., Berthelé et al., 2022; Chai & Bao, 2023; Isphording & Otten, 2014; Schepens et al., 2016) or PHOIBLE (Moran & McCloy, 2019) for particular phonetic features (e.g. Oksuz et al., 2025; Schepens et al., 2020; Van Hout & Van Der Slik, 2025). Others use alternative feature classification approaches (Elder & Davies, 1998).

Phonological distance:

To estimate the phonological distance, differences between the phoneme inventories of languages are compared, typically drawing on databases such as PHOIBLE (Moran & McCloy, 2019). Schepens and colleagues (2020) proposed to operationalize phonological distance through three measures: new sounds, capturing phonological categories present in the TL but absent in the learner's HL, missing sounds, referring to categories present in the HL but not in the TL, and different sounds, representing the overall number of nonoverlapping phonological categories between the two languages. Subsequent studies have adopted this framework and most commonly operationalize phonological distance as the number of new sounds (Oksuz et al., 2025; Schepens et al., 2023; Van Der Slik & Van Hout, 2026; Van Hout & Van Der Slik, 2025).

Typological distance:

To compute the typological distance between the HL and TL, selected typological characteristics are coded as present or absent. Shared features are assigned a value of 0 and differing features a value of 1. The resulting values are averaged to obtain a continuous distance measure. The number of selected features and the composition of language samples vary considerably across studies.

Two studies analyzed a large number of typological characteristics, including phonological, morphological, and syntactic features from WALS. Chai and Bao (2023) estimated a mean value of 153 phonological, morphological, and syntactic features and classified the 24 languages according to this number into three groups. Similarly, Isphording & Otten (2014) analyzed 144 features of linguistic (dis)similarity, yielding scores ranging from 0 to 1.

In combination with a lexical LD, the morphological typological distance was used in three studies with a different number of analyzed features. One typological feature was compared between languages, such as word order typology (Floccia et al., 2018; Kim & Lee, 2010), or the presence or absence of articles (Oksuz et al., 2025). And Elder and Davies (1998) analyzed the differences of three features: subject/verb/object order, topic-prominence/subject-prominence, and pragmatic word-order/grammatical word-order.

Morphological complexity:

A second approach to computing the morphological distance based on language features is the morphological complexity approach. Linguistic features of an HL are coded relative to a TL in terms of their complexity. Lupyan and Dale (2010) proposed a set of 29 WALS features (Dryer & Haspelmath, 2013) ordered along a complexity scale.

Based on these features, Schepens et al. (2013a) calculated three morphological complexitybased measures: similarity, increasing complexity, and decreasing complexity. In six subsequent studies, the complexity-based approach with 23 to 29 linguistic features was applied (Schepens et al., 2016 (Schepens et al., , 2020 (Schepens et al., , 2023;; Van der Slik et al., 2019; Van Der Slik & Van Hout, 2026; Van Hout & Van Der Slik, 2025). Berthelé et al. (2022) used a similar but simplified version with eight morphological features to estimate comparative morphological complexity between 40 HLs and French. In one study, language complexity was operationalized using a broad typological classification of languages as analytic/isolating, fusional, agglutinative, or polysynthetic (Floccia et al., 2018).

Genetic Distance

An alternative approach to computing the LD relies not on the similarity or dissimilarity of words or syntactic features between languages, but on their genetic relation.

Genetic lexical distance is assessed through the phylogenetic cognate distance, which is based on genetic relations between languages following the approach proposed by Gray and Atkinson (2003), and reflects the differences between languages based on their evolutionary history.

In phylogenetic language family trees, lexical distance refers to evolutionary divergence between Indo-European languages based on shared cognates. This distance is quantified by calculating the total branch lengths connecting two languages within a tree (Gray & Atkinson, 2003). The maximum lexical distance occurs between languages from different families. Higher values indicate longer branch lengths and smaller lexical overlap; a value of 0 indicates that languages share all words in the Swadesh list (Schepens et al., 2016; Swadesh, 1952). The genetic lexical LD was calculated in eight studies (Oksuz et al., 2025; Schepens et al., 2013b Schepens et al., , 2016 Schepens et al., , 2020 Schepens et al., , 2023;; Van Der Slik, 2010; Van Der Slik & Van Hout, 2026; Van Hout & Van Der Slik, 2025).

In contrast, focusing on grammar-based structural relatedness between languages, Öksüz et al.

(2025) employed syntactic distance measures based on the Parametric Comparison Method (Longobardi & Guardiano, 2009). These measures, derived from Baker (2021) and Ceolin et al.

(2020), quantify differences between languages using binary syntactic parameter settings, capture variation in underlying grammatical structure. This approach is comparable to phylogenetic lexical distance measures (Gray & Atkinson, 2003), but operates at the syntactic level, reflecting deeper structural differences and representing phylogenetically informed distance measures. Both approaches model historical relationships between languages rather than surface-level similarity.

Borgonovi and Ferrara (2020) used a broader genealogical approach, which derives LD from language family classifications rather than direct linguistic features (Spolaore & Wacziarg, 2016). The genealogical LD is a tree-based genealogical distance measure that captures historical relatedness between languages based on their position within language family trees. A related approach is the TREE measure proposed by Isphording and Otten (2014), which also captures genetic relatedness between languages, but derives distance from the hierarchical structure of language family trees. Specifically, LD is operationalized as the number of branches separating two languages, resulting in a more fine-grained measure than simple family membership while not relying on lexical or structural linguistic features. Other studies operationalize genetic distance more coarsely by assigning languages to nine categories based on language family membership (Kim & Lee, 2010).

Learnability index

In two studies, learnability indices were computed based on difficulty scores and the average time needed by language learners to acquire English (Chiswick & Miller, 2005; Crossley et al., 2019; Isphording & Otten, 2014). The authors outlined that to address LD, it is not enough to trace the evolution of languages through language trees, but to ask how difficult the language is to learn for individuals, i.e., how much time it takes to learn a language.

Regression Coefficients

An alternative approach to operationalizing LD is to estimate language-or language-familyspecific regression coefficients rather than computing a continuous LD measure. Sussman et al., (2023) treated home language as a nominal predictor, with English as the reference category, and estimated language-specific effects on children's language and literacy development. In contrast, Çobanoğlu Aktan and İnan (2017) grouped learners' languages into nine language families and estimated regression coefficients for writing, reading, and grammar achievement. These approaches provide insight into differences between languages or language families, but they do not quantify LD on a continuous scale.

Categorical grouping

Other studies grouped languages into broad categories rather than computing continuous distance measures, for example, distinguishing between English monolinguals, multilinguals literate in an alphabetic L1, and multilinguals literate in a logographic or syllabic L1 (Bérubé & Marinova-Todd, 2012), or classifying languages into categories such as small versus large LD (Blom et al., 2020) or by language similarity according to the Foreign Service Institute classification (Hartshorn et al., 2023). While these approaches provide some insight into cross-linguistic differences, they do not constitute continuous measures of LD in a strict sense and are therefore not considered further in this review.

Areal division

LD has also been assessed by non-linguistic features, such as categorizing immigrants' home countries based on their geographic distance from the U.S, including English-speaking countries, Mexico, other Western Hemisphere countries, other continental European countries, Africa and the Middle East, South Asia, and East Asia (Carliner, 2000).

What is the influence of linguistic distance on second and foreign language acquisition outcomes?

The associations between LD and language learning outcomes were examined across a range of language skills, including reading, writing, speaking, and listening, as well as broader measures of language proficiency, such as grammar and vocabulary knowledge (Table 3). These effects were observed across diverse populations, from a few-month-old toddlers (Sussman et al., 2023) to 80-year-old adults (Chai & Ma, 2022) and across a range of HLs and TLs. The findings consistently showed that greater LD is associated with lower language proficiency across different skills. Table 3 illustrates that various measures and methods were employed to quantify language learning outcomes in terms of LD. The analysis of the effects of LD measures on different language skills also demonstrates that these measures differ in their explanatory power, which is explored in greater detail in the following sections.

Reading skills

Various LD measures were used to assess reading skills in thirteen studies. Across eleven studies, LD consistently showed an effect on reading proficiency (Table A).

In seven of these studies, lexical distance was measured by the ASJP (Wichmann et al., 2025) and six studies report an effect of LD on reading skills (Berthelé et al., 2022; Borgonovi & Ferrara, 2020; Chai & Ma, 2022; Jaekel et al., 2023; Jaekel et al., 2024; Vogel & Stang-Rabrig, 2025), while one study did not (Jaekel et al., 2025). Lexical LD based on cognate distance was also shown to affect reading outcomes (Lindgren & Muñoz, 2013).

In two studies, ASJP-based lexical distance was compared with other LD measures, including morphological complexity (Berthelé et al., 2022) and genealogical LD (Borgonovi & Ferrara, 2020). However, only the latter showed effects comparable to those found for lexical LD.

Similarly, in comparison to phylogenetic lexical LD, phonological similarity and morphological complexity did not show an effect on reading (Schepens et al., 2023).

Estimating the LD through belonging to one of nine language families in combination with word order typology, Kim and Lee (2010) also found an effect on L2 EFL reading skills. Kuperman (2025) examined the effect of LD on reading outcomes in an eye-tracking experiment and reported an effect of phonological-lexical similarity measure on gaze duration, total reading time, and reading rate. In contrast, categorizing languages based on three grammatical features did not predict reading skills (Elder & Davies, 1998).

Writing skills

The effect of LD on writing skills was assessed in eight studies. Six studies found an interaction between LD and writing skills (Table A). Various lexical LD measures were associated with writing skills in all studies, including the ASJP distance (Berthelé et al., 2022; Mavrou & Chao, 2023) the cognate distance (Van Der Slik, 2010) and phylogenetic distance (Schepens et al., 2023; Van Der Slik, 2010). Van der Slik (2010) compared lexical LD measures based on the lexical cognate and genetic distances and reported that the cognate distance measure had greater predictive power than the phylogenetic distance.

Additionally, lexical distance was compared with morphological complexity (Berthelé et al., 2022; Schepens et al., 2023) and both studies showed a smaller effect than the lexical distance.

While phonological distance did not predict writing skills (Schepens et al., 2023).

One study estimated regression coefficients for writing performance across language families and found significant differences among the language-family groups (Çobanoğlu Aktan & İnan, 2017). Alternative LD measures, including dividing languages into nine language families (Kim & Lee, 2010), also showed positive associations with writing skills, while grouping languages into three categories did not predict writing skills (Elder & Davies, 1998; Hartshorn et al., 2023).

Listening skills

In seven studies, listening and comprehension skills were tested, all of which reported associations with diverse LD measures (Table A). Lexical distance measured by ASJP (Berthelé et al., 2022; Jaekel et al., 2023 Jaekel et al., , 2025)), cognates (Lindgren & Muñoz, 2013), and phylogenetic distance (Schepens et al., 2023) all showed an effect on listening and comprehension outcomes.

While Berthele et al. (2022) reported additionally an smaller effect of morphological complexity than lexical distance, Schepens et al. (2023) did not find an effect of morphological complexity and phonological distance on listening skills.

Alternative operationalizations of LD, through the division into nine language groups (Kim & Lee, 2010), or three categories (Bérubé & Marinova-Todd, 2012) have also shown interactions with listening and comprehension skills.

Speaking skills

Speaking skills were examined in twelve studies using various LD measures, of which eleven reported an effect (Table A). Positive effects were reported for lexical LD measures, including ASJP, cognate-based, and phylogenetic lexical distance (Berthelé et al., 2022; Schepens et al., 2013a Schepens et al., , 2013b Schepens et al., , 2016 Schepens et al., , 2020 Schepens et al., , 2023;; Van Der Slik, 2010) phonological distance (Schepens et al., 2020 (Schepens et al., , 2023) ) and morphological complexity measures (Schepens et al., 2013a (Schepens et al., , 2016 (Schepens et al., , 2020 (Schepens et al., , 2023;; Van der Slik et al., 2019) and learnability indices (Chiswick & Miller, 2005; Crossley et al., 2019). While Berthelé et al., (2022) did not find an effect of morphological complexity on speaking skills. In contrast, broader proxy measures such as areal geographic distance (Carliner, 2000) and categorical groupings based on Foreign Service Institute classifications (Hartshorn et al., 2023) did not show significant effects.

Other language outcomes

One study examined the influence of LD on an aggregated measure of English reading and listening proficiency. Van Hout and Van der Slik (2025) assessed lexical, morphological, and phonological distance separately and as a composite LD measure. Lexical and phonological distance were negatively associated with English proficiency, whereas the association with morphological distance was not significant. When the three measures were combined into a composite LD measure, greater LD significantly predicted lower overall English proficiency.

Thus, the findings indicate a negative effect of LD on combined reading and listening proficiency, although the relative contribution of the individual LD dimensions differed.

Across thirteen studies, language proficiency was assessed beyond reading, writing, listening, and speaking skills, from broader measures such as general language proficiency and literacy to more specific skills including grammar, vocabulary, and article use.

Overall language proficiency was assessed for instance, through cross-country TOEFL scores and were found to be associated with morphosyntactic, phonological, lexical, and phylogenetic lexical LD (Van Der Slik & Van Hout, 2026) and LD based on language families in combination with word order typology (Kim & Lee, 2010). Significant effects of various LD measures were also found in two studies on self-reported language proficiency (Isphording & Otten, 2011 , 2014). Chai and Bao (2023) reported a proficiency-dependent effect of LD on Chinese character, vocabulary, and grammar acquisition by classifying 24 HLs into three groups according to the LD value evaluated by 153 phonological, morphological, and syntactic features.

ASJP-based lexical LD was associated with general vocabulary outcomes (Vogel & Stang-Rabrig, 2025), and for expressive and receptive vocabulary, Floccia and colleagues (2018) reported that while LD based on phonological-lexical overlap predicted expressive vocabulary, morphosyntactic distance (word order and morphological complexity) was associated with receptive vocabulary. Using categorical language-group classifications, Blom et al. (2020) found lower receptive vocabulary scores for the large linguistic distance group than for the smalldistance and monolingual groups. Similarly, Bérubé and Marinova-Todd (2012) reported differences in receptive vocabulary performance across language groups, with multilingual children literate in an alphabetic L1 outperforming those literate in a logographic or syllabic L1. Sussman et al. (2023) evaluated overall performance on receptive vocabulary, responsiveness to language, communication and conversation abilities, and literacy interest among pre-school English learners in the US, by computing regression coefficients. The authors did not report a linear effect of LD on language learning outcomes but outlined significant differences between speakers of different L1s., e.g., Spanish-speaking children demonstrated higher levels of language and literacy development compared to those speaking Arabic or Mandarin, especially in the earlier stages of development.

At the level of specific skills, Muñoz et al. (2018) discussed significant effects on grammar using a cognate recognition index. Regression coefficients estimated by language family also revealed differences in grammar performance between language-family groups (Çobanoğlu Aktan & İnan, 2017). And for article use, Oksuz et al. (2025) found significant effects for syntactic and ASJPbased lexical distance, while morphological, typological, and phonological measures did not reach significance.

Discussion

This systematic review aimed to identify the specific measures used to assess LD and to evaluate the association of LD with language learning outcomes, addressing the two research questions: 1.

How has linguistic distance been measured? 2. How is linguistic distance associated with second and foreign language acquisition outcomes?

How has linguistic distance been measured and what are the Advantages and disadvantages of the different LD measures

The review outlines that, within the parameters set in the systematic review protocol in advance, 35 studies used LD measures as predictors for language attainment. While various approaches have been taken, lexical distance measures and those based on the index method have been favored. The analysis further demonstrated that each LD measure has its own advantages and limitations, which are discussed in more detail in the next section.

Among the 35 reviewed studies, a wide range of approaches was used to operationalize LD between home and target languages (Tables 3 and 4). One key distinction concerns the form of operationalization: some measures represent continuous distance metrics (e.g., phonological, lexical, morphological, typological, complexity-based, and learnability measures), whereas others rely on categorical classifications of linguistic background, such as language family groupings. A further distinction relates to their theoretical basis. Some measures are derived from direct linguistic similarities and differences between languages, such as lexical overlap, phonological similarity, grammatical structure, or typological features (e.g., ASJP, cognatebased measures, typological distance). Others are based on historical or genetic relationships among languages, including phylogenetic lexical distance, genetically informed syntactic distance, and genealogical language family approaches. Importantly, genetic relatedness and linguistic similarity are distinct constructs: languages may be structurally similar without sharing a recent common ancestor, whereas genetically related languages may have diverged substantially over time (Georgi et al., 2010).

LD measures based on language similarity are generally more interpretable and more readily relatable to language teaching contexts than measures based on genetic distance.

One key advantage of automated lexical distance measures (e.g., ASJP) is that they provide continuous metrics rather than broad categorical classifications. A further strength is their scalability, as they can be efficiently applied to large datasets covering a wide range of languages. These LD measures lend themselves as alternatives to binary language status variables such as 'native' vs 'non-native' (Jaekel et al., 2024). The main disadvantage is that only lexical similarities and dissimilarities are considered, while other language characteristics, such as morphological and syntactical levels, are overlooked. For instance, if Chinese and Turkish speakers learn Finnish, both HLs might not share any or very few cognates with Finnish, but since Turkish and Finnish belong to the group of agglutinating languages and share morphological characteristics, Turkish speakers might still benefit from their metalinguistic knowledge and experience facilitative language transfer (Westergaard et al., 2017) beyond the lexical level.

The index method may help overcome these limitations by enabling the evaluation and comparison of phonological, morphological, and syntactic properties across languages. Doing so promises to provide a broader view of similarities and differences in language. The primary disadvantage of the index method is the lack of comprehensive guidelines for feature selection and coding. Furthermore, even large language databases like the WALS (Dryer & Haspelmath, 2013) and Grambank (Skirgård et al., 2023) do not cover all language properties with the same precision, and the involvement of various language experts is necessary. Additionally, researchers need to evaluate a large number of items and select the relevant ones. The absence of a systematic framework leads to a high degree of diversity in the chosen characteristics, as well as in scoring systems, which adds to the complexity of comparing results on the effect of LD on learning outcomes.

Another approach to computing the LD between languages was based on genetic relationships and not on similarities and differences. Whether phylogenetic measures are as useful in the current context is a matter for debate. The positive language transfer effect described by research shows that learners can benefit from similarities between HL and TL (e.g., Berthelé et al., 2022; Westergaard et al., 2017) and not from their relatedness. This is also shown in Van der Slik (2010) as the explanatory power of the cognate LD measure based on similarities was much greater than that of the genetic LD measure. In comparing the ASJP lexical distance, morphological distance based on the index method, the relatedness of languages through family trees, and the learnability index, the ASJP LD showed the strongest and most consistent effects, while morphological distance, family tree membership and learnability index showed a weaker effect (Isphording & Otten, 2014).

Alternative ways to assess the association of HL and TL were conducted through the learnability indices (Chiswick & Miller, 2005; Crossley et al., 2019) and regression analysis of language developmental trajectories (Çobanoğlu Aktan & İnan, 2017; Sussman et al., 2023). Both methods provide good estimates to measure the effect of LD on language skills. The main limitation of the learnability indices is that these measures are only available for English as the language of origin.

Some other studies relied on broad categorical groupings of linguistic background or language similarity rather than continuous LD measures (Bérubé & Marinova-Todd, 2012; Blom et al., 2020; Hartshorn et al., 2023), which may offer general insights into cross-linguistic differences but provide a less precise operationalization of LD.

How is linguistic distance associated with second and foreign language acquisition outcomes and how to choose a linguistic distance measure in language learning research?

Our analysis consistently revealed that LD is significantly associated with language learning outcomes. This effect is evident across various HL and TL pairs, datasets, age groups, and language skills tested, in both foreign and second language learning contexts.

The current systematic review has shown that LD measures are informative and helpful estimates to understand the impact of an HL on a TL and highlights the importance of selecting a measure and method that aligns with the specific research question and the language skills being assessed.

The challenge of capturing LD arises from the inherent complexity of languages. Comparing the reliability of various LD measures in explaining variation in language learning outcomes, the lexical distance seems to be a reliable estimate for evaluating how an HL affects TL acquisition and development (e.g., Jaekel et al., 2024; Schepens et al., 2016; Van Der Slik, 2010).

The ASJP provides an efficient preliminary estimation method for LD, accommodating a larger range of languages without requiring extensive additional labor or resources.

For a more detailed and targeted analysis of the differences between languages, the index method could be applied. For LD measures based on the index method, it is essential to consider a skillsbased approach and a careful selection of features and coding systems grounded in theory, such as school curricula or specific research aims. In the current systematic review, articles analyzed using the index method differ in two ways: the number of language-specific features and the coding and analysis methods. The selected features were coded according to two different approaches: I) presence or absence of a language characteristic to compute the typological distance (e.g., Chai & Bao, 2023; Elder & Davies, 1998), and II) based on complexity differences between the HL and TL by evaluating the selected features of whether they are more, equal, or less complex (e.g., Berthelé et al., 2022; Schepens et al., 2016).

Computing the typological LD (I), the selection of analyzed language characteristics varied between three (Elder & Davies, 1998) and 153 (Chai & Bao, 2023) features. Elder & Davies (1998) did not find an effect of LD, raising questions about how informative an LD measure based on only three grammatical features can be in explaining the influence of LD, unless tests are specifically geared towards these selective items. On the other hand, using an extensive list of 153 features in Chai and Bao (2023) it can be challenging for a large sample of languages to gather the needed information and/or language-specific expertise. It is also necessary to discuss whether such an extensive list of features is needed. However, none of the authors used the sum values but grouped the languages further into three to five groups, converting a range of continuous variables to a few categorical variables, without elaborating on their reasoning. This approach seems contradictory to the idea of LD as a continuous measure to overcome binary classification or categorization.

Measuring LD based on complexity differences between HL and TL (II), the authors employed The comparison reveals that the number of features and the analysis method influence the explanatory power of the LD measure. The results suggest the need for a careful selection of characteristics and the use of LD measures as continuous values.

Furthermore, to better understand the influence of HL on TL, the selection of features from the orthographical, phonological, morphological, and syntactic levels should be specifically aligned with the skills necessary for the language competencies being assessed.

For example, when testing reading skills, key factors include phonological skills, vocabulary knowledge, the orthographic systems, and grapheme-phoneme correspondence.

LD measures that emphasize these features tend to yield more informative results compared to those that focus on aspects not directly related to the tested skills. The current systematic review supports this view. Testing, for instance, the effects of LD on reading skills, LD measures based on grammatical properties do not have an effect (Elder & Davies, 1998).

Alternative methods for estimating LD, such as the Learnability Index and Regression Coefficients, create challenges for cross-study comparison due to their methodological differences. These approaches are further limited by their applicability to only a restricted set of languages, preventing their implementation in broader linguistic contexts.

Conclusion

This systematic review underscores the significant role of LD in second and foreign language acquisition outcomes, highlighting the diverse methods of measuring LD and its consistent association with language learning. These measures prove to be valuable predictors of language proficiency across various skills, including vocabulary knowledge, grammatical skills, reading, writing, listening, and speaking.

The findings demonstrate that lexical distance measures, particularly those based on cognates and the ASJP, are widely employed due to their transparent and systematic approach to quantifying language similarities and differences as well as their ease of implementation. To capture a broader understanding of the distance between languages, the index methods can be applied on the phonological, morphological, and syntactic levels.

The study advocates for a more nuanced approach to LD measurement, particularly in alignment with specific language skills being assessed. Ultimately, the research emphasizes the need for further refinement in LD measures, tailored to specific learning contexts, to deepen our understanding of how LD influences second and foreign language outcomes.
