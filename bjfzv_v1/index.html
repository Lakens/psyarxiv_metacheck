Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard), also Internal Review Board - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term); not IEC followed by a number, which is a technical standard - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee/Commission, in the singular or plural - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee, Bioethics Committee/Commission, Ethikkommission - Ethical Review Authority/Unit/Office/Agency - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Human Research Protection Office/Program, Office for Human Subjects Protection - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee(s) on/for (Medical and Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt - Ethics review that was not needed: Ethics review was not required, did not require further ethical review, exempt from formal human research ethics review, no new institutional ethics review

A template line the authors have not filled in (“[Add ethics approval … here.]”) is not a statement.

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

When a statement is expected

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. A TRO uses them for each trov:TransparentResearchPerformance. * run_results$exit_status: the exit status of the process that ran the script (0 = ended normally); NA when the process did not report one (a time limit, or Docker failing to start the container). * artifacts: one row per file, with stage (deposit: as downloaded from the repository, hashed from the downloaded file; before_run: the run folder just before the first script ran, after metacheck’s own changes, such as rewritten paths and unpacked archives; after_run: only the files the scripts made (status new), changed (changed) or removed (removed), as an unchanged file is the one listed before the run), path (relative to the run folder, as valid UTF-8, a byte of another encoding written as <xx>), size (bytes), sha256, mime_type (by extension, NA when unknown), source (the file’s address in the repository, for deposit). The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). Files metacheck itself copied or made between passes (see modifications, output_copied, file_placed) show in after_run too. A TRO uses the rows as trov:ResearchArtifacts and each stage as a trov:ArtifactArrangement. * modifications$sha256_before, modifications$sha256_after: the hash of the changed script as downloaded and as it ran; NA when the script is not at the same path in both (a script made from an R Markdown file). * environment: the system that ran the code (trov:TransparentResearchSystem in a TRO): metacheck_version, started_at, ended_at, host (R version, platform, operating system), sandbox, network_isolated (TRUE only when every Docker run had --network none; the process sandbox does not stop the network), internet, timeout_per_script, total_timeout, python_local (the Python run outside Docker and its version), and docker: runs (for each kind of run, r_run, r_install, python_run, python_install, the image and the docker run arguments, such as --network none, --read-only, --cap-drop ALL, no-new-privileges, the process limit and the memory and processor limits) and images (for each image, its ID and digests from docker image inspect, its creation time and the R and Python versions its environment declares).

run_results$checked says what the run of each script shows about the code, in five groups, so that a script that does not reproduce is kept apart from one the check could not test: ran (it ran to the end); code_error (it did not reproduce: it stopped with an error of the code or the deposit, could not be read, or needs a file the deposit does not hold); dependency (a package or program was not available: a package that could not be installed or was not loaded, a program outside R and Python, a system library, or R crashing in a package’s compiled code); time (it did not finish within the time limit for one script, or was not started because the time for all scripts had run out); and not_checkable (it could not be checked automatically: it needs a person, the internet, a key or another setting of the authors’ computer, one platform such as Google Colab, Windows or OpenSAFELY, more memory than the run allows, a file that was not downloaded, a screen, or a program that runs it, or it is a file of a package of the code that Python runs only as a module of that package). It is NA for files run inside another script, files that are not an analysis, files not read, and a Docker failure. summary_table counts each group (repro_ran_n, repro_code_error_n, repro_dependency_n, repro_time_n, repro_not_checkable_n), and the report’s first sentence gives them. The traffic light reflects the static readiness signals by default; a script in code_error that stopped with an error makes it red, as a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure, and a script in one of the other three groups keeps it from being green. Calls that need a screen, which the run does not have (dev.print(), dev.copy2pdf(), dev.copy2eps(), savePlot(), windows(), win.graph(), x11(), quartz(), View()), are replaced before the run by calls that need none, which modifications records as screen_call_replaced. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

SELF-IDENTITY & TRAUMA-AND STRESSOR-RELATED PSYCHOPATHOLOGY: A SYSTEMATIC REVIEW

The category of trauma-and stressor-related disorders, first introduced in the DSM-5 and updated in DSM-5-TR, is unique within the DSM system in that disorders within this category develop only after exposure to disruptive life events such as potentially lifethreatening accidents, disasters, acts of violence, or the death of a significant other (American Psychiatric Association, 2013 , 2022; Benjet et al., 2016; Contractor et al., 2018). Exposure to the events that give rise to trauma-and stressor-related disorders can fundamentally challenge how individuals understand themselves and their place in the world, disrupting valued goals, threatening important roles, challenging long-standing beliefs, and altering expectations for the future (Conway & Pleydell-Pearce, 2000; Ehlers & Clark, 2000; Janoff-Bulman, 1989; Papa et al., 2014; Park et al., 2010). However, there is substantial variability in individual outcomes following exposure to such events: between 50-70% of affected people report few or no ongoing symptoms (Galatzer-Levy et al., 2018), while lifetime prevalence rates for PTSD are estimated at 5.6% (Koenen et al., 2017) and PGD at 7-10% (Lundorff et al., 2017). This has prompted considerable interest in understanding the factors that contribute to this variability.

Many prominent theoretical models that seek to explain the development and maintenance of PTSD and PGD (e.g., Berntsen & Rubin, 2006; Boelen et al., 2006; Conway & Pleydell-Pearce, 2000; Ehlers & Clark, 2000; Janoff-Bulman, 1989; Maccallum & Bryant, 2013) propose that disturbances in the self are central to both the development and maintenance of distress. For example, cognitive models emphasise the role of negative appraisals regarding the implications of adverse events for the self (Ehlers & Clark, 2000; Janoff-Bulman, 1989) whereas memory-based models highlight the challenge of integrating disruptive experiences into existing identity structures (Berntsen & Rubin, 2006; Boelen et al., 2006; Maccallum & Bryant, 2013). But while there are well-developed and coherent literatures focused on appraisals and coping responses, significantly less attention has been paid to specifying the underlying self-related processes. An exception to this is the self-memory system model (SMS, Conway & Pleydell-Pearce, 2000), which proposes that adaptation following major life events requires simultaneously accommodating new event-related information that may challenge existing goals, roles, and self-representations, while maintaining a coherent sense of identity over time. Distress and dysfunction are thought to persist when a discrepancy between these two objectives cannot be resolved (Conway et al., 2004; Conway, 2005; see also Boelen et al., 2006; Ehlers & Clark, 2000; Janoff-Bulman, 1989; Maccallum & Bryant, 2013).

Despite this theoretical importance, research examining self-identity in trauma-and stressor-related psychopathology has developed in a fragmented manner. A search of the literature reveals a wide range of terms and constructs including event centrality, self-concept clarity, identity disruption, identity processing, self-discrepancy, self-diversity, and selfconstrual (e.g., Bellet et al., 2020; Berntsen & Rubin, 2006; Engelbrecht & Jobson, 2020; Holt et al., 2018; Maccallum, 2022; Papa & Lancaster, 2016). The extent to which these constructs represent distinct or overlapping conceptual constructs is unclear. Moreover, much of the literature has focussed on the single construct of event centrality (e.g., Berntsen & Rubin, 2006; Gehrt et al, 2018).

To better understand which aspects of self-identity have received empirical attention, how these constructs relate to one another, and the extent to which they align with existing theoretical models, we conducted a systematic review of the literature examining associations between self-identity constructs and trauma-and stressor-related psychopathology. Rather than focusing on event centrality, which has received attention elsewhere (Gehrt et al., 2018; James et al., under review), this review presents findings related to a broader range of self-related constructs. Specifically, we sought to identify: a) which self-identity constructs have been examined in relation to DSM-5-TR adult trauma-and stressor-related disorders (American Psychiatric Association, 2022), including PTSD, acute stress disorder (ASD), adjustment disorder (AjD), and prolonged grief disorder (PGD); b) how these constructs are associated with trauma-and stressor-related psychopathology; c) the extent to which these constructs align with theoretical models concerning the role of the self in adaptation following disruptive life events; and d) the extent to which existing findings support these theoretical models. By answering these questions, we aim to clarify the evidence base and provide a framework to guide future research in the context of trauma-and stressor-related disorders. The findings reported here comprise part of a larger project examining self-identity constructs in trauma-and stressor-related psychopathology. In the screening process, studies of event centrality represented the majority of eligible studies (over 80%) and formed a comparatively coherent literature suitable for quantitative synthesis; those findings are thus reported separately (James et al., under review). The present review focuses on other selfrelated constructs, including self-consistency, self-complexity, and identity processing styles, with the aim of mapping the more heterogeneous literature and examining how these constructs align with theoretical models of adaptation following disruptive life events. Classification decisions were guided by the construct definition and theoretical framework adopted by the original study authors, recognising that some self-related constructs share conceptual features.

Method

Study Selection

The selection process followed the recommendations of the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) checklist (Moher et al., 2009) and was mapped using the PRISMA-P chart (Page et al., 2021) (see Figure 1). The results of the literature search were imported into Covidence (Veritas Health Innovation, 2022). Following the removal of duplicates, all titles and abstracts were screened by the first author, with screening replicated by a team of three reviewers. Articles were excluded if they did not meet eligibility criteria. Discrepancies were resolved by team consensus. This same process was then followed for full-text screening.

Inclusion Criteria

Studies were included if a full-text English-language version was available. Crosssectional and longitudinal quantitative studies and randomized controlled trials were eligible for inclusion. Mixed-methods studies were only considered if data from the quantitative components could be clearly extracted.

The inclusion criteria in accordance with the PICO framework were: 1) Population: participants over the age of 18 (with the exception of university student samples, which may have included some participants under 18); 2) Intervention: self-identity factors quantifiably measured in relation to trauma-or stressor-related psychopathology (a complete list of selfidentity factors is included in Table 2); 3) Context: studies involving samples from any country or culture, community samples, targeted (e.g., occupational) populations, and clinical populations; 4) Outcomes: quantifiable scores on at least one measure of trauma-or stressorrelated psychopathology. Eligible measures included clinical interview protocols, diagnostic measures, and screening tools. Studies measuring prolonged grief were only included if participants were bereaved, as bereavement is an essential criterion for a PGD diagnosis.

However, studies of traumatic stress were included regardless of whether participants had experienced a Criterion A event as many studies included a mix of participants with exposure to varying event types.

The exclusion criteria were: 1) Population: studies of children or adolescents, or those including mixed-age samples where data were not presented in an age-stratified manner conducive to extraction of data only for eligible participants; 2) Intervention: self-related constructs that reference evaluations of self-concept (e.g., self-esteem, self-efficacy) or group membership (e.g., social identity); 3) Context: there were no context-specific criteria for exclusion; 4) Outcomes: studies in which trauma-or stressor-related psychopathology was not quantifiably measured.

Data Extraction and Preparation

After the screening process, the first author extracted data from all eligible articles and completed quality assessment. Duplicate data extraction and quality assessment were carried out by the third author. Discrepancies were resolved by consensus. The following data were extracted: study information (authors and year of publication), study aims, design, location (country), study population (e.g., student, occupational, treatment-seeking), number of participants, participant characteristics (age, gender, ethnicity), outcomes measured and measures used, self-identity factors measured and measures used, and effect sizes (large = r > .5, medium = r > .3, small = r > .1).

Data Synthesis

We derived a narrative synthesis in accordance with Cochrane's Synthesis without meta-analysis (SWiM) guidelines (Campbell et al., 2020). This involved thematic coding of text, development of descriptive themes (self-identity constructs and their relationship to outcomes), and generation of analytical themes (concepts that explain how self-identity constructs relate to outcomes). As studies frequently used different terminology and measures to examine conceptually related aspects of the self, an additional aim of the synthesis was to identify broader conceptual similarities and organise constructs into higher-order categories.

Quality Assessment

We assessed study quality using a modified and abbreviated version of the Appraisal tool for Cross-sectional Studies (AXIS tool, Downes et al., 2016). The AXIS tool was chosen as it was designed specifically to address study design, reporting quality, and risk of bias in cross-sectional studies, which comprised the majority of included studies. We modified the tool, removing items not relevant to quality of study design, methodology, and analytical rigour (see supplementary material: https://osf.io/bjm5e/?view_only=e42dc873d0a54537abf4e9b972b1d9c7). Each study was rated on the following criteria: 1) study design; 2) sampling strategy; 3) suitability of measures; and 4) adequacy of analysis. The first author completed the quality assessment in full, and the third author independently completed quality assessment on one third of included studies. Discrepancies were resolved by consensus. Overall quality ranged from 1 (lowest quality) to 14 (highest quality). The quality appraisal is represented in Table 2 with a single score for each study.

Results

Our search returned 5,479 results following removal of duplicates. Following title and abstract screening, 509 records were eligible for full-text screening. After full-text screening, 36 studies were included in this review. These included 34 independent datasets, due to overlap in three studies. There was also ambiguity regarding potential overlap in a further six studies, suggesting the overall sample size may be slightly inflated. The PRISMA flowchart is presented in Figure 1.

Descriptive Analysis of Studies

Table 1 presents an overview of the study characteristics. All studies were published between 2001-2025, with more than half published since 2017. Excluding known overlapping datasets, the studies reported on 7,488 participants. Participants came from nine different countries, with most of the studies involving participants based in the UK (11, 30.6%), USA (8, 22.2%), and Australia (8, 22.2%). They were sampled from a range of populations including community, student, treatment-seeking, military, and other occupations. Inclusion criteria for most (29, 80.6%) studies included exposure to an event that met threshold for potential diagnosis. Where age (33, 91.7%) and gender (36, 100%) were reported, mean participant age was 37.8 years, ranging from 16-81, and 72.5% were female. Similarly, only 16 studies (44.4%) reported on participants' ethnicity. Most studies (28, 77.8%) were focused on investigating traumatic stress and included a measure of traumatic stress psychopathology. Eight (22.2%) studies were focused on bereavement outcomes and included a measure of PGD; one of these also included a measure of traumatic stress psychopathology. This difference likely reflects the relative recency of the PGD diagnosis. No studies examined self-identity constructs in relation to acute stress disorder or adjustment disorder.

Psychopathology was most commonly indexed using a self-report measure. For traumatic stress these included the Patient Checklist (PCL-5) (11, 39.3%) and Posttraumatic Diagnostic Scale (PDS) (6, 21.4%), with all studies providing total scores and six (16.7%) reporting subscale or symptom cluster scores. For prolonged grief, the most commonly used measures were the Inventory of Complicated Grief (ICG) (4, 50%), including revised and abbreviated versions, and Prolonged Grief-13 scale (PG-13), including revised versions (3, 37.5%). Five (13.9%) studies used clinician-rated psychopathology to group participants for comparative purposes as either meeting diagnostic criteria or not.

Self-identity constructs were indexed using a range of measures and methods. The majority of studies (23, 63.9%) used a validated self-report scale such as the Self-Concept Clarity Scale (Campbell et al., 1996). A smaller number of studies (13, 36.1%) coded participants' responses on expressive writing tasks or memory recall tasks (e.g., Singer and Salovey's (1993) self-defining memory task), or free recall tasks (e.g., Kuhn and McPartland's (1954) Twenty Statements Test) in which participants list aspects of their self. For descriptive data relating to each study, see Table 2.

Quality Assessment of Studies

Study quality assessments are presented in Table 2. Only 22 studies (61.1%) described their sample characteristics in sufficient detail to enable replication (Appelbaum et al., 2018).

Only eight studies (22.2%) justified their sample size, with the majority of these published since 2020. No studies described recruiting a representative sample, four (11.1%) a somewhat representative sample, and 32 (88.9%) a non-representative or convenience sample. Most studies employed correlational designs; however, five (13.9%) studies employed a betweengroups design with a meaningful comparison group (e.g., participants grouped by diagnostic status, event exposure, culture, etc.). While the majority of studies were cross-sectional (32, 88.9%), four (11.1%) employed a longitudinal design. All studies had used validated measures (or translations of validated measures) to assess psychopathology and half used validated measures to assess self-identity factors (18, 50%). Most studies reported effect sizes (27, 75%).

Overall study quality scores ranged from 4 to 11 out of a maximum of 14, with a mean quality score of 7.9 (SD = 1.7).

Narrative Synthesis

Our narrative synthesis identified three overarching self-identity constructs among the studies. The first indexed the extent to which self-identity was experienced as consistent (selfconsistency: 18, 50%). The second encompassed the extent to which self-identity was complex and multifaceted (self-complexity: 16, 44.4%). The third, and much less-studied construct, measured ways in which information comprising one's self-identity is processed (self-identity processing: 3, 8.3%). However, within each of these categories, labels used to describe the constructs and methods used varied widely. Some studies indexed more than one identity construct; however, only one study (Papa & Lancaster, 2016) examined interactions between self-constructs. In presenting our synthesis, we first describe associations between self-identity and traumatic stress psychopathology, and then prolonged grief psychopathology.

Self-consistency

The largest group of studies measured concepts reflecting the extent to which one's self-identity was experienced as consistent. This included four related concepts: the extent to which the self is experienced as being internally consistent and stable across time; the extent to which the self is experienced as consistent across specific contexts; the extent to which the individual perceives inconsistencies or discrepancies between their actual self-identity and how they would like to be or think they should be; and the extent to which individuals hold personal preferences for a self that is consistent across roles and contexts.

Studies of Traumatic Stress.

Four studies, all cross-sectional, used the Self-Concept Clarity scale to measure the extent to which the self was experienced as internally consistent (Holt et al., 2018; Keshet & Gilboa-Schechtman, 2017 , 2019) and occupational samples (Pepper, 2022). All reported medium to large negative effect sizes, where lower scores on the self-report scale were associated with higher scores on measures of traumatic stress. Mitchell et al. (2020) examined a similar construct in veterans transitioning from military to civilian life using an expressive writing task. In this task, participants were asked to explore their thoughts and feelings about their transition, and these were then coded for references to identity disruption. A small positive association was observed between the proportion of responses that referenced identity disruptions (e.g., role disruption; disconnection between past, present, and future selves) and more severe traumatic stress symptoms over a six-month follow-up period. Webb and Jobson (2011) generated a 'self-consistency index' by asking traumaexposed students to rate themselves on 20 personality characteristics in general and across four specific relationships (e.g., partner, friendships). They found no association between selfconsistency and traumatic stress severity. A further six cross-sectional studies with traumaexposed community, student, and treatment-seeking populations examined self-consistency by using tasks that required participants to generate their own list of traits (Selves Questionnaire, the Self-Strength Guide, the Self-Description Questionnaire, the Self-Discrepancy Scale) -for example, traits they would ideally like to have (ideal self) or think they should have (ought self). They were then asked to rate the extent to which they felt they currently possessed these traits to provide a self-discrepancy index. Two studies found a medium to large association between greater self-discrepancy and more severe clinician-diagnosed PTSD (Engelbrecht & Jobson, 2020; Sutherland & Bryant, 2008), while three studies, which used participant selfratings of symptoms, found no association between self-discrepancy and traumatic stress (Engelbrecht & Jobson, 2020; Krans et al., 2017; Wessel et al., 2014). The final study, using participant self-ratings of symptoms, examined self-discrepancy in participants exposed to potentially morally injurious events and found a medium-sized association between ideal and ought self-discrepancy and more severe traumatic stress (James et al., 2024a). Boyraz et al. (2019) took a different approach to self-consistency, examining individual differences in the need or desire for one's self-concept to be consistent across roles and contexts, using the Dialectical Self Scale. They hypothesized that individuals with a greater need for self-concept consistency would be more disrupted by trauma exposure and thus vulnerable to poorer outcomes. Providing some support for this proposition, they found a small association between lower tolerance for self-contradictions, lower tolerance for cognitive change, and lower tolerance for behavioral change across contexts, and more severe traumatic stress.

Studies of Prolonged Grief. The Self-Concept Clarity Scale had also been used to study self-consistency after bereavement in two cross-sectional studies and one longitudinal study using community samples. Consistent with findings for traumatic stress, Boelen et al., (2012), found large associations between lower self-concept clarity and more severe PGD symptoms across the three studies. The longitudinal study found that higher self-concept clarity at Time 1 was associated with less severe PGD six months later at Time 2.

Papa and Lancaster (2016) measured perceived self-consistency across time by asking participants to provide open-ended responses to the prompt 'Describe how your roles, identity, and/or self have changed since the loss.' Responses were coded for expressions of confusion, disruption, diminishment, or disengagement from their sense of self (e.g., 'I don't know who I am anymore', 'I feel like a different person'). They reported a medium-sized association between greater identity disruption and more severe PGD. Thomsen et al. (2018) asked participants to provide written descriptions of future life chapters and coded these for role continuity. They found a small association between fewer continuing roles and more severe PGD at baseline, but no association at six-month follow-up.

In summary, across both traumatic stress and PGD studies, the most consistent pattern was that individuals reporting lower self-consistency -particularly reduced self-concept clarity and greater identity disruption -reported more severe symptoms; however, not all studies found associations.

Self-complexity

The second group of studies examined the extent to which self-identity is comprised of multiple goals, roles, and preferences, or is more narrowly defined. These studies were less likely to use validated self-report questionnaires, relying more on coding of free-text responses.

As with self-consistency, there were multiple related concepts. These included the total number of roles and characteristics endorsed or provided by participants and the number of different descriptor categories from which these facets were drawn (e.g., active roles, likes, relationships, skills, physical or psychological trait characteristics). These were termed selffluency and self-diversity respectively, by Bellet et al. (2020), who used a modified version of a verbal self-fluency task to index self-complexity.

Studies of Traumatic Stress.

Five studies reported on the concurrent associations between self-fluency and self-diversity and symptom levels in trauma-exposed community samples. Channer and Jobson (2017) asked participants to select from a list of self-descriptors those that best described aspects of themselves (roles, relationships, activities). Individuals whose self-rated symptoms met diagnostic criteria for PTSD chose more traits from more categories than those without PTSD. They also selected more negative self-descriptors. Clifford et al. (2020a, 2020b), using the same task, found no association between the overall number of descriptors selected and clinician-diagnosed PTSD. However, as with Channer and Jobson, the individuals with PTSD did choose a greater number of negative descriptors. Two studies used the Twenty Statements Test (TST; also known as the 'who am I' task), where participants produce their own list of self-aspects and characteristics. Abdollahi et al. (2012) found no association between the number of self-descriptors produced and traumatic stress severity in a sample of participants with HIV as compared with controls with no trauma history. James et al. (2025) similarly found no association between the number of overall self-descriptors (self-fluency) or number of categories (self-diversity) and traumatic stress severity in a sample exposed to potentially morally injurious events. A further eight cross-sectional studies assessed a concept termed self-construal to explore self-complexity. Self-construal refers to statements about the self -assessed using selfreport questionnaires or freely generated by participants -that are typically categorized according to two dimensions: independent self-construal (statements emphasizing one's individuality, autonomy, and personal uniqueness -e.g., 'One should live one's life independently of others', 'I like my privacy') and interdependent self-construal (statements emphasizing one's relationships, social roles, and connectedness with others -e.g., 'It is important to maintain harmony within my group', 'I usually sacrifice my self-interest for the benefit of my group'). This construct is considered relevant for understanding differences across cultures (Markus & Kitayama, 1991).

Using the self-report Individualism-Collectivism Scale (INDCOL), Mordeno et al. (2016) found that traumatic stress severity was independently associated with both higher independent and interdependent self-construal in a sample of internally displaced Filipinos, and that this varied according to PTSD symptom clusters. Higher independent self-construal was more strongly correlated with severity of intrusions and hyperarousal than interdependent selfconstrual. It was proposed that having a more intrinsic concept of control -associated with independent self-construal -placed these individuals at greater risk of experiencing distress in response to diminished personal control than an individual with a more extrinsic concept of control (Mordeno et al., 2016). However, the authors also conceded that more severe distress may be due to highly independent individuals receiving less understanding and support in the context of a collectivist culture. Highlighting this potential interaction between self-construal and social support, Hansford and Jobson (2021) examined self-construal -assessed by the Independent and Interdependent Self Scale -in a community sample of Asian-Australian trauma survivors. They found higher interdependent self-construal was associated with more severe traumatic stress, but only when social support was low. The level of independent selfconstrual was not associated with the severity of traumatic stress. Robinson et al. (2025) assessed self-construal in trauma-exposed Colombian participants, using the Social and Personal Identities Scale (SIPI), which measures the importance ascribed to one's social identity (e.g., 'The similarity I share with others in my group(s)') and personal identity (e.g., 'My need to be completely distinct and unique from everyone else'). Neither social nor personal identity orientation were significant predictors of traumatic stress.

Three studies examined this concept by coding participants' responses to prompts. One study by Jobson (2011) coded independent and interdependent self-construal present in selfdefining memories provided by participants from individualistic (Australia, New Zealand, Western Europe, and North America) and collectivist (Asia, Africa, Middle East, and South America) cultures. Further pointing to an important role for culture in determining the relationship between self-identity constructs and psychopathology, they found that lower independent self-construal was associated with more severe traumatic stress in participants from more individualistic cultures, but less severe traumatic stress in participants from more collectivist cultures. In a similar study, responses on the TST were coded as independent or interdependent self-construal in trauma-exposed participants from individualistic and collectivist cultures of origin. Consistent with Jobson (2011), Jobson and O'Kearney (2008) found that independent self-construal was associated with more severe traumatic stress in trauma-exposed participants from individualistic cultures, but not in participants from collectivist cultures. Jobson et al. (2014) asked participants to write about one traumatic and one nontraumatic event memory in as much detail as possible and coded their responses for independent and interdependent self-construal. They found that, regardless of culture, participants with PTSD expressed lower independent self-construal than those without PTSD.

More recently, studies have examined the extent to which self-construal may moderate different coping styles thought to be associated with poorer outcomes. Li et al. (2025) used the Self-Construal Scale with trauma-exposed Australians participants of Chinese and European ancestry. They found that, regardless of cultural background, interdependent self-construal moderated the association between rumination and traumatic stress symptoms; specifically, the relationship between rumination and traumatic stress was stronger for those with higher interdependent self-construal. Using the same measure to investigate suppression and traumatic stress in participants from the same sample as Li et al., Jobson et al. (2025) found that, as with rumination, the relationship between greater use of suppression and traumatic stress severity was stronger for those with higher interdependent self-construal regardless of cultural group.

However, for those with lower interdependent self-construal, they found a significant relationship between greater use of suppression and traumatic stress severity for European Australians but not Chinese Australians, highlighting the importance of cultural values.

Studies of Prolonged Grief. Bellet et al. (2020) administered the TST to a community sample of bereaved adults and found that participants with probable PGD (according to their ICG scores) produced fewer overall self-descriptors (lower self-fluency) and descriptors from fewer categories (lower self-diversity) than those without PGD, with the relative size of these effects being identified as small and large, respectively. Participants with PGD also provided a smaller proportion of responses referencing personal activities and likes, but not other categories such as social roles, possessions, demographics, personal traits, and physical attributes. They found that diversity and fluency were highly correlated, but only self-diversity emerged as an independent predictor of symptom severity when simultaneously included as predictors in a regression analysis. These findings suggest that diversity may be of greater importance than fluency, and that the presence of some types of diversity may be more important than absolute diversity. Bauer and Bonnano (2001) interviewed bereaved spouses three-to-six months post-loss about their experience of grieving. They found that the extent to which responses referred to actions (e.g., I did badly) rather than personal traits (e.g., I am bad) predicted lower grief at two years. Papa and Lancaster (2016) administered the TST to investigate a number of aspects of self-complexity and bereavement in a sample of bereaved Americans.

As with Bellet et al, they found that providing fewer self-statements overall on the TST (i.e., lower fluency) was associated with more severe symptoms. They also examined selfconstrual by coding responses according to whether they represented independent or interdependent aspects of the self. They found that greater proportions of both independent and interdependent self-construal were associated with less severe PGD symptoms.

Few studies have investigated how these different aspects of complexity or selfconstructs more generally may interact. Highlighting the potential importance of interactions, Papa and Lancaster (2016) found that the association between greater independent selfconstrual and less severe PGD was partially mediated by greater self-consistency (which they measured as lower 'identity disruption'). However, the relationship between greater interdependent self-construal and less severe PGD was not mediated by self-consistency, suggesting a possible buffering effect of social support following identity disruption. They also found having an equal balance of self-construal types was associated with lower identity disruption, and, in turn, lower grief symptoms.

Self-Identity Information Processing

The final group of studies comprised three cross-sectional studies indexing an emerging construct reflecting ways in which individuals process information related to their self-identity. Philippi et al. (2019) asked women seeking treatment for interpersonal trauma to provide written descriptions of how their experience had impacted them and coded the presence/absence of self-referential language in each sentence (e.g., 'I feel broken', or 'I am stronger because of what happened'). They found no overall association between the amount of self-referential language and traumatic stress severity, but a medium-sized association between less use of self-referential language and elevated hyperarousal. This was thought to be consistent with reduced self-coherence in trauma survivors; however, as the association was specific to hyperarousal, it may also reflect a tendency toward reduced self-focus when hypervigilant to external stimuli (Philippi et al., 2019). James et al. (2024b) used a self-report measure (Identity Style Inventory) to examine identity processing style and traumatic stress in a community sample exposed to potentially morally injurious events. This scale indexes three approaches to processing events that challenge one's identity -a diffuse/avoidant style (e.g., 'I try not to think about or deal with problems as long as I can'), an information-seeking style (e.g., 'I handle problems in my life by actively reflecting on them'), and a normative style (e.g., 'I prefer to deal with situations in which I can rely on social norms and standards') which reflects greater reliance on the expectations and standards of important reference groups. James et al. (2024b) found that participants who endorsed more avoidant processing also reported more severe traumatic stress. Maccallum (2022) used the scale with a community sample of bereaved individuals and found that participants who endorsed more diffuse/avoidant or normative processing also reported more severe PGD symptoms.

Discussion

The aim of this review was to synthesise the literature examining self-identity constructs in trauma-and stressor-related psychopathology and to consider how these constructs aligned with existing theoretical models. While the literature has devoted significant attention to the construct of event centrality and its relationship to outcomes, this review sought to explore the other ways in which the self has been operationalised and studied in relation to trauma-and stressor-related disorders. Across 36 studies comprising 7,488 participants, we identified a diverse but fragmented literature characterised by significant variation in terminology, measurement approaches, and frameworks. Despite this heterogeneity, however, studies could be organised into three broader constructs: self-consistency, self-complexity, and self-identity processing. These constructs varied substantially in the extent to which they had been studied, but most reflected theoretically relevant aspects of how individuals are thought to understand, organise, and maintain their sense of self following disruptive life events. In particular, the constructs identified in this review mapped closely onto the competing demands described within the self-memory system (Conway & Pleydell-Pearce, 2000): maintaining a sense of continuity and coherence in self-identity while accommodating changes imposed by new realities. Overall, the diversity of constructs and measures used across studies highlights a range of avenues for investigation relevant to self-identity, establishing a conceptual and methodological framework for the development of future research in this field.

Self-consistency emerged as the most studied construct. Although studies used different terminology and operationalisations, these represented ways in which individuals experienced their sense of self as coherent, stable, and continuous over time. However, some aspects of self-consistency were more consistently related to outcomes than others. Whereas low selfconcept clarity and high self-disruption were consistently associated with more severe symptoms, a lack of consistency between actual and desired or expected identities (selfdiscrepancy) was less consistently related to outcomes. The former findings are broadly consistent with theoretical models proposing that persistent distress following significant life events arises from both the destabilisation of the self caused by the event and/or a failure to develop a new self that integrates the changed reality (Boelen et al., 2006; Conway & Pleydell-Pearce, 2000; Ehlers & Clark, 2000; Maccallum & Bryant, 2013). However, with the exception of one bereavement study, the evidence base is almost entirely cross-sectional. Thus, more longitudinal research is needed to determine the extent to which this form of self-consistency contributes to adaptation, reflects the operation of other underlying processes, or both.

In contrast, the finding that self-discrepancy was not consistently related to outcomes was somewhat surprising given theoretical suggestions that it is the discrepancy between current and desired self-states that exacerbates distress following disruptive events (Conway & Pleydell-Pearce, 2000). One possible explanation for the lack of consistency may be methodological, with positive associations more likely observed in studies using clinician-rated assessments of psychopathology. Self-report measures may be more subject to retrospective or motivational reporting biases that can inflate psychopathology scores, and this may have impacted effect sizes in these studies (Eaton et al., 2000). Another possible explanation is that different forms of self-discrepancy may have differential relevance across forms of psychopathology. For example, ideal-and ought-self discrepancies, which have previously been linked to depression and anxiety (Straumann & Higgins, 1987), may not map directly onto the most salient concerns for trauma-and stressor-related disorders. Future research would benefit from examining forms of self-discrepancy that more closely reflect theoretically relevant disorder-specific threats to identity. For example, James et al. (2024a) found being more discrepant from how one thinks one ought to be (ought-self discrepancy) was associated with more severe moral injury and traumatic stress, and being more discrepant from how one would ideally be (ideal-self discrepancy) was associated with more severe depression (see also Aardema et al. (2013) for consideration of the 'feared self' in relation to obsessive-compulsive disorder).

Self-complexity emerged as the next most studied construct in our synthesis. Studies under this construct examined different ways in which the self could be multifaceted. Some studies focused on the number of different facets included in one's identity (e.g., relationships, activities, likes, and personal characteristics), whereas another area of the literature was mainly concerned with complexity derived from culturally relevant models of identity, particularly independent and interdependent self-construal (Markus & Kitayama, 1991). Again, findings were heterogeneous. In particular, findings relating to self-diversity suggested that the number of domains from which individuals derive their identity may be more important than simply the number of self-descriptors they can generate, with some evidence suggesting that active goals and roles may be especially relevant (Bauer & Bonnano, 2001; Bellet et al., 2020; see also James et al., 2025). These findings are consistent with theoretical accounts proposing that adverse events may be less disruptive when the self is organised around multiple sources of meaning and purpose, thereby reducing the impact of challenges to any single aspect of identity (Conway et al., 2004; Conway, 2005; Maccallum & Bryant, 2013). However, relatively few studies had examined these constructs, and all were cross-sectional. Further, only one study evaluated relationships between self-complexity and other self-identity constructs (Papa & Lancaster, 2016). It would also be interesting to examine the relationship between self-complexity, event centrality, and outcomes -for example, whether greater event centrality is related to lower self-complexity, whether greater complexity buffers the effects of event centrality, or whether these represent independent self-identity processes. Given that the degree of self-complexity could represent both a pre-existing vulnerability or protective factor, or arise as a consequence of the event, prospective studies with cohorts likely to encounter disruptive events (e.g., police recruits, nursing students) would be particularly informative in teasing apart such causal relationships (e.g., Bryant & Guthrie, 2005).

Studies focused on independent and interdependent self-construal raised additional interesting questions, highlighting that the relationship between self-identity and symptoms may be shaped by cultural context. For example, social support appeared to have a buffering effect in the context of cultures and individuals that showed a greater proportion of interdependent self-construal (e.g., Hansford & Jobson, 2021; Jobson, 2011; Mordeno et al., 2016). These findings were also consistent with literature demonstrating cross-cultural differences in autobiographical memory, emotional functioning, and attentional deployment (Chen et al., 2025; Conway et al., 2005; Falon et al., 2024; Wang, 2016; Wang et al., 2017).

Given that most studies included in this review were conducted in WEIRD populations, greater attention to cultural variation is needed to determine whether existing theories accurately capture self-related processes across diverse cultural contexts.

A much smaller literature examined the ways in which individuals process self-relevant information following adverse experiences. While avoidance as a response to threatening selfrelevant information has received significant theoretical and empirical attention (Boelen et al., 2006; Bryant, 2019; Ehlers & Clark, 2000; Eisma & Stroebe, 2021; Maccallum & Bryant, 2013; Miethe et al., 2023), less attention has been paid to how a desire for coherence across time and integration of group norms within the self may impact outcomes. There is currently insufficient data here to draw conclusions; however, in contrast to the positive association between use of this style and grief severity reported in Maccallum (2022), James et al. (2024b)found greater use of a normative processing style was associated with lower moral injuryrelated distress with a sample of adults exposed to potentially morally injurious events. Thus, as with self-discrepancy findings, this raised the possibility that different processing styles may be differentially related to different disorders. We await further investigation.

It is worth reiterating that few of the constructs identified in this review had been extensively studied, and there was considerable overlap between some constructs, pointing to the possibility that the field is becoming characterised by construct proliferation, whereby similar underlying aspects of self-functioning are investigated under different labels. Findings from this review offer a framework for conceptualizing self-identity constructs in the hope of limiting unnecessary construct proliferation and aiding theoretical integration. Moreover, taken together with systematic reviews of event and identity centrality (Gehrt et al, 2018; James et al., under review), the current review suggests that understanding adaptation following disruptive life events requires consideration not only of how central event-related information is to one's identity, but also on broader characteristics of the self-system, including selfconsistency and complexity, and how the self processes self-relevant information.

The review findings also identified several important avenues for future research. First, the findings highlighted the potential for both transdiagnostic and disorder-specific relationships between self-identity constructs and trauma-and stressor-related disorders. This was also observed in a systematic review of identity centrality constructs (James et al., under review), where the literature has begun to extend beyond event centrality to examine disorderrelevant forms of centrality, such as relationship and role centrality. A second important avenue for future research is to clarify how the constructs relate to one another. Multiple constructs were rarely examined together within the same study. Future research that includes multiple self-related constructs within the one study is needed to determine whether selfconsistency, self-complexity, and self-identity processing constructs represent independent dimensions of self-functioning, the action of one underlying mechanism, or components of a dynamic interacting system. Longitudinal and prospective studies are also needed to determine how self-related processes influence each other and symptoms over time. Moreover, the integration of behavioural measures of the self could help address the limitations inherent in self-report methodologies, such as retrospective recall biases and demand characteristics. Such measures would also allow insights into self-related processes that people might not have explicit self-awareness of. Adapting paradigms that index reaction time (e.g., Maccallum & Bryant, 2010), eye movements (e.g., Eisma et al., 2014), or decision-making (e.g., Maccallum & Bonanno, 2016) may provide important additional insights into the functioning of selfidentity constructs in trauma-and stressor-related disorders.

Despite these limitations, however, there is evidence the field is improving in terms of study design quality, with studies increasingly providing justifications for their selection of participant population, sample size, and other methodological details to assist replication.

Limitations of the Current Study

Several limitations to the current review are worth noting. First, only studies published in English and available in peer-reviewed journals were included. Second, self-identity represents a broad field of study, and it was necessary to establish parameters for inclusion of studies in this review. The review focused specifically on self-identity constructs and excluded constructs such as appraisals of the self (e.g., self-esteem and self-efficacy), meaning-making, and autobiographical memory processes. This decision was made as these represent different components of theoretical models and have been synthesised elsewhere (Barry et al., 2023; Fischer et al., 2020; Moore & Zoellner, 2007; Ono et al., 2016; Park, 2010). Third, although the synthesis identified broader higher-order constructs, some classification decisions necessarily involved judgement as boundaries between self-related constructs were not always clearly defined within the literature. Fourth, for coherence and feasibility, this review included only studies that had indexed trauma-and stressor-related disorders listed in the DSM-5-TR (American Psychiatric Association, 2022). It is important to acknowledge that other forms of trauma-and stressor-related psychopathology are recognized in the literature but are not currently included in the DSM nomenclature (e.g., complex PTSD and moral injury). Distinct self-identity factors are thought to play a role in both conditions (see Cloitre, 2021; James et al., 2024a James et al., , 2024b James et al., , 2025;; Litz, 2023; Litz et al., 2009; Robinson et al., 2025), and so it will be interesting for future research to explore similarities and differences in self-construct functioning across this broader range of potential outcomes, as any differences may help shape interventions. Finally, the substantial heterogeneity in study designs, measures, and conceptual frameworks limited the ability to compare findings directly across studies. Nevertheless, this heterogeneity itself represents an important finding, highlighting the need for greater conceptual clarity and theoretical integration within the study of self-related processes in trauma-and stressor-related psychopathology.

Conclusion

This systematic review identified a growing but fragmented literature examining selfidentity in trauma-and stressor-related psychopathology. Despite considerable variation in terminology, measures, and theoretical frameworks, findings could be organised into three broad constructs: self-consistency, self-complexity, and self-identity processing. Across these constructs, the most consistent evidence supported a role for self-consistency, with greater identity disruption and lower self-concept clarity generally associated with more severe traumatic stress and PGD symptoms. Findings relating to self-complexity suggested that maintaining multiple sources of identity, meaningful activities, and self-definition may also facilitate adaptation following disruptive life events. Evidence regarding self-identity processing remains preliminary. This review also offered a framework for increasing conceptual integration within the field, grouping together closely related aspects of self-functioning studied under different labels. How these constructs relate to one another, and to the more extensively studied self-related construct of centrality, has yet to be investigated.

Advancing our understanding of these processes will not only strengthen theoretical models of trauma-and stressor-related psychopathology but also identify new avenues for clinical intervention.
