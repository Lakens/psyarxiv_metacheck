Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on 226 Psychological Science papers with 5,019 hand-coded p-values, 438 of them imprecise (scienceverse/validation, branch stat_p_exact). Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). Of the misses, 13 are starred table notes (“*p < .05”) that the module leaves out on purpose and 11 are p-values it did not find; of the wrong reports, 20 are p-values the ground truth does not contain (such as “ps < .05”) and 6 use ≤ or ≥. These figures measure agreement with the hand-coding, which has known errors in both directions (scienceverse/validation#48).

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download files from additional data repositories reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. Full text

Abstract

Mental health is shaped by multiple factors, including the built environment. As urbanisation increases, urban living has been associated with higher rates of depression, anxiety and psychosis, although the evidence remains predominantly associative. Here, we synthesise epidemiological, neuroimaging and environmental research into a six-domain framework for understanding how built-environment qualities may relate to mental health risk and resilience. The domains comprise Safety and Accessibility, Cognitive Load and Environmental Complexity, Multisensory Integration, Emotional Valence, Social Connection, and Naturalness and Biophilia. We link these domains to neural, physiological, cognitive, emotional and social pathways, and outline methods for testing them across environmental, experiential and neurobiological levels.

The framework provides a structure for developing more precise and testable hypotheses about how specific characteristics of the built environment may influence mental health and resilience.

Introduction

Mental health is closely associated with overall health, daily functioning and quality of life. 1 Today, more than 50% of the world's population lives in cities, a figure projected to reach approximately 70% by 2050. 2, 3 Alongside rapid urbanisation, mental health conditions are increasing worldwide, 4 and urban living has been associated with a higher prevalence of depression, anxiety, psychosis and schizophrenia. 5, 6 The surrounding environment shapes behaviour, stress and mental health across the lifespan. 7 City living has also been linked to differences in brain structure and function, particularly in systems involved in stress regulation. 8, 9 At the same time, cities offer important benefits, including access to education, healthcare, employment, culture and diversity. 10 Features including greenspace and biophilic design may reduce stress and support mental health and wellbeing, 9, 11 while other environmental characteristics have been associated with cognitive functioning and reduced psychiatric risk. 12, 13 The central question is therefore which features of urban environments contribute to risk or resilience, for whom, and through which mechanisms. 14 Understanding these relationships requires consideration of biological, psychological, behavioural and social pathways, together with individual susceptibility and selection. 15 Vulnerability may vary through gene × environment interaction, 16 while individuals may select environments that correspond with partly genetically influenced characteristics, a process described as selective migration. 17, 18 Individuals with higher genetic risk for psychiatric disorders may, for example, be more likely to migrate to urban areas. 19 Environmental exposure must therefore be distinguished from susceptibility, selection and adaptation when interpreting associations between urban environments and mental health.

Building on evidence from epidemiology, neuroscience, environmental psychology and built-environment research, this article proposes a six-domain neurobiological framework for examining how specific environmental qualities may relate to mental health risk and resilience. The framework connects environmental features with subjective experience and candidate neural, physiological, cognitive, emotional, behavioural and social pathways, while recognising that much of the current evidence remains associative. We first review evidence linking urban and natural environments with mental health and brain-related outcomes, then introduce the six domains and their proposed mechanisms, before considering methodological approaches needed to test the framework. The aim is to support more precise, testable hypotheses about how specific features of the built environment may influence mental health.

Built environment pathways to risk and resilience

A substantial body of evidence suggests that urban living is associated with elevated risk for several psychiatric conditions. 6 In a review combining an ecological analysis across 191 countries with a synthesis of meta-analytic studies, van der Wal et al. 6 found a positive, non-linear relationship between urbanicity and the prevalence of common mental disorders, most pronounced for anxiety, and identified ambient (e.g. air pollution, noise), physical (e.g. housing, urban design, green space), and social (e.g. socioeconomic deprivation, crime, social cohesion) features associated with risk that differed by diagnosis. A more recent narrative review of 48 studies similarly found that natural elements around the home, particularly parks and trees, were most consistently associated with better mental and brain health, whereas generic green-space measures often showed no association, and built elements such as street networks, density and public transport showed mixed effects, reflecting trade-offs between accessibility and exposure to noise and pollution. 20 Urbanicity should therefore not be treated as a single exposure. Associations with mental health likely reflect multiple co-occurring conditions, including air and noise pollution, housing quality, density, and differential access to restorative green and blue space, rather than a single undifferentiated "urban" effect. These exposures are also correlated with broader socioeconomic and institutional conditions that may confound or modify observed relationships. Gene-environment correlation may further contribute: polygenic risk for several psychiatric disorders has been associated with living in, and moving to, more densely populated areas. 19 Depression, the most prevalent mental disorder globally, 4 has also been reported more frequently among urban than rural populations, with similar patterns for generalised anxiety disorder and psychosis. 5, 21 Neuroimaging research further suggests that urban exposure may be associated with variation in neural systems involved in stress processing and emotion regulation. MRI studies have reported associations between current city living or urban upbringing and altered activation in regions including the amygdala and perigenual anterior cingulate cortex. 8, 22 Recent exposome studies provide more detailed evidence beyond broad urban-rural comparisons. In 18,701 participants across 34 countries, Legaz et al. 23 assessed 73 country-level physical and social exposures against structural and functional brain-age gaps. Accelerated brain ageing was associated with reduced green-space access, higher PM2.5 emissions, non-methane volatile organic pollutants, extreme precipitation and higher maximum temperature, alongside lower socioeconomic equality, human capital, gender equality, civil participation, political representation, rule of law, basic welfare and access to justice. Physical exposures were more strongly related to structural ageing, whereas social and political exposures were more closely associated with functional ageing; importantly, combined exposome models substantially outperformed isolated exposures, indicating cumulative rather than single-factor effects.

Much of this literature nevertheless remains limited in its ability to establish causality. Large-scale and longitudinal studies such as Legaz et al., 23 Engemann et al., 12 and Quinones et al., 24 the latter linking neighbourhood-level air pollutants, population density and land-use composition to two-year changes in whole-brain structure in older adults, therefore provide a stronger basis for examining how sustained environmental exposure may shape brain and mental health over time, although socioeconomic conditions and access to care remain difficult to fully disentangle.

Several strategies have been used to strengthen causal inference. Prospective and registry-based longitudinal designs, such as those described above, establish the temporal order of exposure and outcome, although they remain vulnerable to residential selection and unmeasured confounding. Family-based designs address confounding shared within families: in a nationwide Swedish cohort of 2.4 million individuals, associations between population density or neighbourhood deprivation and schizophrenia were explained by familial factors when siblings were compared. 25 Genetic approaches indicate that selection may also contribute, as people with higher genetic risk for schizophrenia are more likely to live in densely populated areas, and Mendelian randomisation analyses suggest that this genetic liability may itself influence where people live. 26 Mendelian randomisation is more difficult to apply to the built environment directly, because residential exposures are not themselves genetically determined; its main value in this context is in testing reverse causation and selection, and in examining intermediate traits. Randomised and quasi-experimental changes in environment provide the strongest designs: in the Moving to Opportunity experiment, offering families vouchers to move to lower-poverty neighbourhoods improved adults' subjective wellbeing over 10-15 years. 27 Within-person studies that follow the same individuals before and after environmental change, and natural experiments arising from urban regeneration or greening, are therefore likely to be central to establishing causal pathways for specific urban exposures (Fig. 1). Within this broader context, natural environments may represent an important source of resilience. A growing body of research links nature exposure to mental health and resilience. 9, 11, 12, 22, [28] [29] [30] In a nationwide Danish cohort, greater residential greenspace exposure during childhood was associated with a lower risk of developing a range of psychiatric disorders from adolescence into adulthood, after adjustment for urbanisation, parental and municipal socioeconomic factors, parental history of mental illness, and parental age. 12 Similar effects have been attributed to bluespace, 31 although greenspace and bluespace commonly co-occur with lower pollution and noise, greater opportunities for physical activity, safer neighbourhoods, and greater socioeconomic resources, making causal attribution difficult.

The benefits of greenspace are not confined to childhood. In adulthood, proximity to greenspace has been associated with reduced antidepressant use after adjustment for individual and neighbourhood socioeconomic and urban-context covariates. 30 Potential pathways include stress reduction, attentional restoration, physical activity, social connection, immune function, and neuroendocrine regulation. 10, 32 Neuroimaging findings point in a similar direction. Proximity to forest coverage has been positively associated with structural amygdala integrity in older adults, 9 while exclusive rural versus urban upbringing has been associated with differences in left hippocampal formation volume linked to spatial processing. 22 Using a hybrid ecological momentary assessment-neuroimaging design, Tost et al. 33 found that greater green-related affective benefit was related to lower prefrontal activity during negative-emotion processing, suggesting that urban green space exposure may compensate for reduced neural regulatory capacity.

More broadly, natural features integrated into architecture, design, and infrastructure may help buffer urban stressors. 11 Lower depression prevalence has been associated with urban environments characterised by both greenspace and high-quality housing, 34 while long-term urban greenness has been linked to slower biological and epigenetic ageing. 28, 35 Sky view in the residential environment has also been associated with prefrontal grey matter in children. 36 These findings support further examination of the amount, quality, connectivity, and proximity of green space required to produce meaningful effects, as well as variation across age, socioeconomic conditions, and neurocognitive profiles.

One potential pathway linking repeated environmental exposure to mental health is stress regulation. The hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis is a central brain-body stress-response system, 37 supporting adaptive responses to acute stress through the release of corticotropin-releasing hormone, adrenocorticotropic hormone and cortisol. 38 Prolonged or repeated activation can, however, contribute to allostatic load and adverse effects across the brain and body, [39] [40] [41] including cardiovascular and immune dysfunction, slower wound healing and greater vulnerability to stress-related illness. 42, 43 Several features of the built environment may contribute to such stress-related pathways. In a pioneering social-stress study, amygdala activation correlated with city size, while perigenual anterior cingulate cortex activation, a region involved in social stress processing, correlated with the duration of urban upbringing. 8, 44, 45 Traffic noise and associated pollution are among the most widely discussed urban stressors. Higher traffic volumes have been associated with more depressive symptoms, and greater traffic-noise exposure with poorer mental health. 46, 47 Noise may activate the sympathetic nervous system and elevate brain-body stress responses, 48, 49 while persistent organic and heavy-metal pollutants are being investigated in relation to psychiatric disorders, 50 and traffic-related air pollution has been linked to psychiatric risk, potentially through neuroinflammatory pathways. 51 Urban form may also influence mental health through behaviour and sensory exposure.

Car-oriented environments can constrain physical activity, an important determinant of mental health, 52, 53 while pavement width and city walkability are associated with residents' physical activity. 54 High-rise environments may also influence affect: high-rise buildings have been perceived as less friendly and open, with participants reporting feeling less happy and less in control than in low-rise environments. 55 High-rise development may additionally reduce access to natural daylight, which is relevant to mental health and wellbeing. 56 Insufficient daytime light combined with excessive artificial light at night can disrupt circadian rhythms and sleep, with consequences for mood and mental health. 57, 58 Allostatic interoceptive overload provides one possible account of how cumulative urban exposures may become biologically embedded. 59, 60 When repeated environmental demands such as pollution, noise, deprivation, social adversity or perceived threat exceed an individual's regulatory capacity, stress responses may become persistently elevated and increasingly poorly matched to bodily needs. Altered regulation across the insula, anterior cingulate cortex, amygdala and connected brain-body systems may then contribute to autonomic, HPA-axis, metabolic, inflammatory and immune dysregulation, potentially increasing vulnerability to anxiety, depression and other brain disorders.

Importantly, people do not ordinarily experience environmental characteristics as isolated variables. Rather, spatial, sensory, social, and emotional features are integrated into higher-order subjective experiences that may mediate how environments influence behaviour and mental health. 61 Research on architectural experience has identified coherence, fascination, and hominess as three important experiential dimensions: coherence reflects the ease with which a setting can be organised and understood, fascination its capacity to attract interest and attention, and hominess the extent to which it evokes warmth, comfort, or personal connection. 62, 63

Domains of neuroenvironmental dynamics

Drawing on existing evidence from neuroscience, psychology, and physiology, we identify six domains of neuroenvironmental interaction (Table 1). Each represents a distinct but interrelated pathway through which environmental characteristics are perceived and translated into responses relevant to mental health, cognitive performance, and wellbeing. Together, the domains capture the relationship between external environmental affordances and internal experiential processes, reflecting continuous interplay between perception, cognition, emotion, and context. 29, [64] [65] [66] The domains were identified through a narrative synthesis of evidence from neuroscience, environmental psychology, physiology and architectural research, rather than through a systematic review or statistical modelling. A domain was included where a class of environmental characteristics had repeatedly been associated with outcomes relevant to mental health, where a plausible neurobiological system mediating that association could be identified, and where the relevant characteristics could, at least in principle, be measured and modified through design. The framework is intended to complement dimensional systems such as the Research Domain Criteria (RDoC), 67 which organise mental functioning into negative and positive valence systems, cognitive systems, systems for social processes, arousal and regulatory systems, and sensorimotor systems. Whereas RDoC describes functional systems within the person, the present domains describe classes of environmental characteristics and the person-environment interactions through which they engage those systems. The two can therefore be mapped onto one another: Safety and Accessibility relates primarily to negative valence systems (threat and uncertainty), Cognitive Load and Environmental Complexity to cognitive systems, Multisensory Integration to perceptual aspects of cognitive and sensorimotor systems, Emotional Valence to positive and negative valence systems, Social Connection to systems for social processes, and Naturalness and Biophilia to arousal and regulatory systems relevant to stress recovery and restoration. This correspondence suggests how environmental characteristics might be linked to transdiagnostic mechanisms, although it remains a conceptual proposal to be tested empirically.

The framework is conceptually informed by environmental enrichment research, largely from animal models, showing that complex, multisensory and socially stimulating environments can influence neuroplasticity, neurogenesis and behaviour. [68] [69] [70] Animal models provide a stronger basis for causal inference than most human studies, because environmental conditions can be randomly assigned and varied in isolation while genetic background is held constant. In rodents, housing in enriched environments that combine physical complexity, novelty, social contact and opportunities for exploration increases adult hippocampal neurogenesis, improves learning and memory, and can reduce the impact of stress and of genetic risk in models of brain disorders. 68, 69 Translation to humans is nonetheless limited: enrichment is usually contrasted with impoverished standard laboratory housing, a contrast with no direct equivalent in human settings, and timescales, species-typical needs and social contexts differ substantially. Animal work is therefore best treated as a source of mechanistic hypotheses, for example concerning hippocampal plasticity or stress-axis regulation, to be tested in human studies of specific environmental features.

Safety and accessibility

The first domain covers environmental safety and spatial affordance, including how the physical environment supports a sense of safety, legibility, and wayfinding. Neural systems such as the amygdala, hippocampus, and retrosplenial cortex underlie spatial orientation, vigilance, and environmental predictability. Feeling safe is foundational to mental wellbeing and closely linked to stress management and comfort. 71 However, perceived safety is shaped not only by spatial form but also by broader individual, social, and contextual factors, including socioeconomic conditions, characteristics of the social environment, and gender-related differences in experiences of urban space. 72 Accessibility, particularly across physical and neurodiverse populations, is essential for inclusivity and autonomy, shaping how individuals perceive control and experience a space. 73, 74 Environments that provide clear sight lines, coherent spatial layouts, and balanced lighting may foster predictability, perceived safety, and ease of wayfinding, thereby reducing cognitive demand and potentially attenuating stress-related responses. Cognitive load and environmental complexity

The cognitive domain explores how environmental structure and complexity shape attention, creativity, and cognitive restoration. The coupling of fronto-parietal control and default-mode networks has been argued to enable transitions between focus and reflection. 78 Stimulating yet coherent environments support curiosity and cognitive resilience. 79, 80 Spatial variety and structured complexity have the potential to encourage fascination without overload, supporting mental restoration and creativity. 81 Evidence relevant to this domain is convergent but methodologically heterogeneous, and further work is needed to clarify how an optimal level of complexity can be distinguished from overstimulation in real-world environments.

Multisensory integration and perceptual coherence

Human experience is inherently multisensory. This domain examines how coherence across sensory channels (sight, sound, touch and temperature) affects comfort and predictability. Neural integration across the thalamus, insula, and sensory cortices creates unified perceptual experiences. 65 Although multisensory coherence is likely to be important for comfort and stress regulation, the built-environment literature remains relatively fragmented, and more precise operationalisation of cross-modal effects is still needed. 61

Emotional valence and aesthetic appraisal

The emotional domain captures how aesthetic and atmospheric qualities of the built environment influence affective states. Neural systems including the orbitofrontal cortex, anterior cingulate, and limbic structures underpin aesthetic pleasure, harmony, and mood regulation. 82 Environments that evoke positive emotions can buffer stress and support mental wellbeing. 83 Studies have reported associations between scenic locations, happiness, and wellbeing, 84, 85 suggesting that aesthetics should not be overlooked. 64 This domain should therefore be regarded as a plausible translational synthesis rather than a fully validated category, given that aesthetic appraisal reflects both shared neurobiological processes and substantial individual and cultural variation.

Social connection and spatial belonging

The social domain examines how architectural spaces can enable or constrain social interaction, togetherness, trust, and belonging, 86, 87 while recognising that spatial design shapes the opportunities for such outcomes rather than determining them, as trust, inclusion, discrimination, social capital, and broader institutional conditions remain decisive. Neural systems including the medial prefrontal cortex and temporo-parietal junction are central to social cognition and empathy, while affiliative and bonding processes have also been linked to oxytocinergic mechanisms, whereby oxytocin released during positive social contact promotes trust and approach behaviour and dampens amygdala-mediated stress and threat responses, thereby reinforcing social bonds. 87, 88 The relationship between spatial form and social connection is also likely to be context-dependent, shaped not only by design features but by population characteristics, cultural norms, and wider social conditions.

Naturalness and biophilia

The final domain addresses the human affinity for natural environments and their restorative impact on physiology and mood. Activation of visual and default-mode networks, together with parasympathetic pathways, contributes to stress reduction, fascination, and recovery. 89 Exposure to vegetation, daylight, and water elements re-establishes sensory balance and fosters cognitive restoration. 7, 10 Being in nature and even in immersive virtual reality nature environments reduces subjective perception of stress, reduces cortisol, and improves heart rate variability (HRV). [90] [91] [92] Although this is one of the more consistent areas of the literature, important questions remain regarding which qualities of nature exposure are most relevant and through which pathways restorative effects are most likely to arise. 61

Integrative perspective

While each domain represents a distinct dimension of neuro-environmental interaction, they are interdependent and mutually reinforcing. At present, however, the framework should be understood primarily as a translational and hypothesis-generating model, rather than as a fully validated empirical taxonomy for real-world application.

A future integrative account of built-environment effects will require nonlinear, multimodal and multilevel approaches that move beyond isolated exposure-outcome associations. 7, 93 Urban conditions emerge from interacting physical, social and biological processes whose effects may be synergistic, threshold-dependent and heterogeneous across individuals and contexts. Computational frameworks should therefore combine environmental measures with neuroimaging, cognition, behaviour, whole-body physiology and multi-omics, and exposome, while accounting for temporal dynamics and individual trajectories. 7 Methods for evaluating the impact of urban environments linked to brain health Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) provides high-resolution measures of brain structure and function and has been central to research linking urban exposure to the brain. 8, 22 MRI has been used to examine structural and functional differences in stress-sensitive regions such as the amygdala, hippocampus and prefrontal cortex in relation to urban and environmental exposure. 12, 22 The principal limitation of conventional MRI in this field is ecological: scanning takes place in a controlled laboratory environment, while environmental exposure is usually inferred from separate geocoded, behavioural or self-report data.

Physiological markers provide a complementary and more portable window onto stress-related processes. Cortisol, an index of hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal-axis activity, can be measured in saliva, blood or hair and is widely used as a marker of physiological stress. 94 It is relevant to environmental research because chronic stress is implicated in many mental health conditions, urban upbringing has been associated with altered stress processing, and sustained cortisol elevation has been linked to structural changes in stress-sensitive brain regions. 8, 95 Heart rate variability (HRV) indexes autonomic function through variation in inter-beat intervals and is inversely associated with sympathetic activation. 96 Higher HRV is generally associated with greater stress resilience and better mental health, whereas lower HRV has been linked to anxiety, depression and stress vulnerability. 97, 98 Wearable and mobile technologies increasingly allow neural and physiological responses to be studied in everyday settings. Mobile electroencephalography (EEG) measures electrical activity at the scalp and can track moment-to-moment neural dynamics as individuals move through real-world environments. It offers high temporal resolution and greater ecological validity than laboratory-based neuroimaging, but has limited spatial resolution and is sensitive to movement and muscular artefacts. 99, 100 Although difficult to achieve in practice, the strongest designs will combine these approaches (Fig. 2). Geospatial and longitudinal data can characterise the distribution and accumulation of environmental exposure; ecological momentary assessment and behavioural measures can capture subjective and functional responses; wearable physiology and mobile neurotechnology can track processes as they unfold; and MRI can provide spatially specific measures of brain structure and function. Operationalising the domains also requires instruments that record environmental experience in a structured and comparable way. One such approach applies the six domains described here as a domain-referenced self-report measure combined with structured environmental profiling, allowing overall and domain-specific scores to be compared across settings and before and after environmental change.

Synthesis and future directions

Three conclusions emerge from the evidence considered in this article. First, the evidence remains predominantly associative. Epidemiological, environmental and neuroimaging studies link urbanicity, limited exposure to natural environments and particular spatial conditions with psychiatric, cognitive and neurobiological outcomes.

However, causal interpretation is constrained by residential self-selection, migration, reverse causation, exposure misclassification, and the frequent use of broad environmental indicators or registry-based diagnoses. The evidence therefore shows more clearly that environmental conditions are relevant to mental health than how particular spatial features produce specific outcomes.

Second, environmental exposures are correlated, interacting and cumulative. Urban environments comprise overlapping conditions, including noise, air pollution, density, housing quality, traffic, daylight, deprivation, social context and access to natural environments. Associations with individual features, including greenspace, must therefore be interpreted within the wider environmental and social exposure profile.

Third, the six-domain framework provides an integrative, hypothesis-generating structure for advancing the field beyond broad measures of urbanicity. Prospective work should test the separability and interactions of these domains and establish their reliability and validity across populations and settings.

An important challenge is to bridge aggregate environmental indicators, subjective experience, biological embedding and mental health outcomes. Existing studies have established associations between urbanisation, greenspace exposure, brain-related outcomes and mental health diagnoses, 5, 12, 21, 22 but a major gap remains in linking specific spatial qualities to particular neurobiological and psychological responses. The evidence also remains heavily weighted towards the Global North, limiting generalisability to informal settlements and rapidly urbanising regions (e.g. sub-Saharan Africa and South Asia). Developmentally informed studies, particularly in childhood and adolescence, will be important for determining when environmental exposure matters most and how effects accumulate across the lifespan. Mechanistic randomised controlled trials are a particular priority. These could test whether modifying features such as daylight, noise, access to nature, sensory conditions or spatial configuration alters hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal-axis activity, autonomic regulation, affective responses or cognitive functioning.

Conclusions

If built environments contribute to mental health risk and resilience, environmental design may represent a scalable and non-stigmatising target for intervention across healthcare, education, workplaces, housing and public space. Translational recommendations should, however, remain proportionate to the strength of the evidence. The next step is to identify testable environmental features and mechanisms through rigorous longitudinal, experimental and real-world research. With greater conceptual precision, methodological integration and empirical validation, environmental neuroscience may help inform built environments that are more responsive to the conditions supporting human wellbeing, resilience and mental health.
