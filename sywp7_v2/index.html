Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). Statcheck might still miss one-sided tests that are described in another sentence, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. A TRO uses them for each trov:TransparentResearchPerformance. * run_results$exit_status: the exit status of the process that ran the script (0 = ended normally); NA when the process did not report one (a time limit, or Docker failing to start the container). * artifacts: one row per file, with stage (deposit: as downloaded from the repository, hashed from the downloaded file; before_run: the run folder just before the first script ran, after metacheck’s own changes, such as rewritten paths and unpacked archives; after_run: only the files the scripts made (status new), changed (changed) or removed (removed), as an unchanged file is the one listed before the run), path (relative to the run folder, as valid UTF-8, a byte of another encoding written as <xx>), size (bytes), sha256, mime_type (by extension, NA when unknown), source (the file’s address in the repository, for deposit). The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). Files metacheck itself copied or made between passes (see modifications, output_copied, file_placed) show in after_run too. A TRO uses the rows as trov:ResearchArtifacts and each stage as a trov:ArtifactArrangement. * modifications$sha256_before, modifications$sha256_after: the hash of the changed script as downloaded and as it ran; NA when the script is not at the same path in both (a script made from an R Markdown file). * environment: the system that ran the code (trov:TransparentResearchSystem in a TRO): metacheck_version, started_at, ended_at, host (R version, platform, operating system), sandbox, network_isolated (TRUE only when every Docker run had --network none; the process sandbox does not stop the network), internet, timeout_per_script, total_timeout, python_local (the Python run outside Docker and its version), and docker: runs (for each kind of run, r_run, r_install, python_run, python_install, the image and the docker run arguments, such as --network none, --read-only, --cap-drop ALL, no-new-privileges, the process limit and the memory and processor limits) and images (for each image, its ID and digests from docker image inspect, its creation time and the R and Python versions its environment declares).

run_results$checked says what the run of each script shows about the code, in five groups, so that a script that does not reproduce is kept apart from one the check could not test: ran (it ran to the end); code_error (it did not reproduce: it stopped with an error of the code or the deposit, could not be read, or needs a file the deposit does not hold); dependency (a package or program was not available: a package that could not be installed or was not loaded, a program outside R and Python, a system library, or R crashing in a package’s compiled code); time (it did not finish within the time limit for one script, or was not started because the time for all scripts had run out); and not_checkable (it could not be checked automatically: it needs a person, the internet, a key or another setting of the authors’ computer, one platform such as Google Colab, Windows or OpenSAFELY, more memory than the run allows, a file that was not downloaded, a screen, or a program that runs it, or it is a file of a package of the code that Python runs only as a module of that package). It is NA for files run inside another script, files that are not an analysis, files not read, and a Docker failure. summary_table counts each group (repro_ran_n, repro_code_error_n, repro_dependency_n, repro_time_n, repro_not_checkable_n), and the report’s first sentence gives them. The traffic light reflects the static readiness signals by default; a script in code_error that stopped with an error makes it red, as a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure, and a script in one of the other three groups keeps it from being green. Calls that need a screen, which the run does not have (dev.print(), dev.copy2pdf(), dev.copy2eps(), savePlot(), windows(), win.graph(), x11(), quartz(), View()), are replaced before the run by calls that need none, which modifications records as screen_call_replaced. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

Healthy relationships are essential for well-being and can buffer against stress. Women in farming communities face numerous challenges which limit both the time and conditions needed to maintain supportive relationships. We examined the satisfaction of relationship needs among women in the UK farming community. In Study 1, 138 women (Mage = 48.43 years, SD = 12.33) completed an online questionnaire including quantitative measures of wellbeing, stress, and relational need satisfaction, along with qualitative open-text responses. In Study 2, 867 women (Mage = 44.21 years, SD = 13.77) completed the same quantitative measures. Across both studies, moderation analysis indicated higher stress was associated with lower well-being when relational needs were met. This notable finding was clarified through analysis of Study 1 qualitative data, which revealed that women in farming often provide support to others, shaped by their farming lifestyle, while receiving little support in return. Our results suggest that, paradoxically, although relationships can meet needs, relationships may also impose significant burdens on women in farming. Further research should build on this study, the first to examine these associations among women in farming, to understand the complex dynamics of reciprocal social support and improve well-being within this vital community.

1. Introduction

Women are a crucial part of the agricultural sector in the United Kingdom (UK). In addition to the documented 27,411 women farmers (Defra, 2016), countless wives, daughters, and sisters, whose contributions often go unrecognised, play a significant role within farming. Furthermore, many farm workers and women are also employed in allied industries, such as veterinary practices. Whether the woman is a farmer, employee, or family member, the farming lifestyle presents a multitude of well-documented social and occupational stressors (Constantine, 2001; Nichols & Davis, 2024; Scheyett et al., 2023; Wheeler & Lobley, 2023).

Stressors may be buffered by supportive and healthy relationships, which act as a source of support during adversity and assist us in managing the negative effects of stress (S. Cohen, 1988; S. Cohen & Wills, 1985; Kawachi, 2001; Plaisier et al., 2007). Yet, as we will consider, women in farming (collectively termed agri-women from here on) can face challenges that limit the time and circumstances needed to develop and maintain relationships which could provide support.

Fulfilling relationships, whether romantic, platonic, or familial, are vital for people both outside and within farming communities (Bjornestad et al., 2019; Caldwell & Boyd, 2009; Cassidy & Carswell, 2024; Dunne et al., 2021; McLaren & Challis, 2009). Satisfactory relationships can help individuals meet their basic psychological needs. According to Self-Determination Theory (SDT), individuals are driven by three psychological drivers: autonomy, competence, and relatedness (Ryan & Deci, 2000). When these needs are met, well-being is increased (Ryan et al., 2022). The driver of relatedness considers the satisfaction of needs within interpersonal relationships (La Guardia & Patrick, 2008; Landon et al., 2021; Martela & Riekki, 2018; Patrick et al., 2007; Ryan & Deci, 2000). One possible framework for understanding how interpersonal relationships can be satisfied is through assessing relational need satisfaction, comprising five dimensions: authenticity, support and protection, initiative, impact, and shared experiences (Erskine, 2018; Žvelc et al., 2020). The farming lifestyle may present dynamics that can both hinder and support women's ability to meet these relational needs.

Relationships in farming can be complex, often characterised by tensions and family conflicts (Marotz-Baden & Mattheis, 1994; Newsome et al., 2024; Pini, 2007; Price & Evans, 2006).

Unlike many other demographics, an agri-woman's home and work life are deeply intertwined, which can intensify relational tensions (Dudensing et al., 2017; McShane et al., 2016; Paskewitz & Beck, 2017). Women marrying into a farming family may also face particular relational difficulties where their impact on decision-making can be limited, relationships with in-laws can be fraught, and they may be viewed with suspicion amid their fear of future divorce and its financial consequences for the farm (Pini, 2007; Price & Evans, 2006; Wicklow & Shortall, 2025). These pressures may further intensify for agri-women if their social networks are restricted or they are geographically isolated from their own family following relocation to begin farming or upon marriage (Bryant et al., 2026).

The farming lifestyle may also increase demands on agri-women's time, limiting their ability to build and sustain supportive relationships (Berkowitz & Perkins, 1984; Brennan et al., 2022; Corbett et al., 2020; Giesen et al., 1989; Kallioniemi et al., 2016). The family home is frequently located on the farm, often leading to several generations living and working together (McShane & Quirk, 2009). Living alongside intergenerational family members can heighten traditional expectations associated with gender roles and norms which often assume that agri-women should perform certain gender-normative tasks. For example, caring for children and elderly relatives often falls to the agri-woman, which they must balance with their onfarm work and off-farm employment in a so-called triple burden (Becot et al., 2025; Gallagher & Delworth, 1993; Satyavathi et al., 2010). In addition to the time required to manage these duties, undertaking them is often seen merely as 'just' part of the role of a farmer's wife, daughter, sister, or mother, and may prevent agri-women from acting, and living authentically (Alston, 1998; Brandth, 2002; Contzen & Forney, 2017; Munz, 2021).

Attitudes and gender norms may also shape how agri-women give and receive support within their relationships (Kempster et al., 2023; Wagner et al., 2026; Wheeler & Nye, 2025). In farming communities, there is a well-documented tendency towards self-reliance, and seeking social support may be perceived as a sign of weakness (Firnhaber et al., 2024).

Although social support typically buffers against the negative effects of stress (S. Cohen & Wills, 1985), reverse buffering -where the relationship between stress and negative psychological outcomes is intensified even in the face of strong social support -has been identified in general populations, (Kokoroko & Sanda, 2019; Liu et al., 2021). Research among agri-women in England and Wales has found that providing instrumental support is associated with both higher perceived stress and higher mental well-being (Kyle et al., 2026). However, there is a lack of contemporary evidence on how relational need satisfaction relates to stress and well-being among agri-women in the UK farming community. This is particularly evident regarding satisfaction in relationships that extend beyond traditional dyadic partnerships, such as those between spouses (Danes & Lee, 2004; Lorenz et al., 1993; Melberg, 2003).

Familial relationships and spousal support have been previously found to be vital for agriwomen in managing stress and enhancing their overall well-being (Giesen et al., 1989; Lorenz et al., 1993; Melberg, 2003; Rudolphi et al., 2024). Given that well-being of agri-women has repeatedly been found to be poor (Jones-Bitton et al., 2020; Kyle et al., 2026; Thompson et al., 2023; Wheeler & Lobley, 2023), interventions and agri-community dialogues need to be developed to effectively support them. It is consequently essential to gather evidence on how satisfying relationships can help alleviate the negative effects of stress to inform support programmes. The association between the dimensions of relational needs, stress, and wellbeing has not been previously investigated in women in farming. To contribute evidence to help address the gap in current research, the present mixed-methods study aimed to address the following research questions: 1.

What is the relationship between stress, relational need satisfaction, and well-being in UK agri-women? 2. Which dimensions of relational needs predict stress and well-being in agri-women? 3. Do higher levels of relational need satisfaction moderate the impact of stress on the well-being of UK agri-women? 4. How are the different dimensions of relational needs contextualised in the experience of being an agri-woman?

We hypothesised that: 1. Higher stress would be associated with lower well-being among UK agri-women, and higher satisfaction of relational needs would be associated with greater well-being.

2. Different dimensions of relational needs would predict stress and well-being to varying extents. No dimensions were specified due to the exploratory nature of the study and lack of previous evidence in this population.

3. Met relational needs would moderate the relationship between stress and well-being.

Specifically, we expected that, among agri-women with higher levels of met relational needs, the association between stress and well-being would be smaller than among those with lower levels of met relational needs, consistent with a moderating effect.

To answer RQ4, we conducted a framework analysis with open-text box data to illustrate how specific dimensions of relational needs apply in a farming context in relation to stress and well-being.

2. Method

>Method

2.1 Study Design

This project consisted of 2 studies. Study 1 employed a sequential mixed-methods design using concurrent quantitative and qualitative data collection (Creswell & Clark, 2017; Ivankova et al., 2006). Data were collected cross-sectionally from farming communities in the UK between February and March 2023. Quantitative analyses were completed before the deductive qualitative analysis, which contextualised and operationalised the functioning of relational needs within agri-women. Study 2 served as a replication sample and collected quantitative cross-sectional data between June and July 2026. Ethical approval was granted by the Northumbria University Human Research Ethics Board (Reference 2370).

2.2 Participants

The inclusion criteria for both studies required participants to identify as a woman, be over 18 years of age, reside in the UK, and have a connection to the farming community, either through work or family. No incentive to participate was offered in either study. Sample characteristics are fully described in the Results section.

Study 1

A concurrent study examining the well-being of women in the UK farming community had recruited 440 volunteers through opportunity and purposeful social media advertising (Leighton et al., 2021). All respondents were emailed the details, offering them the opportunity to also participate in this study. Of these, 275 (62.5%) accessed an online survey.

We excluded 137 participants from the analysis due to incomplete responses. In most of these cases, participants accessed the questionnaire but did not engage with any questions, rendering attrition analysis infeasible. We also removed one participant who did not meet the inclusion criteria and six duplicated responses. The final analytical sample comprised 138 participants (50.2% complete response rate). A sensitivity analysis was conducted using GPower to verify adequate statistical power (Faul et al., 2009). The analysis employed a multiple regression model with fixed predictors and R² deviation from zero. With N = 138, three predictors and α = .05, the analysis indicated 80% power to detect a small to medium effect size (f² = 0.08) (J. Cohen, 1992). The effect size threshold was selected as a conservative estimate for detecting meaningful effects in a psychological moderation model. The sample size was deemed sufficient to detect the hypothesised main effects; however, to achieve greater statistical power, replication in an independent sample was deemed necessary.

Study 2

Participants were recruited through purposive and opportunistic sampling (Leighton et al., 2021). The same 440 participants from Study 1 were again emailed details about the online survey. A further General Data Protection Regulation-compliant mailing list of 98 participants, obtained through a different study from the same research team, was also contacted by email with the survey link. Additionally, the survey link was widely shared in farming-related groups on social media platforms, including LinkedIn and Facebook. The online survey was accessed 1048 times. After removal of incomplete responses, the final replication sample comprised 867 participants. Using the same sensitivity analysis procedure described in Study 1, the final sample provided 80% power to detect an effect size of f² = 0.01.

2.3 Measures

Study 1

Data were collected online as part of a wider study. Here we describe the measures relevant to the current aims and analyses.

Stress Overload Scale-Short (SOS-S)

The 10-item SOS-S scale measures how stressed/overloaded a participant has felt over the past week on a five-point Likert scale (1 = Not at all;5 = A Lot) (Amirkhan, 2018). The maximum score on the SOS-S is 50; higher scores indicate greater stress.

Relational Needs Satisfaction Scale (RNSS)

Participants' satisfaction with their relational needs was measured by the RNSS and analysed according to the scale instructions (Žvelc et al., 2020). The scale, based upon eight needs, measures five sub-scales: Authenticity, Support and Protection, Having an Impact, Shared Experience, and Initiative from the Other (Erskine, 2018). Participants rated 20 items on a 5point Likert scale from 1 (completely disagree) to 5 (completely agree). Item 18 is reverse scored before the mean score for each sub-scale is calculated, with higher scores indicating higher satisfaction levels within each sub-scale. The total relational need satisfaction score is calculated by averaging all subscales.

Warwick-Edinburgh Mental Well-being Scale [Short] (SWEMWBS)

Well-being was measured using the 7-item SWEMWBS scale (Stewart-Brown et al., 2009).

Participants described their experience over the previous two weeks on a 5-point Likert Scale, ranging from 1 (None of the Time) to 5 (All of the Time). Scores are summed according to provided guidelines and converted to a standardised score using a conversion table provided by the SWEMWBS authors. A higher score indicates higher well-being.

Participants were also asked open-ended questions to capture qualitative information about their position and experiences in farming, personal stress, and self-perception of well-being (Table 1). The questionnaire concluded with demographic information, including the participant's age and marital status.

Study 2

The online survey comprised the SOS-S, RNSS, and SWEMWBS, along with age, marital status, and farming role, with options provided for each.

2.4 Procedure

The online survey in both studies was hosted by the Qualtrics Online Survey platform. In both, participants read an online information sheet outlining the study's objectives and provided informed consent. Participants then completed the measures as outlined above. At the end of the survey, participants received a full debrief that included information on mental health support organisations relevant to agri-women.

2.5 Treatment of Data

Data were cleaned in SPSS and RStudio using the tidyverse package (Wickham & Vaughan, 2026). Minimum data thresholds per scale were applied to ensure adequate completion for reliable scale scoring (SWEMWBS ≥6/7; SOS-S ≥9/10 and RNSS ≥18/20, representing ≥90% completion per scale). For participants meeting these thresholds, missing items within a scale were handled using prorated means, which were calculated from the completed items and scaled to the full range (Parent, 2013).

SPSS and R (R Core Team, 2024) were used for all statistical analyses. Reliability statistics (Cronbach's alpha and McDonald's omega) were calculated with 95% confidence intervals using the OMEMA macro for SPSS (Hayes & Coutts, 2020). Bivariate correlations and linear regressions were performed to examine associations between the five RNSS sub-scales and both SOS-S and SWEMWBS as separate outcome variables. A moderation analysis was conducted with SOS-S as the predictor, SWEMWBS as the dependent variable, and RNSS total score as the moderator, using the mean across all five sub-scales (Figure 1). Mean-centring was applied prior to analysis. Simple slopes were computed at ±1 SD from the mean to examine the conditional effect of stress on well-being at low, average, and high levels of relational need satisfaction.

2.7 Researcher Paradigm

Qualitative data were analysed within an interpretivist paradigm to contextualise participants' experiences utilising a deductive application of relational needs to the data (Goldsmith, 2021; Klingberg et al., 2024; Omodan, 2022). Interpretation was also conducted inductively to explore the agri-women's environment and how it might influence relational satisfaction. As the primary researcher (SNK) is the spouse of a farmer, her lived experience is recognised as having influenced the interpretation of the data (Dembele et al., 2024). This was managed by engaging reflectively and critically with the wider research team (EB, SD, and AJ), who all contributed lived experiences from non-farming backgrounds and academic expertise in both psychology, veterinary medicine, and epidemiology to enhance reflexivity.

Coding was performed by SNK before being cross-checked by EB and SD for reliability. Disagreement over coding was resolved through critical engagement with the agreed code descriptions (Table 3). We reviewed the results of the quantitative analysis to provide context for the qualitative responses. >Results

3.1 Sample Characteristics

Participants in Study 1 ranged in age from 22 to 84 years (Mage = 48.43, SD = 12.33, Median = 49). In Study 2, participants ranged in age from 18 to 99 years (Mage = 44.21, SD = 13.77, Median = 44). Age distribution differed significantly between Study 1 and Study 2: χ²(5) = 13.854, p = .017, with proportionally more younger (18-24) and fewer 55-64-year-olds in Study 2; however, there was no significant difference in marital status composition between the samples: χ²(5) = 2.833, p = .726. Table 4 provides a comprehensive breakdown of the sample characteristics. In Study 1, participants described their farming role in a free-text box (rather than selecting predefined categories). Their responses were coded; however, many participants provided a general overview of their duties instead of specifying a formal role. As a result, some interpretation was necessary. For example, Participant 014 described "Secretarial completing paperwork and banking. Physical managing stock, feeding, land work" and was therefore assigned the role of farmer due to their mention of both practical and administrative farm tasks. This does not recognise that this may be undertaken in addition to off-farm employment and alongside the roles of wife and mother, etc. Additionally, many participants described multiple roles. In some instances, we determined their role based on the primary role in their description, even when it included multiple roles. For transparency, we present the full set of de-identified role responses and their codes in the supplementary materials.

The largest category of respondents was farmers (27.5%), although many others (~34 participants) described a range of farming duties despite not classifying themselves as a farmer role. Interestingly, many participants used the word "support" in their description, including Participant 064 who self-described their role as a "supportive partner to a farmer" (categorised as ''wife/partner').

In Study 2, participants were asked to select one main identity within farming from predesignated options. Although 80.7% chose only one category, 19.3% identified multiple categories. Again, this categorisation did not allow for non-farming identities to be selected.

Furthermore, if participants had been asked to choose 'all that apply', we are confident the number of participants selecting multiple categories would have increased. Of those selecting multiple roles, the majority selected two roles (14.3% overall). The largest category of respondents was again farmers (42.3%).

We address the limitations of measuring identity among agri-women in our discussion and consider this a reflection of the multifaceted and complex nature of identity among agriwomen.

The direction of the findings was replicated, with the same significant negative interaction effect observed. Although the coefficients of average and high slopes decreased in strength, the repeated findings indicate a complex relationship among stress, well-being, and relational need satisfaction among agri-women.

Discussion

Although the relationship between stress and well-being is well-established, less is known about how relational need satisfaction relates to these outcomes among women in farming.

Using both an original and a replication sample, our study is the first to examine the associations between stress, well-being, and five dimensions of relational satisfaction within the UK farming community. Higher relational need satisfaction was associated with higher well-being and lower stress. Interestingly, moderation analysis revealed that higher stress was associated with lower well-being most strongly at higher levels of relational need satisfaction.

A sequential framework analysis of qualitative data provided contextual understanding of how traditional, gendered expectations and prioritising the farm influenced spousal and familial relationships. There are complex dynamics and tensions in reciprocal support within farming environments where women might be expected to provide and prioritise support for others' needs, yet are limited in reciprocal support and / or feel constrained from asking for support in return, often because of their gender. Consistent with previous research, agriwomen often described themselves as the providers -rather than recipients -of support (Kempster et al., 2023; Rudolphi et al., 2024). Our findings offer guidance for tailoring interventions to recognise that agri-women need support to advocate for, and express, their own needs. Beyond supporting others, agri-women need help to build their own well-being, whether by developing additional psychological skills or by receiving structural support that provides free time.

The need for authenticity emerged as the strongest predictor of both stress and well-being across both studies. This aligns with earlier research involving the general population, emphasising that genuine self-expression is crucial for enhancing well-being (Wood et al., 2008). Our qualitative findings suggest authenticity for agri-women is influenced by both gender and traditional expectations, particularly those stemming from within intergenerational farming families. Participants expressed a desire to be assessed on their competence as farmers rather than based on their gender. They also described how traditional farming norms restricted their identity and shaped the roles they were expected to fulfil rather than necessarily reflecting the roles that they wanted to undertake. This aligns with previous research that found many agri-women act in accordance with farming norms and need to balance multiple identities, each carrying distinct expectations (Brasier et al., 2014; Wagner et al., 2026). Similarly, agri-women seeking to act as autonomous individuals, separate from traditional farming family or farm business expectations, have previously been found to encounter role and identity conflicts (Danes & Lee, 2004). Collectively, these findings might explain why the need for authenticity emerged as the strongest relational need for agriwomen and why it can contribute to increased stress and decreased well-being.

Contrary to our expectations, we found a modest, significant interaction in both studies where higher relational need satisfaction strengthened, rather than buffered, the negative association between stress and well-being. We had expected that higher relational need satisfaction would reflect more supportive relationships. In line with the stress-buffering hypothesis (S. Cohen & Wills, 1985), we anticipated higher relational satisfaction would reduce the negative association between stress and well-being. Our counterintuitive finding might be explained by considering the distinctive, paradoxical dynamics of support among agri-women. Our qualitative analysis indicated that, rather than receiving support, agriwomen often see themselves as 'supporters' within their relationships and in the farming home and business. Restricted or non-supportive social networks may increase men's reliance on agri-women for emotional support (Alston, 2004; Melberg, 2003; Nye et al., 2022; Stain et al., 2008). For many, this dynamic can position them as the main or sole provider of emotional support to their spouses and families -often without reciprocation (Malone et al., 2025; Rudolphi et al., 2024; Scheyett et al., 2024). Although this explanation focuses on spousal and familial relationships, given their centrality to agri-women, the reverse buffering effect may also extend to agri-women's friendships. In this study, we did not explicitly specify which relationships were under consideration. Relational need satisfaction could therefore relate to friendships as well as marriage. However, our qualitative data did not address how women provide and receive support within their wider social networks, and extant research in this area remains limited.

Along with providing emotional support, agri-women also offer practical support in their relationships. Many agri-women assume the majority of household and caregiving responsibilities for both children and elderly relatives (Becot et al., 2025; Heenan, 2000).

Although this caregiver role may be actively chosen, for some it may be shaped by traditional farming expectations (Contzen & Forney, 2017; Heenan, 2000; Wagner et al., 2026).

Consequently, although relationships fulfil important needs for agri-women, participation in them may also increase their responsibilities towards others. Esteem Enhancement Theory suggests that providing support can enhance well-being even as it causes emotional strain (Batson et al., 1988). Therefore, for agri-women some of their identity may be derived from the support that they offer others. Similarly, although social support is typically seen as most beneficial when both given and received (Brown et al., 2003; Shakespeare-Finch & Obst, 2011), women who perceived themselves as providing more support have previously reported better long-term health outcomes (Väänänen et al., 2005). Relational need satisfaction may therefore simultaneously be a source of fulfilment and increased responsibility, which could negatively impact well-being. SDT posits that satisfying relational needs enhances well-being (Ryan & Deci, 2000). However, for agri-women, this duality (where fulfilment is coupled with additional responsibilities) may offer one explanation as to why, at higher levels of satisfaction with relational needs, the relationship between stress and wellbeing was strengthened rather than buffered across both our studies.

Within the five relational need dimensions, we identified several sources of relational stress, including familial tensions and a lack of prioritisation in favour of the farm (Danes & Lee, 2004). This was despite the need for impact not being a significant predictor quantitatively of stress in either study. Our findings suggested that tensions within multi-generational farming families can significantly affect the well-being of agri-women, particularly regarding their influence and participation in decision-making. It has been argued that women marrying into a farming family may be seen as a threat in the event of a potential divorce (Price & Evans, 2006). The woman's perceived ability to jeopardise the farm through asset splitting may lead to her exclusion from the business and to her remaining an 'outsider', thus limiting her ability to meet her need to have an impact (Marotz-Baden & Mattheis, 1994; Pini, 2007).

Consequently, agri-women can be excluded from farm business decisions, as farming families prioritise protecting farmland and assets (Price & Evans, 2006). Previous research among agriwomen in England and Wales has identified the lack of decision-making ability as a predictor of both stress and loneliness (Kyle et al., 2026). Our qualitative identification of relational stressors, therefore, extends understanding of the challenges and potential threats to identity faced by agri-women.

Regarding the need for shared experience, our qualitative analysis suggested that being part of a group of women in farming could be viewed as a form of comradeship. Bryant et al. (2026) similarly found that agri-women sought opportunities to connect with others who shared socio-cultural farming experiences, including whilst experiencing relational and geographic isolation from their families and their existing network of friends as they entered the farming community. However, some in our sample reported that their only source for social networks were others in farming, largely due to the lack of understanding from those outside the farming lifestyle. This does not necessarily represent comradeship; rather, it may potentially reflect a sense of separation and isolation from the rest of the community. This aligns with previous research, which considered how agri-women felt misjudged by nonfarmers and consequently sought friendship and support from others in farming (Tutor-Marcom et al., 2014). Finally, the need for initiative was a significant predictor of both stress and well-being in Study 2; however, there were little qualitative data to contextualise this need among agri-women. This might reinforce our earlier supposition that agri-women offer more support than they receive. For agri-women, asking for support from others may require them to defend their roles, abilities, or contributions. Such defence may also weaken their need for authenticity and affect their mental well-being.

According to the SWEMWBS (Stewart-Brown et al., 2009), 30.4% of participants in Study 1 and 45.2% in Study 2 reported scores indicative of low well-being. These poor figures align with studies among agri-women in the UK (Kyle et al., 2026; Wheeler & Lobley, 2023). The higher percentage of those reporting low well-being in Study 2 may reflect differences in datacollection periods or differences in the age composition of the samples. In Study 2, our sample was significantly younger and may reflect those women who are providing the bulk of childcare whilst managing their on-and off-farm duties (Elliot et al., 2018). The experience of caring for children whilst experiencing isolation and limited support has been previously described as 'overwhelming' by agri-women (Bryant et al., 2026). Irrespective, these troubling figures highlight the clear need to improve well-being in this population, and our results can provide evidence for health promotion programmes. Based on our findings and the importance of authenticity, we advocate raising awareness of the vital role of agri-women among partners, families, and the general public. This could take the form of an educational campaign that encourages spouses and other people involved in interpersonal relationships with agri-women to recognise their individuality, support their capacity to make an impact, and offer them emotional and practical support. This may involve facilitating time to support them in maintaining meaningful friendships and including them in decision-making.

We recognise the need for structural support changes to lessen the burden on agri-women, including better rural facilities for medical care, childcare, and the elderly (Becot et al., 2025).

However, such systemic and structural changes can take considerable time. Whilst advocating for these, the findings presented in this study can offer guidance to inform support programmes which can simultaneously offer agri-women the chance to learn stress management techniques and receive personalised support for their well-being. Instead of placing additional responsibility or strain on individuals, psychological interventions can empower people to cope with life's demands. Given this, although the farming lifestyle can shape relational experiences, the underlying need for satisfying and supportive relationships is universal (Baumeister & Leary, 19950801; Gökdağ et al., 2025). This suggests that formal interventions can draw on general population approaches while adapting to the specific stressors experienced and strains within the farming environment.

A widely held belief is that farmers are more likely to trust other farmers (King et al., 2023; Rust et al., 2022; Scheyett et al., 2023). Well-being interventions built around peer support networks could therefore be particularly suitable (Faulkner & Basset, 2012; King et al., 2023; Wang et al., 2021). Peer support networks foster connectedness and social support (Hardy et al., 2019). They can also help reduce the negative effects of stress, as shown in farming populations and other high-stress occupational groups, including public safety workers (Fallon et al., 2023; Liang et al., 2023). The above findings should be considered in light of this study's strengths and limitations. The main strength of this mixed-methods paper is in its integration of qualitative insights with quantitative findings from two independent samples, including replication of the original and unexpected moderation pattern using a large sample. Our inclusion of agri-women outside the primary farmer role also recognises the diverse roles of women in the farming community.

Although previous research has examined the mediating role of relational needs in psychological outcomes (Gökdağ et al., 2025), the RNSS (Žvelc et al., 2020) has not been used in a farming population before. This makes the study a novel contribution to psychological and agricultural academic literature. However, our study is limited by its use of cross-sectional quantitative data, primarily obtained via social media. This approach may have attracted women who identified with farming and were actively connected online to farming networks.

Future research among agri-women should therefore focus on longitudinal studies to capture how relational experiences change over time both within families and friendship groups.

Recruitment should also use a wider sampling strategy. This could include using direct mailing to farm households to capture women who may live on farms and are impacted relationally yet do not identify or actively participate within online farming networks.

We also recognise that our classification of participants' identities did not in some instances fully reflect the range of their roles or permit adequate interpretation. Previous literature has investigated the identities of agri-women (Brandth, 2002; Kempster et al., 2023). We recommend this body of work be extended quantitatively to identify potential differences in relational need satisfaction and well-being among various farming roles (e.g. primary farmers and farmers' wives). This type of study could incorporate a social network analysis to examine how connections in relationships differ across roles and how friendships in agri-women are experienced. Research on friendships among agri-women and how social support is offered and received across non-spousal relationships remains limited. Understanding variations in the quality and quantity of connections experienced by agri-women with diverse identities could improve the design and dissemination of interventions to enhance well-being within the farming community.

5. Conclusion

This study provides novel evidence on the relationships among stress, well-being, and relational need satisfaction among women in the UK farming community. Including women who are more indirectly connected to farming broadens the understanding of a group often overlooked in UK research. Our findings revealed complex relational dynamics, including an unexpected discovery that the association between stress and well-being was stronger at higher levels of met relational needs. As the first study to apply the Relational Need Satisfaction Scale in a UK farming context, we discuss how relationships within the farming community can both assist and burden women who typically provide support to others. Given these findings, this research advocates the development of well-being programmes specifically designed to address the unique challenges women in farming face. This includes expanding peer support networks and initiatives to raise awareness among women in farming about the crucial role they play in the community. Overall, this research lays a foundation for future studies on social support, relational needs, and the well-being of women in farming communities.
