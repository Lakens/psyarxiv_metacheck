Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

It missed 136 instances of imprecisely reported p-values (false negatives) and correctly identified 4557 cases of precisely reported p-values (true negative). Additionally, 78% of positive detections were correct (positive predictive value).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. Qualitative reports were coded according to the five-factor model of substance use motives assigning material to the dimensions of enhancement, coping, expansion, conformity, social and other motives.

Results: A total of 50 articles were included. Three survey studies were identified that reported enhancement as the major motivational drive. Discussion: The non-medical use of N2O is related to a diverse array of motivational forces, with enhancement and coping with negative psychological and physical sensations appearing to be particularly relevant. Recreational non-problematic use seems to be dominated by enhancement motives, while escalated and use with adverse consequences is more strongly related to coping motives. As systematic assessments with validated instruments regarding the motivational drives behind N2O use are lacking, further research is needed to make reliable statements about the motive profile of N2O.

1. INTRODUCTION

Nitrous oxide (N2O) is a colourless and sweet-smelling gas with an established role in anaesthesia, analgesia, anxiolysis during medical interventions, dental procedures, during childbirth, and emergency medicine (1) (2) (3). For recreational use, the substance is of interest primarily because of its psychoactive effects. Subjective effects include euphoria, dream-like states, distortion of temporal and spatial perception, and acoustic and optical illusions and hallucinations (4) (5) (6) (7).

Potential adverse effects include dizziness, nausea, vomiting, dissociation, disorientation, numbness and weakness, as well as impairment of cognitive, psychomotor and memory-based performance (7). Use over a longer period of time or in large quantities can be associated with significant health consequences. This includes nerve damage such as polyneuropathies and myelopathy due to vitamin B12 deficiency (8) (9) (10) (11). Additionally, psychological side effects such as worsening of existing affective disorders, suicidal tendencies and psychotic manifestations might occur (12, 13). Next to the high potential for neurological and psychological adverse events from N2O use, there also exists a moderate risk of developing a corresponding substance use disorder based on epidemiological (8, 14) and pharmacological (15) (16) (17) data. In addition to the risks induced by the use of N2O itself, side effects might occur as a consequence of polysubstance use which is highly prevalent during N2O use (5). A special feature of recreational N2O use is the widespread belief that it is a comparatively harmless practice (5, (18) (19) (20).

Non-medical use of N2O is relatively widespread with the Global Drug Survey reporting a lifetime prevalence of 18.8% for all respondents (21). Additionally, data from France, Germany, and New Zealand show lifetime prevalence rates of 26%, 17% and 12% of N2O use respectively (18, 20, 22), with typical N2O users being young, male adults (23, 24). Accordingly, this group has also seen the largest increase in the N2O use since 2010 (18, 23). The National Poison Information Service of the United Kingdom (25) as well as The European Monitoring Centre for Drugs (4) also report rising numbers of emergencies and health consequences associated with N2O use.

While the increase of non-medical use and associated health consequences is well documented, there is a lack of explanatory approaches for these developments. Therefore, we aim to implement a motivational perspective that may help explain N2O use patterns. Cox & Klinger (26) posited a fundamental motivational model of substance use based on the pursuit of positive incentives and the avoidance of negative ones in order to maximize positive affective changes. The decision to use a psychoactive drug is therefore a motivational one and is made by weighing the expected emotional consequences of use against the expected affective changes caused by refraining from use (26, 27). Since its publication, the motivational model of (originally alcohol) use has been adapted and expanded to account for motives for the use of other substances, incorporating substance specific subjective and pharmacological effects. Jones and colleagues (28) developed a four-factor model for use motives of prescription opioids. Due to the pharmacological effects and the associated physical stimulus of pain management, pain was added as a motive and thus replaced conformity motives that seem to be less relevant for this group of substances (28). For cannabis use, the model was extended by the addition of an expansion motive, which captures the consciousness-expanding and perception-altering components as incentives for use. This fivefactor model explained additional variance in use compared to the original model (29) and has been successfully applied to analyse the motivational drives of psychedelic substances (30), which are similar to N2O in terms of the subjective effect profile.

As substance use motives have repeatedly been shown to predict alcohol use (27, 31, 32), cannabis use (31), treatment response in substance use disorders (SUDs) (33), as well as development of a SUD (34), they might also be relevant for explaining N2O use patterns. The five-factor approach of substance use motives may therefore provide a helpful framework for the assessment of non-medical N2O use. However, to date this framework has not been systematically applied to investigate N2O use motives.

Thus, the aim of the present review is to systematically survey the existing literature on the motives for non-medical use of N2O in order to develop a deeper understanding of the underlying motivational drives of N2O use and their association with use patterns.

2. METHODS

>METHODS

2.1. Selection criteria

The present review examines the motives for non-medical N2O use, from which the following inclusion and exclusion criteria, based on PICOS (35, 36) were derived. Studies and reports that reported or collected data on explicit motives for the non-medical use of N2O by one or more people were included. Studies in which N2O use was part of a medical procedure were excluded. This includes studies in which N2O was used as part of scientific trials or as part of medical or dental procedures for analgesia or anaesthesia. No further restrictions were made on the population in terms of age, sex, medical history, or other sociodemographic variables. Studies involving animals were excluded, as the aim of this review was to investigate motives for human N2O use. Given the nature of the research question, no comparison group was included. Studies and reports had to be published in English or German. Furthermore, reports, abstracts, and posters prepared and published in the context of conferences were excluded. At the level of study design and publication time frame, no restrictions were applied, as limited available data was assumed. This systematic review was carried out in accordance with PRISMA guidelines (37).

2.2. Data sources

The data was obtained by searching in bibliographic databases PubMed (MEDLINE), EMBASE, Web of Science, and PubPsych. The simultaneous search of the PsycInfo and PsycArticles databases was carried out via the EBSCOhost platform.

2.3. Search strategy

The search algorithm followed Boolean logic and the search terms were connected accordingly with Boolean operators. All studies published up to March 28th, 2026 were identified using the following search terms: An exception was EMBASE, which provides a "Sources" filter that allows records indexed exclusively in Embase to be identified. This enabled exclusion of records also indexed in MEDLINE or in both MEDLINE and EMBASE for deduplication purposes. The filter ("AND [embase]/lim NOT ([embase]/lim AND [medline]/lim)") was automatically integrated by the platform into the search strategy to generate the final filtered results.

2.4. Selection procedure

The selection procedure was based on the inclusion and exclusion criteria defined a priori.

The selection process began with the manual review of titles and abstracts of the individual studies. Finally, the remaining full texts were assessed for eligibility. Prior to study selection, an automated deduplication was carried out using the Automated Systematic Search Deduplicator (ASySD) (38). Possible duplicates that could not be clearly identified by the system were flagged and subsequently reviewed manually after import into the reference management program Zotero (Version 6.0.37). Duplicate identification in Zotero was partly performed using the program's builtin function, which also requires manual deduplication. Additional duplicates were identified during the screening process and removed manually.

2.4.1 Data Extraction

The extraction of the relevant data was carried out on the basis of the defined criteria. The following information was documented in tabular form: the title of the study, the names of the authors, the year and country of publication, the number of people as well as their age and gender, as well as the reported or collected use motives in the form of data or quotes from the respective studies. In addition, it was determined whether the assessment of use motives in each study or report followed a quantitative or qualitative approach.

2.5. Data synthesis and analysis

To provide unified assessment of the included quantitative and qualitative studies but not case reports, all appropriate studies were assessed via the Standard Quality Assessment Criteria for Evaluating Primary Research Papers from a Variety of Fields (39) by one author (KG) and validated by a second author (LAB). According to this rating scheme, a score of 55% is considered the minimally acceptable level of quality and scores above 75% would be considered good (39).

The results of quantitative surveys were reported descriptively. Qualitative reports of use motives, such as case reports or interview analyses, were extracted as citations and assigned to the five-factor model of substance use dimensions: conformity, coping, enhancement, expansion, and social motives. Statements that justified the use decision, but could not be classified in these dimensions, were categorized as "Other". Each study was coded with "1" if a motive was reported and "0" if the corresponding motive was not present and performed by two independent coders (AES and LAB). In cases of differences in motive assignment, disputes were decided by discussion and compromise. More than one motive could be coded per study. The classification was based on the theoretical models described above, example statements from (30), questionnaire items from the Substance Use Motives Measure (SUMM) (31), and the Drinking Motive Questionnaire Revised (DMQ-R) (32, 40). The frequency of each dimension reported was determined and expressed in percentages. Externally reported or justified-suspected motives were grouped, analysed and presented separately.

4. DISCUSSION

In this systematic review, we examined the use motives for the non-medical use of N2O.

The central finding of this review is that the number of studies on this topic is considerably limited and distinguished by a lack of systematic investigations using standardized questionnaires of substance use motives. Only three systematic surveys were found which converge on enhancement as the most prominent use motive. The results of merged qualitative reports showed a different distribution pattern. Here, coping motives were mentioned most frequently, followed by the highly heterogeneous category of Other Motives. Enhancement and expansion motives formed the third and fourth largest motive groups. Social motives and use out of conformity hardly played a role in the included reports.

Interestingly the reported motives in systematic surveys differed from the results of the qualitative reports. Specifically, the quantitative survey studies (41) (42) (43) reported enhancement as the most prominent use motive, in line with previous literature (90). However, qualitative reports mostly contained use motives related to dealing with negative physical and psychological sensations such as stress management and self-medication. Thus, qualitative reports contain coping as the most prominent motive. This difference is likely due to selection bias, as a large proportion of the people interviewed in the qualitative reports had to seek medical treatment due to the negative consequences of their severe N2O use. The systematic surveys in comparison contained reports from convenience and student samples without any medical indication. This difference in pattern suggests that people with problematic use behaviour tend to use N2O out of coping motives, while people with unproblematic use behaviour are more likely to report enhancement and expansion motives which is in line with general literature on use motives in SUDs (91) (92) (93).

In terms of coping motives reported in the literature, the specific forms of coping reported fits the original formulation of the motivational model of substance use and its extensions as well as the specific subjective effects of N2O. For example, N2O use to manage depressed mood or withdrawal symptoms is reflected by clinical trials using N2O to treat these symptoms (94, 95).

Additionally, use for pain relief is in line with the analgesic properties of N2O and its use in medical practice (96). However, since the effects of N2O are only short-lasting, this motive was initially not considered as a primary motive for non-medical use.

Social motives and use out of conformity were reported by very few individuals in the qualitative reports while being a prominent motive in quantitative surveys. On the one hand, these motives also seem to be less relevant to similar psychoactive substances, such as serotonergic psychedelics (30). On the other hand, this might be another case of different samples reporting different types of use. Whelan et al. (42) reported socializing as one of their main motives and N2O users frequently report seeing their friends use N2O as well (20). Indeed, N2O is often inhaled at parties in private environments, a context of use that includes many social interactions. As such the recreational non-coping use is often related to group pressures (19) and often occurs through friends and social groups (80, 97). The recreational users, without severe problematic use and rare coping motives seem to be more strongly influenced by social contexts and social use motives. In comparison, N2O use with a problematic, coping-related use motive, might be of a more solitary nature providing less social pressure but at the same time less opportunity for social control.

In addition to the motives subsumed under the traditional motivational model of substance use, the qualitative reports contained specific use motives that might have been overlooked in previous literature. First, specifically for young adults and adolescents boredom was associated frequently with N2O use (18, 58, 79, 80), in line with previous literature on psychoactive substance use among young people in general (98). Second, the use of N2O in the context of autoerotic activities was an unexpected addition. As this motivational drive was only reported in qualitative reports related to medical emergencies, there was no indication in the included literature with regard to the prevalence of this practice. Third, several cases were found where N2O use was related to suspected suicide attempts. The cases recorded as suspected suicides were classified as such by investigating persons or relatives (88, 89). An exception is the case reported by Chan, Alfonso and Comer (67), in which the subject expressed the suicidal intention of inhalation to the medical staff in the subsequent treatment of his frostbite. It is unclear if these suicide attempts were related to previous problematic N2O use (12) or factors completely unrelated to their N2O use. Fourth, we report two cases of using N2O as a sleeping aid which has rarely been mentioned outside of the studies included here, apart from individual cases among dentists (99).

These diverse motivational facets in addition to the lack of systematic research and differential findings related to the classic motivational dimensions of substance use highlight that N2O use is not easily assigned to a specific motivational drive. Indeed, our review highlights a diverse landscape of motivations that can drive N2O use. In relation to the question of increases in N2O use and corresponding health issues our review on one hand supports previous findings that increased use among young people might be specifically related to a desire for enhancement (100) and a desire to deal with boredom (101).

On the other hand, we also report a high frequency of coping motives and issues related to problematic use. Our results suggest that the userbase of N2O is not a homogeneous group solely consisting of young people using for enhancement purposes but contains a wide variety of people of all ages and motivational drives which supports a therapeutic approach marked by respect for individual circumstances (e.g., harm reduction psychotherapy (102, 103)). Thus, an appropriate response to N2O in terms of prevention and clinical settings should be flexible insofar as being able to incorporate and deal with heterogeneous motivational drives. Most importantly however, is a proper research literature that systematically assesses use motives using established measures in combination with qualitative methods to provide a solid database of relevant use motives across the diverse userbase of N2O.

4.1. Limitations

First, a general limitation of the significance of use motives is the recording of the theoretical construct via retrospective reports. Recent research shows that motives assessed via ambulatory assessment might differ substantially from retrospective reports (104) (105) (106). This literature implies that retrospective measurements are less about the motivation present at the moment of use, but rather about attributions that the person makes about themselves with regard to their use.

Specifically, the attributions may be subject to memory errors and excessive attention to negative emotions, which could lead to more frequent reports of coping motives (104). Additionally, retrospective reports might represent an average of use motives across all use situations, while ambulatory assessments capture state motives that are present in the respective specific situation and shed light on the variance of use motives within the person (27).

Second, extracted use motives from case reports were mostly collected in the course of history taking in medical settings. Since the use of psychoactive substances is stigmatized and these conversations took place in person, unlike an anonymous survey of students, responses may have been influenced by social desirability or contextual factors. Additionally, the responses were documented according to the interpretation and understanding of the respective questioner. All of which might distort the reliability of reported use motives. Nonetheless, these case reports offered a unique data source underlining the heterogenous nature of N2O use motives and are a valuable contribution to this understudied field.

Third, the coding of the case reports was based on the motivational model of substance use which limited the available categories for coding. On the one hand this limited the number of available categories and led to our second-largest category being Other motives. On the other hand, this model provided a useful structure to compare case reports with systematic survey results.

Additionally, this allowed us to identify a number of additional use motives that do not fall into the classical structure and thus highlights the diversity of N2O users.

Finally, the contributions included in the analysis differ in their quality and methods. The studies included in the review are only of limited use to provide a representative answer to the question of the motives for N2O use, as none of these studies identified the recording of motives as the primary goal and none used a standardised measure for this purpose. With the exception of the exploratory interviews by Nabben and colleagues (80) the exploration of use motives was a secondary goal. This highlights again the need for systematic research on this issue.

5. CONCLUSION

The results of this review compile the findings of the currently existing research on use motives of non-medical use of N2O and point to a research gap on this topic. This is the only review on this question to date and the results are to be understood as an exploratory analysis due to the very limited literature on the topic. Based on our results we report that N2O is consumed for a variety of reasons, including enhancement motives, such as the search for euphoria, intoxication and new experiences, but also to cope with negative psychological and physical sensations. Specifically, enhancement motives seem more pronounced in N2O users without problematic use, while coping motives seem to play a larger role in problematic N2O use. However, systematic research on use motives across the heterogenous population of N2O users and use of appropriate wellestablished measures is still lacking.
