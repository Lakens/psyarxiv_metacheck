How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard), also Internal Review Board - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term); not IEC followed by a number, which is a technical standard - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee/Commission, in the singular or plural - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee, Bioethics Committee/Commission, Ethikkommission - Ethical Review Authority/Unit/Office/Agency - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Human Research Protection Office/Program, Office for Human Subjects Protection - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee(s) on/for (Medical and Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt - Ethics review that was not needed: Ethics review was not required, did not require further ethical review, exempt from formal human research ethics review, no new institutional ethics review

A template line the authors have not filled in (“[Add ethics approval … here.]”) is not a statement.

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

When a statement is expected

The module sorts each paper by where its data came from (the internal .data_source(); the signs and how the rule was tested are described in R/data-source.R): - own: the authors collected data from people or animals, for this paper, in an earlier study of their own, or (a protocol) will collect them; - existing_records: records about individual people that the authors obtained for this study, such as hospital records or a cohort; - existing_unclear: existing data about people from a source the module could not identify; - open_resource: an open resource any researcher can use, such as UK Biobank or NHANES; - none: no data about people or animals (a review, simulation, commentary, theory, tutorial, coding of published papers, or figures about groups); - uncertain: the signs were missing or pointed both ways; - no_text: the paper has no text.

A statement is expected for own, existing_records and existing_unclear. A paper without a statement in these classes, or in uncertain, is flagged. The module reads the text, and the results of power, stat_p_exact, stat_check, repo_check, data_check and prereg_check when they ran before it (run it after them); without stat_p_exact, it finds the p-values in the text.

On 199 PsyArXiv and medRxiv preprints labelled after the rule was fixed, the module decided right whether a statement is needed for 86.9% (the earlier live-data search: 83.4%). Its main errors: other people’s studies described in the words of a new study, records and specimens described only as counts, and open resources the paper does not call open.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine, Daniel Lakens and Cristian Mesquida

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download information from other preregistration sites, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Therefore 0% of true instances were missed, and 43% of detections were false positives. See https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/ for more details of the validation.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

How Effect Sizes in t-tests and F-tests works

The Effect Size module checks if effect sizes are correctly reported in t-tests and F-tests.

The Effect Size check searches for regular expressions that match typical ways in which effect sizes are reported. It subsequently checks different ways in which Cohen’s d, g, ηp2, and ωp2 can be computed against the reported value. If effects are missing, or might be incorrect, you the module provides a warning. The module was validated on APA reported statistical tests, and might miss effect sizes that were reported in other reporting styles. It was validated by the Metacheck team on papers published in Psychological Science.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. A TRO uses them for each trov:TransparentResearchPerformance. * run_results$exit_status: the exit status of the process that ran the script (0 = ended normally); NA when the process did not report one (a time limit, or Docker failing to start the container). * artifacts: one row per file, with stage (deposit: as downloaded from the repository, hashed from the downloaded file; before_run: the run folder just before the first script ran, after metacheck’s own changes, such as rewritten paths and unpacked archives; after_run: only the files the scripts made (status new), changed (changed) or removed (removed), as an unchanged file is the one listed before the run), path (relative to the run folder, as valid UTF-8, a byte of another encoding written as <xx>), size (bytes), sha256, mime_type (by extension, NA when unknown), source (the file’s address in the repository, for deposit). The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). Files metacheck itself copied or made between passes (see modifications, output_copied, file_placed) show in after_run too. A TRO uses the rows as trov:ResearchArtifacts and each stage as a trov:ArtifactArrangement. * modifications$sha256_before, modifications$sha256_after: the hash of the changed script as downloaded and as it ran; NA when the script is not at the same path in both (a script made from an R Markdown file). * environment: the system that ran the code (trov:TransparentResearchSystem in a TRO): metacheck_version, started_at, ended_at, host (R version, platform, operating system), sandbox, network_isolated (TRUE only when every Docker run had --network none; the process sandbox does not stop the network), internet, timeout_per_script, total_timeout, python_local (the Python run outside Docker and its version), and docker: runs (for each kind of run, r_run, r_install, python_run, python_install, the image and the docker run arguments, such as --network none, --read-only, --cap-drop ALL, no-new-privileges, the process limit and the memory and processor limits) and images (for each image, its ID and digests from docker image inspect, its creation time and the R and Python versions its environment declares).

run_results$checked says what the run of each script shows about the code, in five groups, so that a script that does not reproduce is kept apart from one the check could not test: ran (it ran to the end); code_error (it did not reproduce: it stopped with an error of the code or the deposit, could not be read, or needs a file the deposit does not hold); dependency (a package or program was not available: a package that could not be installed or was not loaded, a program outside R and Python, a system library, or R crashing in a package’s compiled code); time (it did not finish within the time limit for one script, or was not started because the time for all scripts had run out); and not_checkable (it could not be checked automatically: it needs a person, the internet, a key or another setting of the authors’ computer, one platform such as Google Colab, Windows or OpenSAFELY, more memory than the run allows, a file that was not downloaded, a screen, or a program that runs it, or it is a file of a package of the code that Python runs only as a module of that package). It is NA for files run inside another script, files that are not an analysis, files not read, and a Docker failure. summary_table counts each group (repro_ran_n, repro_code_error_n, repro_dependency_n, repro_time_n, repro_not_checkable_n), and the report’s first sentence gives them. The traffic light reflects the static readiness signals by default; a script in code_error that stopped with an error makes it red, as a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure, and a script in one of the other three groups keeps it from being green. Calls that need a screen, which the run does not have (dev.print(), dev.copy2pdf(), dev.copy2eps(), savePlot(), windows(), win.graph(), x11(), quartz(), View()), are replaced before the run by calls that need none, which modifications records as screen_call_replaced. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

Although number neutralisation-the loss of singular-plural distinctions-is relatively rare in paradigms of personal pronouns, it is the most common pattern of pronominal syncretism cross-linguistically and exhibits systematic asymmetries across person values. Number distinctions are most frequently neutralised in third person, less so in second person, and least often in first person. While this generalisation has been widely noted, its empirical foundation to date remains relatively weak, and no clear mechanistic explanation has been established for why this typological asymmetry might arise. To address this gap, we investigate whether cross-linguistic patterns of number neutralisation might reflect biases in learning, such that patterns that are easier to learn are more reliably transmitted over time and consequently become more prevalent across languages. We first conduct a large-scale synchronic analysis of pronominal paradigms (1,243 languages), confirming a frequency-based hierarchy (3rd > 2nd > 1st) but challenging its previously assumed implicational nature (1st > 2nd > 3rd; i.e., if in 1st, then in 2nd, and if in 2nd, then in 3rd). A diachronic phylogenetic analysis suggests a tendency towards greater stability of number neutralisation in the order 3rd > 2nd > 1st. Overall, however, it provides limited support for the frequency-based hierarchy and no clear support for the implicational hierarchy. Finally, an artificial language learning experiment testing the learnability of different neutralisation patterns reveals that learners do not mirror typological tendencies: contrary to predictions-and in line with an author-based bias-first-person number neutralisation appears to be most learnable. These findings suggest that the typological evidence for the number neutralisation hierarchy might not be as robust as previously assumed, and that learning biases alone might not straightforwardly explain the typological tendencies. This challenges the link between learning biases and typological distributions, suggesting that pronominal paradigms evolve in a manner that is less constrained by learning biases, and/or that alternative processing and communicative biases may better account for crosslinguistic distributions of pronominal number neutralisation.

1 Introduction

Languages vary in how they express number distinctions. Some languages may morphologically mark differences between singular and plural referents via the use of distinct word forms or affixes on nouns (e.g., English cat vs. plural cats) and/or pronouns (e.g., English she vs. plural they or Spanish ella vs. ellas), whereas other languages do not mark such distinctions on the noun and/or pronoun directly (e.g., Japanese neko, which can refer to either a single cat or a group of cats, depending on the context). Moreover, it is not unusual for number distinctions expressed in some parts of the system to be neutralised in others. For example, in standard English, you can refer to either a single addressee or a group including the addressee, despite number being distinguished in other parts of the English pronominal system; in this case, number is neutralised for second person pronouns.

Here we focus on number neutralisation 1 in personal pronouns (e.g., as in the case of English you), where data are most readily available. In particular, we investigate a well-known cross-linguistic regularity whereby number neutralisation is more frequent in third-person than in second-person pronouns, and more frequent in second-person than in first-person pronouns (Corbett, 2000; Cysouw, 2001 Cysouw, , 2003a)). This paper has two parts. In the first part, we analyse, both synchronically and diachronically, the hitherto largest typological dataset of pronominal paradigms to quantitatively assess the extent to which this regularity is supported by the currently available cross-linguistic evidence. We find clear synchronic support for the frequency-based hierarchy (3rd > 2nd > 1st), alongside weaker support for its probabilistic implicational interpretation (1st > 2nd > 3rd). The phylogenetic analysis reveals some tendencies consistent with these asymmetries, although the evidence is more limited and uncertain. In the second part, we investigate one possible explanatory mechanism for this typological tendency. Building on the idea that patterns that are easier to learn are more reliably transmitted over time and consequently become more prevalent across languages (Culbertson, Smolensky, & Legendre, 2012; Gentner & Bowerman, 2009; Kirby, Griffiths, & Smith, 2014; Maldonado & Culbertson, submitted; Saldana, Herce, & Bickel, 2022a), we experimentally assess the learnability of different patterns of number neutralisation. 1 Throughout this paper, we use number neutralisation descriptively to refer to the non-expression, or collapse, of a number distinction. In this descriptive sense, we use neutralisation for what may also be more broadly referred to as syncretism. We prefer neutralisation to syncretism here because it foregrounds the absence of the number contrast rather than the resulting identity of forms. We do not, however, engage with the distinction between neutralisation and uninflectedness in the stricter morphosyntactic sense (Baerman, Brown, & Corbett, 2005). Although apparently typologically rare, the absence of a number distinction in personal pronouns (our paradigms of study) does not necessarily entail its absence elsewhere in the morphosyntactic system: languages are attested in which a relatively sparse inventory of number distinctions in independent pronouns coexists with a richer set of distinctions in verbal inflection (see, e.g., cases in Nuclear Trans New Guinean languages such as Kuman discussed in Corbett, 2000; Foley, 1986). Since our investigation is restricted to the default S personal pronouns-i.e., those that can function as the sole argument (S) of an intransitive clause-and does not systematically consider verbal agreement or other categories, we do not attempt to distinguish such cases and use number neutralisation in the descriptive sense defined above.

In phonology, for example, neutralisation typically affects natural classes defined by shared phonological values: for example, in German, the voicing distinction is neutralised among obstruents in word-final position (e.g., rad 'wheel' /Ka:d/ → [Ka:t], becoming homophonous with rat 'advice' /Ka:t/ → [Ka:t]).

In Cysouw (2003b)'s proposal, the hierarchy was also formulated as 1 > 2 > 3 with an implicational reading: when number neutralisation is found in first person, it is also found in second person, and if it is found in second person, it is also found in third person (section 10.1.4, pages 299-301). 3 This implicational formulation thus makes stronger predictions than the frequency-based formulation. The latter is necessarily implied by the former, but not vice versa. The data reported in Cysouw (2001) (i.e., the same as in Cysouw, 2003b) support the implicational generalisation, and thus also the frequency ranking (see Table 2).

Importantly, Cysouw (2003b) also distinguishes between the synchronic and diachronic interpretations of the hierarchy. While arguing that the Horizontal Homophony Hierarchy is a strong typological generalisation over pronominal paradigms cross-linguistically, he concludes that it is too strong when interpreted as a hypothesis about diachronic change.

Based on a qualitative analysis of cognate paradigms, he observes that paradigms can move through the hierarchy relatively easily, without necessarily following the intermediate steps predicted by the proposed ordering.

Furthermore, Cysouw (2003a) later questioned the traditional reading of implicational hierarchies and proposed that what looks like an implicational hierarchy may actually be a side effect of a symmetrical correlation combined with skewed frequencies of occurrence of the individual phenomena-potentially the frequency hierarchy 3 > 2 > 1 in this case.

In this paper we take a step back and aim to provide additional evidence for the Horizontal Homophony Hierarchy, which could help elucidate and formulate a hypothesis about the source of this typological tendency.

The contribution of our typological quantitative analysis of cross-linguistic asymmetries in number neutralisation across person values-to the already available description of the phenomenon reviewed from Cysouw (2003b)-is threefold. Firstly, we survey data from 1,243 languages from 257 different families; moreover, this dataset focuses on pronominal paradigms alone in order to maximise comparability. Secondly, we assess the cross-linguistic statistical asymmetries of number neutralisation across person values in the available synchronic data controlling for family relations. Thirdly, we use phylogenetic modelling to compare the probabilities of gaining and losing number neutralisation across person values, as well as their long-term evolutionary preferences, across a wide range of language families.

2.2 Phylogenetic modelling

While the synchronic analysis establishes the cross-linguistic distribution of number neutralisation across person values, it cannot determine whether these asymmetries arise from differences in the rates at which neutralisation emerges or is lost. To investigate these evolutionary dynamics, we use phylogenetic comparative methods-a methodology borrowed from computational biology (e.g., Felsenstein, 1981; Harvey & Pagel, 1991) and increasingly applied to questions in linguistics (e.g., Cathcart, 2018; Cathcart & Bickel, 2025; Greenhill, Heggarty, & Gray, 2020)-to estimate person-specific gain and loss rates of number neutralisation, as well as the stationary probabilities implied by these evolutionary processes.

Data. As in our previous analysis, we used data from the Parabank Pronoun Database (Greenhill, 2022), Tymber (Ivani & Zakharko, 2019), and ATLAs (Chousou-Polydouri et al., 2025; Inman et al., 2025). However, here we restrict analyses to languages (N = 220) from the subset of seven families for which reliable Bayesian phylogenies with realistic time-calibrated chronologies (representing divergence times in years) are available: 75 languages from Austronesian (Gray, Drummond, & Greenhill, 2009), 51 from Indo-European (Chang, Hall, Cathcart, & Garrett, 2015), 42 from Pama-Nyungan (Bowern & Atkinson, 2012), 16 from Sino-Tibetan (Sagart et al., 2019), 14 from Uto-Aztecan (Greenhill et al., 2023), 11 from Bantu (Grollemund et al., 2015), and 11 from Dravidian (Kolipakam et al., 2018). These 220 languages are all found within the Parabank (Greenhill, 2022) and Tymber (Ivani & Zakharko, 2019) databases, and exactly 10% of them contain number neutralisation (i.e., N = 22). Unlike in our previous synchronic analysis, here we include paradigms with missing data (N = 1), treating missing values as ambiguous-assigning equal likelihood to the presence and absence of number neutralisation rather than assuming either state-following standard phylogenetic practice (Felsenstein, 2004).

Phylogenetic models.

We fit two phylogenetic models, a binary one to estimate the likelihood of finding number neutralisation for a given person value (i.e., 1, 2 or 3), and a multistate one to estimate the likelihood of finding a type of patterns of number neutralisation in pronominal systems (i.e., 1 only, 2 only, 3 only, 2 & 3, 1&2, all, etc.; see Table 3). We fit a binary in addition to a multistate model because the multistate model cannot generate unattested combinations, and therefore each model sheds light on certain properties of evolution that the other cannot.

We assume that within the evolution of categorical linguistic data (such as the data we work with), features undergo state changes over a phylogeny according to a Continuous-Time Markov (CTM) process (e.g., Cathcart, Hölzl, Jäger, Widmer, & Bickel, 2020). Given the linguistic data and phylogenetic representations of the languages under study, the transition rates of the process can be estimated via Bayesian inference to test hypotheses regarding the dynamics of change of the features in question. In other words, given a language tree and data at the tips (i.e., terminal nodes = attested languages) of the tree, we can recover the transition rates most compatible with the observed data and tree, which can tell us, e.g., whether one transition is more frequent than another, among other matters. CTM models can tell us a great deal regarding the evolutionary trajectories of number neutralisation. Certain change types may be more frequent than others, and relative frequencies of one type of change with respect to another can be quantified as well. Another useful property of CTM chains is the stationary probability distribution, which represents the proportion of time that the system is expected to spend in a particular state as time approaches infinity, and can be interpreted as representing long-term preferences for different states.

Typological tendencies can arise from different sources. For example, a given pat-353 tern might be more prevalent than others because it is easier to represent cognitively and 354 consequently easier to learn, or because it facilitates our communication. In the domain of person systems, linguistic theories often posit representational constraints for typological tendencies, grounded in morphosyntactic or semantic similarity. For instance, syncretism is more likely to appear across forms that share morphosyntactic or semantic values. For example, loss of a formal distinction between 3SG and 3PL is more likely than the loss of a distinction between 1SG and 3PL due to the 3rd person value overlap in the former (Baerman, Brown, & Corbett, 2002; Corbett, 2000; Harbour, 2008; Harbour, Adger, & Béjar, 2008; Harley & Ritter, 2002).

Interestingly, previous research has shown that the tendency towards similaritybased structure in morphological paradigms is also reflected in language learning: adult learners find systems with syncretism between meanings or functions that share feature values easier to learn (Johnson, Gao, Smith, Rabagliati, & Culbertson, 2021; Maldonado & Culbertson, 2021; Nevins, 2015; Nevins, Rodrigues, & Tang, 2015; Pertsova, 2011 Pertsova, , 2014;; Saldana, Herce, & Bickel, 2022b). The alignment between typology and learning has often been taken to suggest a causal relation: typologically frequent patterns may recur, in part, because they are easier to learn (e.g., Culbertson et al., 2012; Gentner & Bowerman, 2009; Herce, Saldana, Mansfield, & Bickel, 2023; Kirby et al., 2014; Maldonado & Culbertson, submitted; Maldonado, Saldana, & Culbertson, 2020; Saldana et al., 2022a). The underlying assumption is that learning biases (i.e., systematic preferences in language learning that make some linguistic patterns easier to learn than others) are replicated through linguistic transmission and, over time, contribute to shaping cross-linguistic distributions (Bickel, 2015; Blythe & Croft, 2021; Culbertson & Smolensky, 2012; Reali & Griffiths, 2009; Smith, 2018). Crucially, learning biases themselves are taken to reflect our cognitive architecture: a pattern is more easily learned when it aligns with specific aspects of our mental representation, such as natural classes, shared feature values or core concepts.

In this context, accounting for the Horizontal Homophony Hierarchy purely by relying on a representational constraint-namely, based on shared feature values-presents a theoretical challenge. Under a simple framework where person-number paradigms are defined by a person feature with three values (i.e., 1 for the author who is the speaker/signer, 2 for the addressee, and 3 for other), and a number feature with two values (sg and pl), all three forms of number neutralisation (1sg=1pl, 2sg=2pl, and 3sg=3pl) entail the same degree of feature value overlap. This simple framework therefore predicts equal frequency and learnability for different patterns of number neutralisation, failing to explain why their cross-linguistic distributions diverge systematically. However, we do observe a number of interactions of person and number features that may be connected to this empirical asymmetry.

First, it has long been observed that third person behaves differently from first and second person, both semantically and morphosyntactically (Benveniste, 1956; Corbett, 2000; Forchheimer, 1953; Greenberg, 1963; Harley & Ritter, 2002; Siewierska, 2004). Unlike first and second person, which are true indexicals, third person often shows zero agreement marking while first and second have overt marking (Bickel, Witzlack-Makarevich, Zakharko, & Iemmolo, 2015; Siewierska, 2013; Watkins, 1962). Additionally, third person is never dominant in coordinated or quantificational structures, meaning that it rarely controls agreement in mixed-person contexts (Greenberg, 1963; Sauerland, 2008, and references therein). For example, in the Spanish sentence Ella y yo fuimos al cine ('She and I went to the cinema'), the coordination of a third-and a first-person pronoun triggers first-person plural agreement on the verb. These patterns suggest that third person is the most unmarked value, possibly lacking semantic import altogether (see Sauerland, 2008, for an account where third person has no semantics and only gets the 'neither author nor addressee' meaning by pragmatic reasoning) or even morphosyntactic features (Harley & Ritter, 2002, for an analysis where third person equals the absence of features). Further evidence for this view comes from the fact that third person does not inherently exclude reference to the author or addressee. This is clear in quantificational statements such as Each of us/you loves music or Everyone loves their mother, where the set quantified over includes the author or addressee.

Second, and crucially for our purposes, the interpretation of plurality also appears to vary across person values. First and second plural forms (e.g., 1pl we and 2pl you, youse, yinz, or y'all in English) are not simply the plural equivalents of their singular counterparts in the way that dogs is the plural of dog. Unlike standard nominal plurals,foot_8 1pl and 2pl pronouns do not necessarily refer to a collection of authors or addressees (additive meaning).

Rather, they often refer to a group of people that just includes the author or addressee (associative meaning) (Corbett, 2000; Cysouw, 2003b; Daniel, 2020; Moravcsik, 2003b). For example, the pronoun we usually refers to the author and some associated individuals and not to several people speaking in unison (except in some rare chorus we cases). Similarly, the plural you might refer to a group of addressees (i.e., an audience) or to a single addressee plus associates. In contrast, 3pl pronouns refer to a group of people where no one needs to be participating in the conversation. As such, they behave just like standard nominal plurals, referring to a homogeneous group of participants (additive meaning). Interestingly, recent experimental work (Maldonado & Saldana, 2022) has shown that the interpretation of plurality does indeed differ between 3rd and 1st/2nd persons.

Furthermore, this interpretative difference correlates with morphological expression in the nominal domain: the more likely a plural is interpreted additively, the more likely it is to share standard inflectional morphology with nominals (Corbett, 2000). For example, when there are splits in the regularity of number morphology within pronominal paradigms, we can find paradigms where the 3rd person's number inflection is regular (i.e., shared with the nominal plurality) and 1pl and 2pl forms are irregular (e.g., like in Turkish or Hungarian; Kornfilt, 1997; Moravcsik, 2003a), while other types of regularity splits are rarer (Moravcsik, 2003a).

These correlations make it tempting to partly explain the Horizontal Homophony Hierarchy by relying on the contrast between associative and additive interpretations of plurality. However, to the best of our knowledge, there is no clear account of the Horizontal Homophony Hierarchy that spells out this connection, and in particular, why we should expect differences between 1 and 2 (see, however, Maldonado & Saldana, 2022).

Given the simple account above, the differences in how plural personal pronouns are interpreted cannot come from number values, as they are consistent across person values.

Person-number combinations have thus been modelled in a way that allows their morphosemantics to accommodate both associative and additive interpretations of plurality, leaving room for preferred interpretations to vary depending on world knowledge or other properties of the referent, such as animacy or indexicality (Bickel & Nichols, 2007; Comrie, 1989; Corbett, 2000; Dixon, 1994; Forchheimer, 1953; Siewierska, 2004; Silverstein, 1976; Smith-Stark, 1974). 10Other possibilities are conceivable, such as assigning different number values to associative and additive pluralityfoot_10 , or to assume that the person and number features do not interact in a fully compositional way. The degree of compositionality may itself vary across person-number combinations, such that plural forms associated with first or second person are less decomposable than third-person plural forms. For example, 1pl may be less decomposable than 3pl, such that it does not consist straightforwardly of a person value combined with a number value but instead forms a more unitary representation. There is, however, some evidence in favour of compositionality in person-number systems (Wang & Maldonado, 2026). At the same time, we do not want to exclude the possibility that compositionality is gradual, and that the relation between 1sg and 1pl is less compositionally transparent than that between 3sg and 3pl. However, we currently lack a formal theory that fully explains this connection.

3.2 Aim of the experimental study

We hope it is clear from the above that we cannot formulate a specific hypothesis regarding how these person-number interactions are cognitively encoded and may affect learning and, in turn, typology. In this study, however, we aim at investigating more directly the link between typological patterns and learning. The presence of a learning bias that correlates with typology would highlight the need to develop such a theory. Conversely, the absence of such a bias would suggest exploring alternative explanations not rooted in representational constraints-including other cognitive biases related to language use or communicative efficiency (e.g., Gibson et al., 2019; Hahn, Mathew, & Degen, 2022; Hawkins, 1994; Regier, Kemp, & Kay, 2015).

To investigate whether the patterns consistent with the Horizontal Homophony Hierarchy could be partly explained by learning biases we use an Artificial Language Learning paradigm (ALL). Specifically, we will test whether adults learning a novel person-number system find certain patterns of number neutralisation easier to learn than others. ALL paradigms serve to test the existence of learning biases in a controlled environment. The assumption is that preferences observed in ALL experiments, at least partly, reflect learning biases in adult language transmission that influence language change. As new variants spread through learning and use by adolescents and adults (Aitchison, 2003; Blythe & Croft, 2021; Raviv, Blasi, & Kempe, 2025), repeated transmission favours the selection and maintenance of cognitively preferred patterns (Bickel, 2015; Reali & Griffiths, 2009; Smith, 2018).

Here, we can test directly whether learners' preferences align with the cross-linguistically attested frequency asymmetries of number neutralisation across person values, which, at least synchronically and considering all data, follow the Horizontal Homophony Hierarchy.

In the following sections, we discuss our findings and their implications 676 for the relationship between individual learning biases and cross-linguistic regularities. Altogether, our results thus corroborate a frequency-based hierarchy of number neutralisation 3 > 2 > 1 in a large (largest to date) cross-linguistic dataset of personal pronouns; they also suggest that we should question a strict implicational reading of the hierarchy.

In order to test whether or not these tendencies are also found diachronically (as we would expect with robust typological tendencies), we use phylogenetic modelling techniques (Bickel, 2015; Cathcart, 2018; Greenhill et al., 2020; Jäger & Wahle, 2021). Our phylogenetic analyses, however, are based on a notably smaller sample of languages (N = 220), as they are restricted to families for which reliable phylogenetic trees are available. Unlike the synchronic analysis, the phylogenetic analysis did not provide clear support for the full frequency ordering 3 > 2 > 1, although it showed support for a higher stationary probability of number neutralisation in third than in first person, and a tendency towards a higher probability in third than in second person. Results also suggested a gradual decrease in loss rates from first to third person, despite relatively similar gain rates. This raises the possibility that differences in the stability of number neutralisation, rather than its emergence, contribute to the observed typological asymmetry. However, the considerable uncertainty in the phylogenetic estimates prevents firm conclusions about these diachronic tendencies.

Results from the multistate phylogenetic model likewise did not provide independent support for the full implicational hierarchy 1 > 2 > 3: the apparent strict implication from first to second person follows trivially from the restriction to attested states, and there is only limited support for the implication from second to third person, with relatively low or moderate estimates, wide credible intervals and uneven posterior support across comparisons.

The ordering of the posterior means of the conditional probabilities also differs between the two analyses: while P (3|1, 2) is higher than P (3|¬1, 2) in the synchronic analysis, the phylogenetic posterior means show the reverse ordering, although these estimates are highly uncertain.

The difference in the strength of support for the cross-linguistic tendencies found across our synchronic and diachronic models is (at least partially) driven by the difference in the data samples. The data used in the phylogenetic model represents only 18% of the languages with number neutralisation we test in the synchronic analysis. Moreover, in this subset of the data there is no representation of the rarest patterns such as and . The differences in probabilities across the two models may also stem from their different scopes: while the regression model captures only synchronic associations between pattern types, the phylogenetic analyses explicitly model their diachronic dynamics and long-term behaviour.

Altogether, the results of our typological analyses support the Horizontal Homophony Hierarchy no-neutralisation > 3 > 2 > 1, where > denotes differences in frequency.

They are also compatible with a weak probabilistic interpretation of the implicational hierarchy 1 > 2 > 3, although this pattern is driven primarily by the high probability of number neutralisation in third person, rather than by consistent implications across all person values. The typological dataset used in our study thus allows us to quantitatively assess (Cysouw, 2001 (Cysouw, , 2003b) ) proposal using a larger dataset and more comprehensive statistical models. Note however, that this applies to the synchronic model, because as mentioned in the previous paragraph, we have unattested patterns in our phylogenetic models and are thus cautious in interpreting the latter results. It is also worth noting that our results speak only to tendencies in personal pronouns; whether or not the tendencies are the same in person-number verbal agreement paradigms (included also in Cysouw, 2001 Cysouw, , 2003b) ) remains to be tested in future work.

4.2 Learning biases do not mirror typological tendencies

In our experimental study, we assess whether this cross-linguistic pattern involving number neutralisation in pronominal paradigms (i.e., the Horizontal Homophony Hierarchy) has a cognitive correlate during language learning. We aimed to provide behavioural evidence that could be used to construct/support an explanation for this typological regularity, in the spirit of the Typological Prevalence Hypothesis (Gentner & Bowerman, 2009) and the growing body of Artificial Language Learning experiments linking learning and usage to typological distributions (e.g. Culbertson et al., 2012; Herce et al., 2023; Maldonado & Culbertson, 2021, submitted; Martin, Abels, Ratitamkul, & Culbertson, 2019; Saldana et al., 2022a).

We compared how well Spanish-speaking adults learned six different systems of personal pronouns. Each of these systems instantiated a specific pattern of number neutralisation. We expected that ease of learning would mirror cross-linguistic frequency: systems involving number neutralisation in third person were predicted to be easier to learn than systems with number neutralisation in the second person, and these, in turn, easier than systems where it is found in the first person. For systems where number neutralisation occurred across two person values we predicted that a system where number is neutralised both second and third person should be the easiest.

Our findings, however, reveal that learners' preferences in our experiment do not match our preregistered predictions. Systems involving number neutralisation in 3rd person are the hardest to learn. A closer examination of our results shows that differences in learning alternative systems mostly stem from a special status for the first person value (see Figures 11 and 12). Forms for the first person are generally easier to learn, especially when they are shared across singular and plural referents (and therefore number is neutralised): a single form for first singular and plural is easier to learn than a single form for second or third persons. It is not entirely clear whether the presence of number neutralisation differs across 2nd and 3rd person in terms of learning difficulty as number neutralisation seems to offer the same advantage (over distinct forms) for the two values. However, we find that forms for the 2nd person seem to be generally harder to acquire than for 3rd (which are in turn lower than for 1st). Putting together these results, we can conclude that what drives the difference we observe in our confirmatory models is the greater advantage of number neutralisation in 1st person and the overall worse performance for acquiring 2nd person (which is worst in systems where singular and plural values are distinctly marked).

These experimental results do not support a straightforward learning bias underlying the Horizontal Homophony Hierarchy, altogether challenging the link between learning biases and typological distributions in this domain, provided that the learning preferences observed in our ALL experiment are informative about those operating during language learning and transmission. However, we cannot rule out the possibility that the absence of the predicted bias reflects limitations of the experimental task, which may not adequately approximate the ecological conditions under which pronominal systems are learned and transmitted. Moreover, even though we have found support for cross-linguistic regularities in number neutralisation patterns (and robust, at least synchronically), number neutralisation in pronominal paradigms is rare. It is thus also possible that such rare (although robust) regularities are less likely to be reflected in learning biases at the level we are testing them. If these regularities were motivated, however, our findings indicate that those regularities regarding number neutralisation patterns in pronominal paradigms may be better explained by alternative pressures in language use such as communicative need (i.e., how often we need to convey a certain meaning versus others), discourse saliency (i.e., how relevant signalling a distinction is to the discourse), or efficiency in information processing (i.e., how easy it is to process linguistic information in context) (e.g., see Gibson et al., 2019; Hawkins, 1994; Regier et al., 2015; Zaslavsky, Maldonado, & Culbertson, 2021). For example, one could imagine that in real-life conversations, it is more common to need to distinguish cardinality among referents involving the author or the addressee because these are the speech participants and they might often refer to themselves (see also Bickel & Nichols, 2007). In contrast, the need to distinguish might be less frequent between one or more individuals who are not part of the conversation (i.e., third person).

Likewise, these person asymmetries could be accounted for by positing a bias based on efficient information processing, specifically linked to pronoun resolution. Third-person pronouns frequently refer back to discourse antecedents expressed by proper names or definite descriptions, which can independently provide information about the number of the referent. In such cases, neutralising number in a 3rd-person pronoun may involve less loss of information, because the distinction remains accessible elsewhere in the linguistic context. This could make number neutralisation in 3rd-person pronouns less costly from the perspective of communicative efficiency than in 1st or 2nd person pronouns, which are true indexicals and whose referents are identified primarily through the speech context. The impact of communicative need, context salience and pronominal resolution was purposely excluded from our study. Therefore, evidence supporting these alternative hypotheses remains to be provided in future work, which will specifically target these pressures.

We have so far discussed what could explain the typology in the absence of evidence for a learning bias. Before we conclude, let us briefly discuss what our learning results might reflect. Learnability here could still reflect meaning similarity, as it does in other instances of syncretism in pronominal systems (Maldonado & Culbertson, 2021; Saldana et al., 2022b, among others). Our results suggest that the plural and singular 1st person referents are more similar to each other than the corresponding singular and plural in the 2nd and 3rd persons. In other words, there is more in common between referring to the author alone and referring to a group that includes the author than between, for example, referring to a single other person and to multiple others.

To encode this idea representationally, one needs to posit a hierarchical representation of person values, where the author's role associated with the first person is represented differently from other person roles (e.g., it has more weight or it's considered default; see Harley & Ritter, 2002; Zaslavsky et al., 2021). This idea is not new: the author is known to be a particularly salient concept, and learners are known to prefer systems that have specific forms to refer to the author (i.e., they show an author-based bias, e.g., Maldonado & Culbertson, 2021; Maldonado & Saldana, 2022, which can be related to a more general self or egocentric bias, e.g., Sui, He, and Humphreys, 2012 and Moyer, Harrigan, Hacquard, and Lidz (2015)). Arguably, the special status of the author role impacts the interaction between person and number values. Under this view, singular and plural first person are more similar to each other because the author role is more central in their representations than, for example, the addressee role in the representation of second person referents. Essentially, this means that learning patterns of number neutralisation in person systems will depend on how important or salient the referent of a person value is. Since the author is more important than anything else, using the same form to refer to an author and a group that includes the author is relatively easy because the crucial distinction (author versus non-author) has already been made.

In principle, this idea should also extend to second and third persons, predicting differences between them. Following the same rationale, third person singular and plural are arguably the least similar to each other. Since the third person is defined by opposition (i.e., it means neither author nor addressee) or by a lack of person features (Sauerland, 2008), it is not entirely clear what is common between singular and plural referents with the same person value. The same is not true for the second person, which involves the presence of the addressee role. The high learnability of is consistent with this account. However, the absence of an overall advantage for second-over third-person number neutralisation in our experiment makes it difficult to fully endorse this explanation.

But why do we not find a bias favouring the neutralisation of the singular-plural distinction in first person in typology? Possibly, this bias specifically shows up in our experimental study because of the particular setup we use, and the focus on categorisation, which in this case might not be the driving force shaping cross-linguistic tendencies. In our experiments, the importance of the author role might be inflated because, for example, the avatar corresponding to the author is more fixed than the others. This could explain why this bias might play a stronger role here than it does in real language use (and thus in typology).

Further research should aim to better understand the impact that the representation of person and number might have on our conception of number neutralisation.

5 Conclusion

In this paper, we provide a comprehensive assessment of number neutralisation in personal pronoun paradigms, combining typological analysis and behavioural experiments to evaluate both cross-linguistic regularities and the potential role of language learning in shaping them. We examined typological asymmetries in number neutralisation across person values by testing the Horizontal Homophony Hierarchy through a large-scale typological dataset (1,243 languages), a hierarchical regression model, and a diachronic phylogenetic analysis. Our findings support a frequency-based hierarchy 3rd > 2nd > 1st and are compatible with a weak probabilistic implicational hierarchy 1st > 2nd > 3rd, driven primarily by the higher probability of number neutralisation in third person rather than by consistent implications across person values. Evidence for this implicational pattern is, however, substantially less clear in the phylogenetic analysis where we could only include 18% of the data. To investigate whether learning biases could explain this typological tendency, we conducted an artificial language learning experiment. Our results show that first-person number neutralisation appears to be most learnable, suggesting that learning biases alone cannot straightforwardly account for typological distributions.

Our study calls for a re-evaluation of how learning biases based on meaning categorisation influence robust cross-linguistic regularities for rarer phenomena and emphasises the need to further investigate the role of communicative pressures and language use in shaping typological patterns of number neutralisation in personal pronouns. More broadly, our findings highlight the importance of integrating large-scale typological synchronic and diachronic analyses, as well as direct tests of underlying mechanisms to better define typological regularities and understand the forces driving them.
