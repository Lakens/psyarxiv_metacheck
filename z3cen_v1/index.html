Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine, Daniel Lakens and Cristian Mesquida

Overall, among all instances flagged as power statements, 90.6% were correct (positive predictive value).

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). Of the misses, 13 are starred table notes (“*p < .05”) that the module leaves out on purpose and 11 are p-values it did not find; of the wrong reports, 20 are p-values the ground truth does not contain (such as “ps < .05”) and 6 use ≤ or ≥. How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Introduction

One of the oldest problems of psychometrics involves understanding what the items of a test or questionnaire measure and how well they measure it (Lord & Novick, 1968; McDonald, 1999). For more than half a century, two traditions have addressed this problem in paralell: factor analysis and item response theory (IRT). The factor analysis tradition investigates item quality via item loadings, communalities and uniqueness using the empirical correlation matrix, while IRT estimates parameters such as discrimination and difficulty, that define the probability of a response to an item as a function of one or more latent traits (Reckase, 2009; Wirth & Edwards, 2007).

These two traditions are connected, since for binary items the normal-ogive item response model and the common factor model are reparametrizations of each other (Kamata & Bauer, 2008; Takane & Leeuw, 1987). This has allowed limited-information estimators based on tetrachoric correlations to coexist with full-information item response theory estimators (Bock & Aitkin, 1981; Christoffersson, 1975; Knol & Berger, 1991; Muthén, 1978).

In the past decade or so, a third perspective was proposed from the field of network psychometrics (Epskamp, Maris, Waldorp, & Borsboom, 2018). In this perspective, psychological attributes are represented as a system of mutually interacting variables, rather than a product of common latent causes (Borsboom & Cramer, 2013; van der Maas et al., 2006). For binary items, the corresponding network model is the Ising model (Ising, 1925; van Borkulo et al., 2014), whose formal relation to item response theory was established by Marsman et al. (2018), who showed, among other results, that its Curie-Weiss form, which assumes a single latent dimension, is statistically equivalent to a Rasch model. Although network psychometrics has moved in a direction in which the identification of latent dimensions or clusters of nodes is not considered relevant, the field of exploratory graph analysis evolved into a full-blown measurement research area. Golino and Epskamp (2017) proposed the technique of exploratory graph analysis (EGA) to estimate the dimensionality of multivariate data by combining networks and community detection algorithms to identify sets of densely connected nodes or variables. Simulation studies have shown that EGA is one of the most accurate methods for dimensionality assessment, being in several conditions more accurate than the gold standard methods in psychometrics for factor retention, such as parallel analysis (Christensen, Garrido, Guerra-Peña, & Golino, 2024a; Cosemans, Rosseel, & Gelper, 2022; Golino et al., 2020).

EGA has since been extended to assess the fit of dimensional structures (Golino et al., 2021), the stability of dimensions across bootstrap samples (Christensen & Golino, 2021a), local dependence and item redundancy (Christensen, Garrido, & Golino, 2023), unidimensionality (Christensen, 2024), and the dynamic structure of intensive longitudinal data (Golino, Christensen, Moulder, Kim, & Boker, 2022; Golino, Nesselroade, & Christensen, 2025).

A central development for the EGA framework was the introduction of network loadings by Christensen and Golino (2021b). The original network loadings partition the connections of the items or nodes across the communities estimated via EGA, producing an item-by-dimension matrix akin to factor loadings. More recently, Christensen, Golino, Abad, and Garrido (2025) corrected the recovery of the network loadings signs, adjusted the within-community sum for the missing self-edge, and stabilized loadings magnitude across communities with different sizes. In a simulation study with data generated from factor models (Christensen et al., 2025), the revised network loadings reproduced the population factor loadings with a correlation ranging from 0.953 to 0.966. Furthermore, Christensen et al. (2025) showed that network scores computed from the revised network loadings recovers the correlations between factors without requiring a rotation. With these developments, EGA moved from being a dimensionality assessment technique to a general methodology that can be used to describe where each item is positioned relative to those dimensions.

Based on the connection between IRT and full-information factor models, it is reasonable to think about using the EGA-based network loadings to estimate multidimensional item parameters in a network perspective. However, in order to do this effectively, a problem needs to be addressed, i.e., the difference between a coordinate and a magnitude.

From a practical perspective, a researcher using EGA to identify the partition of the multidimensional space, and that wants to estimate multidimensional discrimination, intercept, or difficulty, needs to fit a separate multidimensional IRT model (MIRT; Reckase, 1997b Reckase, , 1997a Reckase, , 2009)), with its own estimator, rotation, and potentially its own allocation of items to dimensions. Therefore, the parameters estimated following this procedure have no relationship with the structure estimated via EGA. This apparent shortcut, i.e., to treat revised network loadings as standardized factor loadings and convert them into item response parameters, does not work, and the reason is quite straightforward. Christensen et al. (2025) shows that the magnitudes of network loadings are determined by: 1) the topology of the network, and 2) by the standardization used to compute the network loadings. Network loadings are not a by-product of the proportion of the variaance of the latent-response that the item shares with the latent dimensions, as in factor models or IRT. Treating network loadings as factor loadings will generate three negative consequences: 1) inadmissible uniquenesses, 2) vector norms that depend on the coordinate system, and 3) duplicated normal-ogive-to-logistic conversions.

In summary, there is an indentification gap between the direction of an item in the space spanned by the EGA communities (identified by the network loadings) and the magnitude of its common component (which network loadings do not identify).

The current paper solves this gap by using a simple, but consequential, logical observation. Since EGA can be used to estimate more than the partition of the multidimensional space, with the revised network loadings identifying the direction of every item in the multidimensional space, the only quantity that remains unindentified (from a factor-compatible latent-response model perspective) is one positive scalar per item that could generate the magnitude of the items. That scalar can be estimated from the same correlation matrix that EGA uses. Building on this observation, the current paper introduces the Directional Minimum-Distance calibration (DMD), a new estimator that keeps the EGA directions fixed and estimates a metric akin to item communalities by reproducing the observed off-diagonal correlations. Analytically, it leads to multidimensional EGA-based discrimination vectors, intercepts, and scalar difficulties without the need to use a latent variable model (factor or IRT).

The Present Study

The aims of the current paper are threefold. First, it explains the theory behind the DMD calibration method. Then, the current paper presents an EGA-based multidimensional discrimination vectors, intercepts, and scalar directional difficulties.

Third, the current paper demonstrates that the factor carrying the uniqueness cancels from the difficulty, leading to a geometric interpretation as the location at which the response hyperplane of an item cuts the space spanned by the communities, termed in the current paper the community thresholds. In this paper, DMD is evaluated in a Monte-Carlo simulation and compared to a exploratory Multidimensional Item Response Theory model with the correct dimensionality. Two data generation mechanisms are used: the probit data-generating model favors the tetrachoric geometry used by EGA, while the logistic data-generating model favors the link fitted by the MIRT model. The current paper also reports a very interesting consequence of the DMD equation: once the EGA directions are taken into consideration, the correlations betwen items factor into a product of item magnitudes, and as a consequence the relative magnitude of any two items is the same irrespective of which third item is used to compare them.

It is important to note up front that EGA-DMD is not proposed as a replacement for confirmatory MIRT. It is a calibration method for those interested in obtaining item parameters after using EGA as a dimensionality assessment technique. EGA-DMD can be seen as a bridge to classical item parameters under a factor-compatible latent-response model. Under other data-generating mechanisms, such as the network models that motivate EGA in the first place (Epskamp, Maris, et al., 2018), the resulting values are calibrated summaries of the EGA geometry and should not be interpreted as the parameters of latent causes.

Monte Carlo Simulation

Design

The simulation has two data generation meachnisms. A probit latent-response model produces exactly the tetrachoric common factor geometry that EGA uses as its correlation target, while a logistic model produces exactly the link fitted by the MIRT model. If only the probit condition were studied, the design would favor EGA, and if only the logistic condition were studied, it would favor MIRT. Crossing both links therefore provides a direct test of robustness to reciprocal link misspecification. The design also separates calibration from dimensionality assessment. The MIRT model was given the correct number of dimensions, because the central question is the recovery of item parameters, while EGA was evaluated both with the generating item memberships supplied (a known-communities arm, which isolates the loading directions and the magnitude calibration) and in a fully empirical arm, which includes network estimation, dimensionality assessment, community detection, revised network loadings, and DMD. Reporting both arms prevents error in community recovery from being mistaken for error in the calibration formula.

Four factors systematically manipulated in the simulation: sample size (500, 1,000, 2,000, and 5,000), the correlation between the three latent dimensions (.00, .30, and .60), the loading structure of a fixed 30-item bank with ten items per dimension (a simple structure and an empirically derived structure with cross-loadings), and the data-generating link (probit or logistic). Appendix E describes each factor and the data-generating equations in detail. Before sampling error was introduced, a deterministic formula audit evaluated DMD with the exact generating directions and the exact population correlations, and a population EGA-native target was computed for every combination of link, loading structure, and factor correlation by applying EGA and DMD to the population correlation matrix. Sample estimates were evaluated against both the classical item parameters and these targets (Appendix E).

The four factors were fully crossed, yielding 4 × 3 × 2 ×2 = 48 conditions (Table 1).

For each condition, 250 datasets were generated, for a total of 12,000 datasets, and each dataset was analyzed by three estimators, yielding 36,000 fitted or calibrated solutions.

Estimators

Three methods were applied to every dataset.

1. MIRT. A three-dimensional exploratory two-parameter logistic model was estimated with the mirt package (Chalmers, 2012) using quasi-Monte Carlo expectation-maximization with 3,000 quadrature points, a convergence tolerance of 10 -4 , and a maximum of 1,000 cycles. The fitted slope matrix was whitened to an orthogonal latent metric before recovery was evaluated.

2. EGA-DMD, known communities. The sample EGA network and the revised loading directions were computed with the generating memberships, and communalities were estimated by Equation ( 9).

3. EGA-DMD, estimated communities.

The fully empirical chain estimated the network, the dimensionality, the communities, the revised loading directions, and the DMD communalities (the EGA settings are listed in Appendix E).

Evaluation Criteria

Estimated slope and loading matrices were aligned to their targets with an orthogonal Procrustes transformation (Schönemann, 1966), which leaves row norms and angles between items unchanged. Recovery was quantified with the correlation, the root mean square error (RMSE), and the bias of the communalities, the discrimination norms, the intercepts, and the scalar directional difficulties, and the recovery of item directions with the mean row cosine between estimated and generating discrimination vectors.

Because every method analyzed the same datasets, EGA-DMD and MIRT were also compared within replication (Morris, White, & Crowther, 2019), and analyses of variance quantified the effects of the design factors with partial eta squared (Golino et al., 2020).

Appendix E gives the formal definitions and the computational details.

Results

Population Results

The population analyses address two questions. The first question is whether the DMD calibration formula is correct, and this was tested by supplying DMD with the exact generating directions and the exact population correlation, so that if there are any discrepancy it should have been due to the formula or from its numerical optimization. Indeed, under these conditions, DMD returned the population communalities in all six conditions (combinations of loading structure and factor correlation) with the errors at the level of numerical precision (RMSE below 7 × 10 -8 ), confirming Corollary 1 (Table F3 and Figure F2 in Appendix F).

The second question is how much accuracy is lost if we replace the generating directions by the directions that EGA estimated from the population correlation matrix. In other words, the distance between the directions of revised network loadings and those of the factor model in the case of an infinite sample. Nothing is lost if the dimensions are orthogonal with a simple structure. The EGA directions coincide with the generating directions, and DMD perfectly recovers the classical item parameters exactly under the probit link. Under all other scenarios, the loss was negligible. The directions were nearly parallel to each other (mean row cosines of no less than .996, where 1 is for perfectly parallel directions), the discrimination norms were kept in order relative to the classical ones (correlations of no less than .990), and the communalities and difficulties were near their classical counterparts (RMSEs of no more than .030 and .016, respectively), with the biggest difference being seen in the cross-loadings model under the logistic link (Table F1 in Appendix F).

Finite-Sample Recovery of Item Parameters

The finite-sample comparison of fully empirical EGA-DMD with MIRT involved the same 12,000 simulated datasets, but now separately analyzing each component of the items-their directions in the latent space, measured by the row cosine between the estimated and generating discrimination vectors, and their magnitudes and locations, measured by the communalities, the discrimination norms, and the difficulties. Figure F1 and Table F2 in Appendix F show the design results.

Item directions. EGA-DMD recovered the item directions almost as accurately as MIRT did. On average across all conditions, both methods achieved a mean vector correlation of .984, and their mean row cosines were .995 and .996, respectively. Moreover, the recovery of both methods improved at about the same rate with an increasing sample size; thus, the row cosine of EGA-DMD increased from .992 at N = 500 to .998 at N = 5,000, and that of MIRT increased from .991 to .999. The performance comparison was dependent on the loading structure (Figure F1). In the orthogonal simple structure, EGA-DMD recovered the item directions more accurately than MIRT, with mean row cosines ranging from .996 at N = 500 to 1.000 at N = 5,000, and it produced a higher row cosine than MIRT in more than 99% of the paired replications of that condition, as well as in 89% of the paired replications of the simple structure with a factor correlation of .30.

When it came to the cross-loading structure, the row cosine of EGA-DMD was only marginally smaller than that of MIRT (by less than .003), because it was approaching the values of the population EGA directions themselves (ranging from .996 to .998; see Table F1). Thus, this extremely small difference cannot be explained by sampling. Finally, when the performance of the estimate was compared to the population parameter intrinsic to EGA, the row cosine of EGA-DMD increased from .993 for N = 500 to .999 for N = 5,000, and the communality RMSE decreased from .057 to .018, confirming consistency of EGA-DMD to its population estimand, and leaving the remaining difference to be attributable to the small population difference between revised network-loading directions and factor directions under cross-loading.

Communalities, discriminations, and difficulties. EGA-DMD and MIRT were also similar in recovering magnitudes and locations. On average across all conditions, the communality RMSE of EGA-DMD was .040 and .038 of MIRT, the correlation between the estimated and generating discrimination norms was .966 and .970, and the difficulty RMSE was .066 and .063, respectively. Within individual replications, the average difference in the difficulty RMSE between the two methods was no larger than .006 in any combination of the link and sample size (Table F4 in Appendix F). As was predicted by the reciprocal design, the data-generating link was responsible for which of the methods outperformed.

Thus, under the probit link, which agrees with the latent response model used in EGA, EGA-DMD resulted in a lower communality RMSE than MIRT in 53% to 64% of the paired replications, depending on the sample size, and in 70% to 79% of those under the simple structure conditions. Under the logistic link, which matches MIRT, MIRT had an advantage over EGA-DMD, and its advantage increased with sample size, since the approximation error that DMD makes under this link (see the population analysis) does not shrink as the sample size increases. Still, even at the largest sample size, the difference was small, with a paired difference in communality RMSE of .001 at N = 500 and .009 at N = 5,000.

Direction and Magnitude as Separate Sources of Error

The major empirical finding of the paper is that direction and magnitude function as separate sources of error, exactly as suggested by the decomposition. Given the item directions from EGA, the communalities and scalar directional difficulties provided by DMD were recovered almost as accurately as those from the correctly dimensioned full-information MIRT model whose accuracy increases with the sample size at the same rate. The mean difficulty RMSE of the two approaches differed by .004 overall, and by no more than .006 in each combination of link and sample size; in addition, EGA-DMD provided more accurate communalities than MIRT under the probit link in the majority of paired replications. This is an empirical demonstration of Proposition 1. As the factor containing the uniqueness drops out of the formula for the difficulty, the difficulty was robust even in the small sample datasets, where communalities close to one resulted in large discriminations and intercepts of both approaches.

However, the recovery of item directions was dependent on the agreement of the revised network-loadings with factor directions. For orthogonal simple structures, where EGA-DMD outperformed MIRT, the agreement was perfect using the composite coordinate correlations of Equation ( 2). Otherwise, it stayed strong for all cases of EGA-DMD with population row cosines between .996 and .998 in the cross-loading structure. In the effect-size analysis, the direction recovery of EGA-DMD, just like MIRT, was dominated by sample size, while the loading structure and the factor correlation played a major role. EGA-DMD, in comparison with its population target, was consistent with a row cosine of .999 at N = 5,000. This way, the remaining small gap in the cross-loading structure does not reflect an imperfection of the calibration process, but a fundamental gap between the directions themselves. This agrees with the finding of Christensen et al.

(2025) that, when a factor model generates the data, rotated factor loadings remain somewhat more accurate than revised network loadings.

This difference has a direct implication: Since DMD is invariant to the chosen coordinates (Lemma 1) and relies solely on the geometrical properties of the loadings, any increase of the accuracy in the direction recovery of the network loadings will automatically lead to a better performance of DMD without changing the calibration procedure, as seen for the composite coordinate correlations. Conversely, when factor directions are targeted and the items cross-load, the discrimination vectors should be interpreted as calibrated EGA directions.

Limitations and Future Directions

The current study, in addition to being inherently limited due to its sample-based nature, is constrained in several ways in terms of methodology. First, the simulation included only 48 conditions with one 30-item bank with three dimensions, whose simple and cross-loading variants give a useful reciprocal comparison but do not span the entire factorial space of test lengths, number of dimensions, loading sparsity, discrimination differences and item difficulty. Second, the study considered only dichotomous items. The direction magnitude decomposition can be readily applied to ordinal latent response models: indeed, the several thresholds for one item share the same direction and communality, yet a separate derivation and study is necessary to extend DMD to this case.

Third, the exact population bridge holds only when the common factor representation is adequate and the EGA directions recover the directional geometry of the underlying factors; data generated by network models, on the other hand, need not satisfy Equation ( 18), in which case the DMD procedure becomes a minimum-distance summary of the EGA geometry, and the estimated item parameters should not be interpreted as the parameters of latent causes. Fourth, the marginal threshold transformation is exact for the standardized probit latent response model, and approximate for the logistic latent response model. Fifth, MIRT benchmark received the true number of dimensions, whereas the empirical EGA arm had to estimate dimensionality first: in effect, this made the recovery of item parameters easier for MIRT, but allowed DMD to be used in its full procedure.

Runtime depends on implementation details, hardware and convergence criteria, and thus should be considered along with recovery and convergence and not as an inherent feature of the statistical model. Directions of future research are discussed in Appendix I.

Conclusion

In this study, DMD calibration has been formulated, proven and simulated to estimate item parameters from the multidimensional structure found using EGA. DMD possesses several features. First, it maintains the EGA structure solution but uses only one more quantity, the magnitude of the items that the EGA does not provide. Second, DMD is exact at the population level whenever the directions are correct, the factor carrying the uniqueness cancels out of its difficulties, and those difficulties were nearly as accurate as the difficulties of the full-information MIRT model in all conditions examined here. Third, it is fast, reliable and clear, making it possible to analyze how much the magnitude estimation contributed to the item parameters obtained after the dimensionality assessment and direction estimation procedures. The major disadvantage of DMD is also its main feature -the discrimination vectors of DMD are as accurate as directions it takes from the revised network loadings, which are different from the factor directions in case of cross-loadings. Fixed-direction DMD also guarantees magnitude invariance under the exact fit condition. Violation of this condition can be seen as an indicator of incompatibility of the correlation matrix and the estimated geometric structure. In summary, DMD closes the gap between the EGA approach and the IRT theory, providing the natural item parameters calibration for the EGA and indicating the conditions when it corresponds to multidimensional item parameters.

Relation to Measurement Theory

Reference invariance recalls the principle of specific objectivity proposed by Rasch (1977), according to which the comparison of two objects should not depend on the agents used to compare them. In Proposition 2, however, both the objects and the agents are items, and the invariance holds within a fixed directional geometry. It does not imply that comparisons between persons are independent of the items administered, and it does not guarantee that the EGA solution, and therefore the geometry, remains the same across populations or item sets.

The logarithmic form of Equation ( 24) also implies the cancellation conditions familiar from additive conjoint measurement (Krantz, Luce, Suppes, & Tversky, 1971; Luce & Tukey, 1964). For example, if S ab ≥ S cd and S ce ≥ S f b , multiplying the two inequalities and cancelling the positive factor m b m c gives S ae ≥ S f d , provided that these pairs are retained. This is a consequence of the product representation. The item pairs available in a finite test do not form the Cartesian product structure assumed by the conjoint measurement theorems, and the remaining axioms and uniqueness conditions of those theorems are not established here.

A Connection to the TMFG

Proposition 2 also suggests a sparse way of examining magnitude separability, based on the triangulated maximally filtered graph [TMFG; Massara et al. (2017)]. A triangle of items is exactly identified, whereas a clique of four items has six correlations and four magnitudes and therefore supplies two independent tetrad equalities. The TMFG starts from a clique of four items and adds one item at a time to a triangular face of the graph, so that the final graph is a chain of four-item cliques joined by shared triangles. Suppose that every edge of the graph has a nonzero directional similarity and a positive direction-adjusted correlation. If every clique satisfies its two tetrad equalities, each clique has a unique product factorization, and neighboring cliques agree on the magnitudes of their shared triangle, since a positive triangle identifies its three magnitudes uniquely. The local factorizations then join into a single factorization over all the edges of the graph. For J ≥ 4 items, the TMFG has 3J -6 edges and J -3 four-item cliques, which gives 2(J -3) independent restrictions on the logarithms of the direction-adjusted correlations for a fixed geometry, with the additional requirement that all the recovered communalities lie below one.

In this use, the TMFG only selects the pairs of items to be examined, and the direction-adjusted correlations are still computed from the latent-response correlations R ij , not from the partial correlations of the network. Estimating the EGA network itself with the TMFG (Golino et al., 2020) is a different choice, which may also change the directions.

It is also important to note that the separability of the retained edges says nothing about the pairs that the graph omits, and that the number of restrictions is an algebraic count, not the degrees of freedom of a statistical test. Such a test would need to account for the estimation of the correlations, of the directions, and of the graph, which is left for future research.

Population Analyses

Two sets of population analyses were carried out before sampling error was introduced. The first was a deterministic formula audit: using the exact generating directions and the exact probit population correlation matrix, the DMD formula was evaluated for every combination of loading structure and factor correlation. This audit tests Corollary 1 directly. The second computed a population EGA-native target for each of the 12 combinations of link, loading structure, and factor correlation. Once the network is estimated, EGA applies a community detection algorithm for weighted networks (Christensen et al., 2024b), most commonly the Walktrap algorithm (Pons & Latapy, 2006), which uses short random walks to define a distance between nodes and merges nodes hierarchically until the partition that maximizes modularity is found (Newman, 2006). Under a network account, the same communities can instead be interpreted as emergent clusters of mutually related variables (Epskamp, Maris, et al., 2018).

Revised Network Loadings

EGA identifies the partition of the multidimensional space, showing which items compose the communities, but it cannot show how strongly each item is related to its own community and to the others. Network loadings (Christensen & Golino, 2021b) address this question by computing, for every node, how much it contributes to the emergence of the communities, using an standardized version of the strength of its connections to the members of each community. The resulting item-by-community matrix is akin to a factor-loading matrix, and Christensen and Golino (2021b) showed that the two are closely related when data are generated from a factor model. The revised network loadings of Christensen et al. (2025) improved this construction in three ways: they recover the sign of each connection, they adjust the within-community sum for the self-edge that a node cannot have with itself, and they stabilize loading magnitudes across communities of different sizes. It is also important to note that Christensen et al. (2025) showed that the rotated exploratory factor loadings remained, on average, somewhat more accurate than revised network loadings when data were generated from a factor model, a result that becomes relevant when the recovery of item directions is discussed below.

Why should a researcher use EGA-DMD rather than fit MIRT directly? Three reasons emerge from the present results. With the composite coordinate correlations, the residuals were of similar size within and between communities, and no retained pair showed a sign discrepancy. The CRT example made the boundary behavior concrete: the triangle formula flagged CRT07, the item with the highest DMD communality, as locally inadmissible without optimization, and the confirmatory model also identified Heywood cases. The discount examples showed how different reference items can be used to examine magnitude separability directly. Together, the examples demonstrate both calibration and model diagnostics within the EGA solution.

Effects of the Data Generating Mechanism

The probit latent-response model is exact for the tetrachoric geometry used by EGA, while the logistic model is exact for the MIRT model. The results showed that the link had negligible effects on difficulty recovery for both methods and on the communality recovery of MIRT (η² p ≤ .02), and a medium effect on the communality recovery of EGA-DMD (η² p = .08). Under the logistic link, DMD carried a small population approximation error, visible even with the generating directions, and MIRT gained a small advantage in communality recovery that grew with sample size. Under the probit link, the advantage reversed. In neither case did the link change the substantive conclusions. The population EGA-native target was essential for this interpretation. Evaluating sample EGA-DMD only against the parameters of the generating model would have confounded a difference between estimands with an inconsistency of the estimator, whereas reporting both targets showed that the population estimand and sampling variation provide distinct explanations for the observed finite-sample discrepancies.

A Curious Consequence: Magnitude Separability

Magnitude separability is an exact consequence of the fixed-direction representation (Proposition 2). Once the EGA directions are divided out, the correlations between items factor into a product of item magnitudes, so that the relative magnitude of any two items is the same whichever third item is used to compare them, and the direction-adjusted tetrads vanish. This result generalizes the vanishing tetrads of the one-factor model (Bollen & Ting, 1993; Spearman, 1904) to the multidimensional geometry estimated by EGA, and it resembles the specific objectivity of Rasch (1977), although it concerns item magnitudes within a fixed geometry rather than comparisons between persons, and it does not establish the axioms of additive conjoint measurement. Its practical value is that it can be examined without fitting the magnitudes. In the running example (Appendix D), the reference items that disagreed pointed to the same source of misfit that the residuals revealed in the empirical illustration, namely the slightly stronger associations between communities implied by the EGA directions. The DMD communalities also quantify one of the two requirements for an additive person-item structure on the inverse-link scale, the other being a common item direction.

DMD in Practice

Which results should applied researchers inspect when using DMD with empirical data? Four recommendations follow from the present study. First, the stability of the EGA solution should be examined before calibration, for example with bootstrap EGA (Christensen & Golino, 2021a), because DMD inherits the communities and the directions of EGA. In the present simulation, community recovery was nearly perfect, but empirical data with weaker structures may be less forgiving. Second, the DMD residual matrix, E = R -ˆ︂ R, and the frequency of boundary communalities should be reported. A communality at or near the upper bound indicates that the fixed directional geometry requires nearly all of the latent-response variance of an item to be common in order to reproduce its observed correlations, which is a warning to examine sampling uncertainty and compatibility of the fixed-direction representation, rather than a value to be silently repaired. Structured residuals can reveal local dependence (Christensen et al., 2023), omitted dimensions, or directional mismatch. Third, the discrimination norms and intercepts of items with very high communalities should be interpreted with caution in small samples, whereas Proposition 1 removes the singular uniqueness factor from scalar directional difficulty. This finite limiting behavior does not establish empirical precision or model fit. Fourth, the comparisons of item magnitudes implied by different reference items can be displayed across eligible reference items, as in Figure D1 of Appendix D, as a simple visual check of whether the observed correlations and the EGA directions agree with the separability of Proposition 2.

Future Directions

Several directions for future research follow from the limitations discussed in the main text. The most immediate is the extension of DMD to ordinal items with multiple thresholds per item. A second is the development of formal residual-based fit statistics and item-level misfit diagnostics for the DMD solution, possibly building on the entropy fit indices developed for EGA (Golino et al., 2021). A third is uncertainty quantification, which can be approached through bootstrap EGA, repeated network estimation, or a sandwich approximation conditional on the estimated directions. A fourth is the development of an inferential test based on the directional tetrads of Proposition 2, possibly organized around the four-item cliques of a TMFG, whose sampling distribution must propagate the uncertainty of both the correlations and the estimated EGA geometry, together with the evaluation of the triangle formula of Equation ( 27) as a closed-form initializer for the optimizer. A fifth concerns the directions themselves: because the results identified the coordinate correlation matrix and the remaining direction discrepancy under cross-loadings as the limiting factors for discrimination vectors, further improving the directional accuracy of network loadings in that setting would directly improve DMD.

Finally, the method should be evaluated with empirical instruments for which independent MIRT calibrations and substantive validity evidence are available.

What DMD Preserves and What It Adds

Taken together, the results of the section on DMD calibration in the main text show that DMD preserves three outputs of EGA, namely the detected dimensionality, the organization of items into communities, and the relative multidimensional direction of every item, and adds exactly one scalar magnitude per item. It does not alter the network, reassign items to communities, or rotate directions to maximize agreement with any external model. This separation has an important consequence for the evaluation of the method. Any disagreement between DMD and classical item parameters can be decomposed into three sources: (a) a population difference between EGA directions and factor directions, (b) finite-sample error in the estimation of EGA directions, and (c) error in the calibration of magnitudes. The simulation reported in the main text was designed so that each of these sources can be observed separately.

Relationship to Factor Analysis and MIRT

DMD is closely related to limited-information factor analysis: it minimizes residuals in an observed correlation matrix while holding the loading directions fixed, and with unit weights it is a constrained minimum residuals estimator (Harman & Jones, 1966). The novelty lies in where the fixed directions come from and in their inferential decomposition.

EGA provides the dimensionality, the communities, and the directions, while DMD estimates only the unidentified magnitudes with the item response transformation following analytically.
