Power Analysis Check

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine, Daniel Lakens and Cristian Mesquida

Overall, among all instances flagged as power statements, 90.6% were correct (positive predictive value).

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Of 1,783 non-significant p-values in the ground truth, version 0.4.4.3 found 1,719 (3.6% missed), and 94.8% of its 1,814 detections were in the ground truth. How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

Abstract

We propose rhythm cognition, the perception of and engagement with patterns of stimuli organized in time, as a prism for investigating predictive coding in autism. Predictive coding theories of autism are common, driven by the prevalence of predictive coding in contemporary cognitive models and their efficacy relating autism to other neuropsychiatric conditions. However, different theories manifest in similar behavior, making them difficult to compare, and rely on an over-encompassing, domain-general conceptualization of predictive coding itself. Here, we conduct a narrative review demonstrating how computational models of rhythm processing enable precise, highly individualized characterizations of predictive coding theories of autism. To do this, we synergize behavioral and neuroscientific scholarship on rhythm processing in autistic and nonautistic individuals with research probing developmental trajectories of beat and meter induction. We then discuss several canonical models of rhythm processing, detailing the strengths and shortcomings of mapping each model onto specific manifestations of predictive coding in autism. We conclude that rhythm processing can reveal individualized differences in predictive coding mechanisms in autism, moving beyond a categorical, subtypefocused view of cognitive mechanisms in autism.

Introduction

Autism is a common (1 in 31 [1]) neurodevelopmental condition characterized by differences or impairments in social function and restricted interests or repetitive behaviors [2]. Autistic individuals are quite heterogeneous in presentation, leading some scholars to advocate for considering a plurality of autisms, rather than one singular condition [3] [4] [5]. Since any presentation can be driven by several underlying cognitive mechanisms, a plethora of mechanistic theories of autism have emerged over the last several decades. While such theories share common characteristics, they often operate from entirely different perspectives. Some theories are based on a cognitive perspective, whether characterizing differences in cognitive processes like mentalizing [6, 7], or comparing reliance on cognitive styles such as systemizing over empathizing [8, 9] or deliberative over intuitive [10]. Other theories focus on sensory perception, asserting that autism is related to changes in the inputs to cognition rather than cognitive processing itself [11]. An additional approach details precise neuroanatomical shifts such as excitatory-inhibitory imbalance [12]. Different theories prioritize wholly different facets of autism, from fluidity in social contexts [13] to sensory stimulation [14] and information integration [15]. Such variety makes them resistant to direct comparison, and attempts at finding neuroanatomical biomarkers of autism, or even subtypes of autism, have produced mixed results [16]. Several approaches frame autism within the prevailing theoretical frameworks of their time [17], and while recent studies differentiating between local and global processing display promise probing some of these theories, most notably the weak central coherence and enhanced perceptual functioning accounts [18, 19], their findings are often contradictory [20].

In recent years, frameworks increasingly characterize autism in terms of predictive coding (see Table 1), the theory that human cognition is a process of applying mental models to make predictions, assessing those predictions against observations, and using the error or gap between the prediction and observation to update the models themselves. It is predictive in that it involves making and assessing predictions, and incorporates both encoding, or translating perception into the brain, and decoding, or making meaning of brain representations, within one iterative framework. This process is analogous to minimizing the "free energy," or unexplained error, in an observed world [21]. Each loop through this cycle can be modeled using Bayesian inference (Figure 1).

Rhythm as a Prism

Rhythm is uniquely suited to disambiguating predictive coding theories of autism, because different predictive coding theories manifest differently in rhythm processing tasks, and because rhythm is a core component of several autistic traits. In short, rhythmic processing acts as a prism, splitting the "white light" of behavior, which is the end product of a wide range of possible cognitive and mechanistic components, into its constituent parts. Rhythm tasks are particularly useful because they are highly applicable beyond the experimental task itself; informative at every developmental stage from early infancy through late adulthood; and are well-framed in testable, computational terms.

Passive behavioral tasks are particularly promising in probing temporal prediction in autism: while visual attention to a singer's eyes synchronizes to the beat of the song they are singing as early as infancy [56], autistic toddlers' visual attention adapts to temporally unpredictable stimuli consistent with a slowly-updating rhythmic prior [34]. While neural approaches to studying several constructs, most notably synchronization, oddball or stimulus detection, and adaptation, are well-established, they are not applied in autism as frequently as with nonautistic populations.

However, since the neurological evidence dovetails with behavioral correlates in nonautistic participants, they could provide similarly informative results when investigating autism-related changes in patterns of neural activity. As a result, studying the neural correlates of temporal prediction and rhythm processing in autism is an extremely powerful, yet underutilized, tool for probing nuanced variation in predictive coding in autism.

Different rhythm tasks also evince different patterns of performance in autism, which can be mapped onto predictive coding processes. For instance, Vishne et al. used changes in the tempo of an ongoing stimulus to demonstrate that autistic individuals did not update their inferred tempo as quickly or accurately as nonautistic peers [32], while Cannon et al. found that autistic participants adjusted to temporal offsets similarly to nonautistic peers, although they did show evidence of a less precise internal timing mechanism in autism [40]. These tasks are similar, since they both contend with error correction, but while Vishne et al. investigated changes in tempo (i.e. how fast stimuli are happening), Cannon et al. investigated changes in phase (i.e. location in a rhythmic cycle). While these results may at first appear contradictory, they may be collectively explained by a nuanced view of predictive coding that allows for heterogeneity across individuals and separability of different components or parameters of predictive coding itself. This task-specificity means that rhythm tasks can reveal predictive processing in autism with unprecedented detail and precision.

Notably, and in contrast to older research, these recent studies leverage rhythmic perturbations, or moments where a predictable rhythm is defied. Such perturbations may be necessary for revealing nuanced differences in rhythm processing mechanisms, since each model behaves similarly when the stimulus is highly predictable. Put simply, if the task is too easy, every model or participant looks the same. Harnessing rhythmic complexity or unpredictability increases rhythm's usefulness as a prism into underlying mechanisms in autism.

Empirical Applicability to Autism

Rhythm processing's utility for probing predictive coding in autism rests on two key properties. First, while no single mechanism addresses all aspects of cognition, and no single measurement modality can capture variation in every characteristic of autism, rhythm is central to a remarkably broad range of autistic traits, ranging from fluid social interaction and communication to motor impairments. This considerable overlap between rhythm processing and autism's heterogeneous presentation greatly enhances its direct applicability.

Second, rhythm processing is not constant over an individual's life. Instead, it changes in response to maturing sensory processing, enculturation and statistical learning, and overall patterns of neurodevelopment. It is also relevant at every age; beat induction, for example, has been observed even in perinatal populations, and rhythm scaffolds (visual) attention patterns in infants as young as 2 months, limited mostly by the postnatal development of the visual system [78]. Because of this, rhythm is well positioned to investigate neuroplasticity early in childhood.

Rhythm in Autism

Using rhythm tasks to disambiguate predictive coding mechanisms has linked performance patterns to social communication skills in autism [32] and may distinguish clinically relevant autism subtypes [40]. In our model (Figure 2), the light source represents a specific trait or aspect of autism, so changing the light source represents rhythm's ability to probe multiple dimensions of autism, since a prism can split any light into its components. Social interactions are highly rhythmic [79]. Rhythm is such a prominent feature of social interaction that rhythmic entrainment for social bonding and effective communication has been posited as an evolutionary role for music and musicality [80]. Synchrony and rhythmic entrainment may help explain the consistent link between interpersonal synchrony and pro-social behaviors and empathy [81, 82]. Children who play rhythm games together are more cooperative [83] and display greater empathy and closeness towards peers [84, 85]. Rhythmic synchrony also supports social attention to others, both in therapeutic contexts [86] and social settings more generally [87]. Interpersonal rhythmic synchronization directs helpfulness even as early as infancy [88, 89]. Temporal predictability is crucial for supporting how conversational partners navigate and parse a complex sensory environment [90]. Autistic individuals have difficulties across a variety of social rhythm contexts [91], including reduced entraining of their overall speech rate with conversational partners [92, 93], reduced movement synchrony with partners in social interactions [94], and impaired conversational turn-taking [92, 95].

In language, rhythmicity manifests in multiple time-scales and modalities [96], including rhythmic patterns within utterances [97, 98] and turn-taking across conversational partners [99]. Language delays and differences are common in autism [2], and communication skills interact in several ways with the development of social skills [100]. Speech and language skills throughout development are strongly linked to rhythm abilities, as evidenced by substantial overlaps in neurophysiology [101] [102] [103] [104], genetic influences [105, 106] and widespread behavioral correlations [107]. Rhythm perception relies on similar neural mechanisms across speech and music (e.g. sensorimotor coupling; neural entrainment), further positioning musical rhythm as a useful complement for the study of language [103]. In autism, links between active rhythmic engagement to music and language development are mediated through both general and musical social interactions [108], suggesting impaired human engagement with rhythm may be an underlying causal factor in the prevalence of language delays in autism. This link is bolstered by substantial overlap between the neuroanatomical foundations of rhythm processing and autism. Rhythm processing relies heavily on motor and subcortical neural substrates, particularly the thalamus, basal ganglia (especially dorsal striatum), and supplementary motor area [109], along with the dorsal auditory pathway, connecting auditory and motor cortices [110]. Motor neuroanatomy is so closely tied to rhythm processing that auditory temporal orienting may be driven by simulated motion [111]. This neuroanatomical network is also heavily implicated in autism. Both the relative size of basal ganglia components and their excitatory/inhibitory balance may drive the emergence of autistic traits, especially repetitive behaviors [112], and thalamocortical connectivity is altered in autism [113, 114]. In addition, functional connectivity between motor and visual brain regions is disrupted in autism [115].

Activity in this shared cortico-striato-thalamic rhythm network can be characterized via dynamic attending theory [116, 117], which argues that people entrain their attention to temporal patterns in an external stimulus. This manifests in the brain via neural oscillators, or rhythmic changes in the excitability of neuronal populations [118]. Since the neural discharge rate in the putamen and supplementary motor area entrains to rhythmic stimuli [119], such oscillations are likely grounded in the basic neurobiology of specific brain regions also implicated in autism.

Much of this biological overlap implicates motor function as a key component of rhythm processing, and although not a clinical criterion for autism, motor impairments are common in the autistic population [120], and may be linked to social skills challenges [121]. Autistic individuals' rhythmic movements are slower and less accurate, particularly in social contexts [49, 122]. In children, these motor challenges closely resemble the diagnostic criteria for developmental coordination disorder (DCD), which is comorbid with autism [123], and span all areas of motor function [124]. An extensive body of literature links motor function and rhythm across the lifespan [125] [126] [127]. Rhythm holds potential for mitigating motor impairments in a variety of clinical settings [128], including autism [129, 130] and DCD [131, 132], along with neurodegenerative conditions such as Parkinson's [133, 134], and multiple sclerosis [135], as well as recovery from stroke and traumatic brain injury [136, 137]. Although these conditions follow distinct neurophysiological pathways, rhythm's ability to support motor coordination across such a wide range of conditions provides further evidence for its central role in motor function more broadly. Taken together, investigating rhythm can provide insights into motor function in autism, which may in turn be linked to core clinical aspects of the condition, and can reveal mechanistic explanations for motor function differences in autism, even in non-social domains [32].

Lastly, but most crucially, rhythm skills are heterogenous within the autistic population, and that heterogeneity is linked to distinctions among clinically-relevant subtypes of autism [31]. This implies both that understanding individual differences in rhythm skills is directly relevant for clinical purposes, and that the cognitive mechanisms underpinning rhythm may interact strongly with those underpinning autism and co-occurring conditions.

In summary, rhythm is linked, directly and indirectly, to social interaction, language skills, motor function, and clinical heterogeneity in autism. Rhythm impairments have also been linked to phenotypic symptoms in communication, attention, and motor domains in developmental speech and language disorders [138, 139] and neurodevelopmental disorders more broadly (e.g., ADHD, DCD) [48]. In addition, while many people with no clinical diagnoses also display rhythm impairments, indicating that rhythm is one causal factor among many, it is mechanistically and neuroanatomically central to variation in many core aspects of autism. Therefore, results pertaining to rhythm processing and temporal prediction are highly informative for predictive coding in multiple domains of autism and autism research.

Developmental Trajectories

Entrainment to an external beat appears to be an innate property of human cognition [140], while identification and synchronization to complex rhythms develops throughout childhood [141]. These procedures share a common foundation in beat induction, but incorporate learned components in different ways. This means that longitudinal studies probing the developmental trajectory of rhythm processing can reveal underlying learning processes that are highly unlikely to be rhythm-specific, but which are central to cognitive theories of autism.

While beat perception has been observed in newborns, it becomes more precise over the early years of life [142].

Neural markers of beat perception, such as changes in the strength of neural oscillations around 20 Hz (i.e. beta-band oscillatory power), enhancement of oscillations at the beat frequency, and surprisal responses to missing beats, have all been observed in children (Cirelli et al., 2014; 2016), including as young as 2-3 days postnatal [143] and infants born preterm [144], demonstrating that these processes are already developed during the third trimester [145]. For infants, temporal regularity is necessary for detecting statistically anomalous events [146], implying a strong relationship between temporal predictability and attention early in life. For instance, children's visual attention entrains to a regular rhythmic pulse in infancy, with this pattern strengthening across the first year of life as their sensory and motor processing capacities mature [56] and appearing in autism at an attenuated level [147].

Children also demonstrate a rapidly maturing capacity to infer abstract patterns from rhythms and use those patterns to scaffold attention and behavior. In particular, they learn to recognize and rely on hierarchical rhythmic structures, or meters, where some beats are stronger than others. Infants can distinguish between metrical groupings as early as 7 months [148], and show patterns of neural responses commensurate with inferred meters [149]. However, meter perception is highly enculturated, with infants' musical exposure shaping their preferences for different metrical structures [150]. Meter perception is, therefore, the outcome of a complex learning and classification process that applies top-down predictive processing to help navigate a temporally complex sensory environment. Unlike beat induction, which matures with sensory and motor functionality, meter induction evolves with exposure, enculturation, learning, and the development of classification abilities.

Taken together, these findings imply two distinct contributors to rhythm processing: bottom-up beat entrainment, which develops as sensory and motor skills mature; and top-down metric inference, which relies on learning and classification mechanisms [90]. Since these two components follow distinct developmental trajectories, but each rely on domain-general aspects of cognition, studying how each property changes over time within an individual can reveal underlying processes that impact brain development more broadly. In addition, both the sensorimotor processing crucial for beat induction [151] and the hierarchical inference-making central to meter induction [152] are altered in autism, making trajectories of rhythm processing highly informative for studying autism's underlying cognitive mechanisms.

Computational Models of Rhythm

Small gradations in a model of temporal prediction can lead to quite different behavioral and neural responses. In our model schematic (Figure 2), this is indicated by the attributes and orientation of the metronome, representing rhythm's role as a prism. Changing the angle of the prism even slightly can cause dramatic changes in how the incoming light is split, and changing the settings on the metronome is like changing the physical characteristics of the prism itself.

Such manipulations can be characterized in precise mathematical terms, as can the predictions they engender, so given a sufficiently detailed and cognitively plausible computational model of temporal prediction, experimentally manipulating rhythm can probe underlying cognitive mechanisms of prediction with great specificity.

There are far too many models of rhythm processing to discuss in detail here, so we will focus on three influential models that demonstrate different canonical approaches to temporal prediction: the linear error-correction mechanism proposed by Wing and Kristofferson [153], for simplicity and historical context; Cannon's model of continuous Bayesian inference [154, 155]; and Large's neural resonance theory [156]. For a thorough discussion of current developments in computational rhythm modeling, see [157].

Linear Error-Correction

The earliest example of a rhythmic theory dynamically incorporating prediction error is Wing and Kristofferson's linear error-correction model [153]. Their framework represents someone tapping a constant beat as an internal timekeeper that triggers a motor response at regular intervals. It has two potential sources of noise: variance in the intervals measured out by the timekeeper, and variance in the motor delay between the timekeeper triggering a pulse and the person producing a tap. Given a sequence of taps, this model decomposes variance in the inter-tap interval into estimates of timekeeper noise and motor noise, which in turn determine the linear coefficient by which tap onset error is corrected.

In a predictive coding context, this linear error-correction model substitutes for an internal model of the rhythmgenerating process, or a temporal theory of mind. Under this analogy, variance of the temporal timekeeper corresponds to stability of the rhythmic prior, while variance of the response delay corresponds to sensory noise. Since this approach explicitly couches rhythm perception as an internal cognitive model of an external process, it capitalizes on established links between predictive coding and mentalizing, which is known to be altered in autism.

While this mapping is very clean, making this model very interpretable, it has several key limitations. First, it assumes that the error correction is linear, so an error of a given size will always result in the exact same correction.

Second, it assumes that the error correction is constant, rather than learned and adapted over time. Third, it is only specified for isochronous beats, and does not generalize easily to more complex rhythmic structures. Last, although it is simple enough to be interpretable and easily solved, it is underspecified compared to more recent models of timing.

While it is a useful starting point for this kind of analysis, since it demonstrates how models of rhythm perception can map onto broader theories of cognition, newer models will provide more opportunities to study rhythm processing. These models largely fall into two categories: top-down probabilistic models, usually employing Bayesian mathematics; and bottom-up neurodynamic models.

Continuous Bayesian Inference

Cannon's phase inference through point process estimation (PIPPET) model [154] and its extension to simultaneous inference of cyclic phase and tempo [155] represents a more nuanced approach to temporal prediction. Unlike the linear error-correction model, this class of model relies on continuous Bayesian inference. In its most complete manifestation, this approach models rhythms as a system of differential equations where the probability of an onset is a function of phase (metric template), phase changes at a rate determined by tempo (the derivative of phase is a function of tempo), and both phase and tempo are sampled from some prior distribution. At each time step, the presence or absence of an onset is observed and compared against the expectation, and that error updates the probability distributions over phase and tempo. This model is governed by several key parameters, which may admit a few kinds of cognitive interpretations. Uncertainty over both tempo and phase reflect the strength of a prior distribution, in conjunction with the ratio governing whether error is ascribed primarily to phase misalignment or incorrect tempo specification. The specificity of the metric template also is a form of prior, as is the tempo correction coefficient governing how strongly a listener returns to their preferred tempo in the absence of reinforcement. Together, this presents a much more realistic rhythmic prior than Wing and Kristofferson's timekeeper. Sensory noise, like in the linear error-correction model, manifests as a single parameter.

Such a parametrization within a Bayesian context enables precise mappings between model parameters and theorized cognitive or perceptual parameters. In theory, the complexity of the rhythmic prior enables an extraordinary degree of nuance for a cognitive model of rhythm processing. However, it is constrained in two key ways. The detailed specification of the model means it can be difficult, in practice, to distinguish between behaviors driven by certain parameters. In addition, as a top-down model, it is difficult to connect specific parameters to underlying neurological correlates. These limitations are not necessarily fatal for use of the PIPPET family of models in fitting predictive coding parameters to rhythmic prediction. The difficulty specifying which parameters drive observed changes in behavior or neural activity may be resolved by simplifying the parameter space itself. For instance, uncertainty over tempo, uncertainty over phase, and the ratio governing how learning is distributed across tempo and phase could all be combined into two parameters, one specifying overall uncertainty and the other distributing that uncertainty between phase and tempo. Using places where changes in parameters result in strongly connected shifts in predicted behavior as a basis to simplify the PIPPET model could improve its empirical applicability.

The absence of neural correlates, meanwhile, is counteracted by the nature of PIPPET's outputs. At each time step, the model generates expectations for tempo, phase, and the likelihood of an onset, along with a measure of the surprisal generated by comparing the last of these expectations against an observation. Each of these output measures does map onto an observable feature of standard neuroelectric imaging such as magneto/encephalography (M/EEG): expected tempo relates to frequency tagging, beta-band oscillatory power maps onto expected phase, and surprisal is indexed by the mismatch field event-related potential. As a result, the lack of direct identifiability between model parameters and neural substrates is secondary to model-fitting, a property typical of probabilistic cognitive models in general.

Predictive coding theories of autism generally involve shifts in one of two sets of parameters: the specificity of a perceptual prior, or the precision of a perceived error. The PIPPET family of models includes priors over phase, tempo, and pattern of onsets, so changing the uncertainty over any of these alters the specificity of the prior. These changes can be global, implying uniformly weaker or stronger priors, or parameter-specific, although this may be constrained by limited identifiability. Error precision is not implemented as directly in PIPPET, but can be manipulated by changing the background probability of an onset not driven by the rhythmic pattern, since a higher such probability leads to integrating errors more hesitantly. Changing the uncertainty over observation timing (i.e. sensory noise) would also impact how readily errors were integrated into the model. The key difference between these two mechanisms of changing error precision is the source of uncertainty: the probability of a non-rhythmic event represents error from outside the perceiver, while sensory noise is error from inside.

Neural Resonance

Neural resonance theory is perhaps the most neurologically grounded rhythmic synchronization theory. It builds on coupled populations of excitatory and inhibitory neurons with simple differential equations governing how their activity dynamically interacts. First described by Wilson and Cowan [158], these coupled neural populations behave like driven damped oscillators given a regular sensory input. These excitatory and inhibitory neural populations map very cleanly onto the "canonical microcircuit" view of predictive coding [159]. Sensory input arrives at the internal granular layer, where prediction errors are encoded, and then propagated in two streams: an excitatory stream making inferences about the hidden schema driving the observations, and an inhibitory stream making inferences about the (also hidden) state of the rhythmic process. This resembles a neural oscillator, albeit much more complex than a classical Wilson-Cowan manifestation.

The neural resonance approach can, with some modifications, also integrate with a more recent neuroanatomical theory called predictive routing [160]. Unlike the proposed neural predictive coding microcircuit, which proposes that predictions and associated errors are encoded by neuron-level spiking activity, predictive routing characterizes prediction and error integration via oscillatory activity in neural populations Low-frequency oscillations propagate from higher cortical areas (i.e. prefrontal cortex) to sensory regions, while high-frequency oscillations go the opposite direction. Since low-frequency oscillations inhibit high-frequency activity at certain phases (e.g. delta-beta phase locking), while high-frequency activity is enhanced by sensory input, predictive routing takes on the same classic inhibitory-excitatory oscillator structure as well. This structure could explain canonical patterns like shifts in beta and gamma oscillatory power associated with the beat frequency [60], which the classic predictive coding version does not.

Although this model is based on excitatory and inhibitory neural activity, it incorporates several simplifications that are not neurobiologically justified. It assumes all connections are instantaneous, rather than incorporating time delays associated with signal transmission through axons, myelinated or otherwise. In addition, it does not integrate any structural hypothesis of where rhythm sits in the brain, instead relying on generic neural populations. On a less neuroanatomical level, these oscillator models assume that the broader neural context surrounding the excitatory and inhibitory populations is static; essentially, this assumption means that nothing outside rhythm processing affects rhythm processing. Unlike PIPPET, the internal parameters of a neural resonance model can, in theory, be fit to observable neurological data. In addition, although a simple Wilson-Cowan oscillator alone cannot account for complex rhythm structures or microtiming variation, more complex collections of oscillators are sensitive to such phenomena [161].

Under the canonical view of predictive coding, where predictions propagate as inhibitory activity from higher to lower cortical layers or brain regions and errors return as excitatory activity the other direction, error precision is easily parametrized as the connection from excitatory to inhibitory populations within the oscillator. The strength of the sensory prior would, in a naive sense, be the complementary inhibitory connection. This implies that all predictive coding theories of autism may be simply instantiated by increasing or decreasing one of those parameters. Furthermore, the Wilson-Cowan excitatory-inhibitory loop is only one instantiation of a driven or forced oscillator. In general terms, such oscillators are defined in terms of a natural frequency and damping coefficient, each of which may include (relatively small) variation in time; introducing both random variation to these parameters can approximate the uncertainty typical in a predictive coding framework. Taking such a tactic allows neural resonance models to represent predictive coding theories of autism in very nuanced ways. Neural resonance's focus on oscillatory dynamics situates neural resonance squarely in a dynamical systems framework, where making predictions is largely unnecessary [162].

However, since an oscillatory model can be modified to represent predictive coding theories of autism, it is ideally suited for bridging predictive coding and neurobiological modelling architectures.

Conclusions

We have proposed rhythm processing as a prism through which we can probe predictive coding mechanisms of autism. This approach is in line with recent proposals for a temporal science of behavior [163] and arguments that temporal prediction is a useful bridge between music and language perception in autism [164], making it both relevant and timely for current directions in the field. Furthermore, temporal prediction capitalizes on existing techniques to test theories of autism, rather than requiring the invention of novel measurement approaches. This immediacy makes temporal prediction particularly suited to filling the urgent need for deeper understanding of how autism works in practice, rather than just in theory, while also providing a solid foundation for further methodological development and innovation. It also allows for increased sensitivity to individual differences and task characteristics, which in turn allows researchers to parse which theories are applicable for which people, and in which contexts. Given rhythm's applicability to a wide range of autism symptoms, such specificity is of particular use in translational scenarios, where empirical results are used for clinical applications.
