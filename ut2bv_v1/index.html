Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

Metacheck does not yet analyze automatically whether sentences which include non-significant p-values are correct, but we recommend manually checking the sentences below for possible misinterpreted non-significant p values.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard), also Internal Review Board - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term); not IEC followed by a number, which is a technical standard - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee/Commission, in the singular or plural - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee, Bioethics Committee/Commission, Ethikkommission - Ethical Review Authority/Unit/Office/Agency - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Human Research Protection Office/Program, Office for Human Subjects Protection - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee(s) on/for (Medical and Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt - Ethics review that was not needed: Ethics review was not required, did not require further ethical review, exempt from formal human research ethics review, no new institutional ethics review

A template line the authors have not filled in (“[Add ethics approval … here.]”) is not a statement.

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

When a statement is expected

The module sorts each paper by where its data came from (the internal .data_source(); the signs and how the rule was tested are described in R/data-source.R): - own: the authors collected data from people or animals, for this paper, in an earlier study of their own, or (a protocol) will collect them; - existing_records: records about individual people that the authors obtained for this study, such as hospital records or a cohort; - existing_unclear: existing data about people from a source the module could not identify; - open_resource: an open resource any researcher can use, such as UK Biobank or NHANES; - none: no data about people or animals (a review, simulation, commentary, theory, tutorial, coding of published papers, or figures about groups); - uncertain: the signs were missing or pointed both ways; - no_text: the paper has no text.

A statement is expected for own, existing_records and existing_unclear. A paper without a statement in these classes, or in uncertain, is flagged. How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. Abstract

Background: Individuals at high risk for suicide are phenomenologically heterogeneous, yet most evidence-based interventions deliver the same content to everyone. Dialectical behavior therapy (DBT) is effective but lengthy, in part because every patient receives the full skills curriculum regardless of which skills address their own drivers of risk. PeRsonalizEd Clinical Intervention for Suicidal Events (PRECISE) uses idiographic models derived from each participant's ecological momentary assessment (EMA) data to identify and teach only the DBT skills most relevant to that individual, allowing treatment to be delivered in six weeks.

Objective: This study aimed to evaluate the feasibility, acceptability, and preliminary impact of PRECISE on suicidal thoughts and behaviors (STBs), depressive and anxiety symptoms, and related secondary outcomes. Methods: Thirty young adults (ages 18-26) with active STBs, recruited from a university and the surrounding community, enrolled in a six-week course of PRECISE alongside five times per day EMA in a single-arm open trial. STBs were assessed at baseline, 6 weeks, and 12 weeks using the Columbia-Suicide Severity Rating Scale (CSSRS) and the Modified Scale for Suicidal Ideation (MSSI); depressive and anxiety symptoms (PROMIS), coping self-efficacy (CSES), and emotion regulation strategy use (CERQ) were also assessed. Bayesian multilevel models with participantlevel random intercepts estimated change over time.

Results: Retention was 93% (28/30) at 6 weeks and 87% (26/30) at 12 weeks. Participants attended a mean of 5.4 of 6 coaching sessions and rated the intervention as highly acceptable (AIM: M = 17.2), appropriate (IAM: M = 16.9), and feasible (FIM: M = 17.2; each out of 20). From baseline to 12 weeks, suicidal ideation declined on both measures (CSSRS: -2.28 points, 95% CrI [-2.75, -1.81]; MSSI: -5.38 points, 95% CrI [-8.21, -2.53]; probability of direction [pd] ≥ 99.99%).

Depressive symptoms (-6.51 T-score points, 95% CrI [-8.60, -4.38]) and anxiety symptoms (-3.60, 95% CrI [-5.70, -1.50]) also declined, and coping self-efficacy increased (+29.30 points, 95% CrI [18.21, 40.50]; all pd ≥ 99.95%). Maladaptive emotion regulation strategy use showed a smaller, less certain decline (-3.20 points, 95% CrI [-6.42, 0.03]; pd = 97.36%), and adaptive strategy use showed no credible change (0.97 points, 95% CrI [-3.75, 5.63]). No serious adverse events or suicide attempts occurred; 11 mild episodes of non-suicidal self-injury were reported by 5 participants.

In this open trial, a brief intervention that personalizes DBT skills using participants' own idiographic EMA data was feasible, acceptable, and associated with clinically meaningful and sustained reductions in STBs, psychiatric symptoms, and maladaptive coping skills as well as improvements in coping self-efficacy. Pending further confirmatory testing, PRECISE may be a useful suicide prevention approach.

1. Introduction

Suicide remains a leading cause of death in the United States; an estimated 48,824 people died by suicide in 2024 alone 1 . At the same time, approximately 11% of U.S. young adults aged 18-25 have seriously contemplated suicide in the past year 2 . Although the causes of suicide are complex and multiply determined, most theoretical models posit that self-injurious thoughts and behaviors (SITBs) are negatively reinforced by reductions in distressing emotions; that is, negative affect tends to rise before a suicidal thought or behavior and fall afterward [3] [4] [5] [6] . This affect-regulation pattern has been documented across intensive longitudinal studies conducted around the world 3.

Dialectical behavior therapy (DBT) directly targets this pathway and has demonstrated efficacy for reducing self-injurious behavior 7, 8 , with skills training in particular emerging as an active ingredient 9 . However, standard DBT is time-intensive, complex, and difficult for many help-seeking individuals to access, motivating efforts to develop briefer, more efficient alternatives that retain DBT's active ingredients [10] [11] [12].

Although clinicians routinely tailor treatment informally, existing suicide-specific interventions provide no systematic, data-driven method for deciding which skills a given individual needs; as a result, protocols such as DBT teach the full skills curriculum to everyone. This approach implicitly assumes shared pathways to STBs and their resolution 13, 14 . However, research with ecological momentary assessment (EMA) that captures STBs as they occur in daily life suggests the precursors to STBs are quite heterogeneous 15, 16 . Idiographic research investigating personalized risk for each individual, including prior work using vector autoregression and group iterative multiple model estimation (GIMME) 14, 17 , has demonstrated substantial between-person heterogeneity in the emotional and behavioral precursors to suicidal thoughts. This suggests that personalized interventions informed by an individual's own data may be more efficient and potentially more effective than one-size-fits-all approaches 18.

PRECISE (PeRsonalizEd Clinical Intervention for Suicidal Events) is a brief, blended individual therapy and EMA intervention using an idiographic approach. Over six weeks, participants complete EMA five times each day while attending six weekly coaching sessions; at each session, participants' idiographic models, refit to all EMA data collected to date, are reviewed to identify person-specific emotional and behavioral correlates of suicidal ideation, and these outputs are used to tailor delivery of DBT-based skills in real time. PRECISE was iteratively refined through a user-centered design process and an initial case series (N = 3), which demonstrated high engagement and satisfaction and preliminary reductions in suicidal thoughts and negative emotions 18 . Building on this formative work, the present study reports feasibility, acceptability, and preliminary effects of PRECISE from an open trial in young adults referred for elevated suicide risk. In addition to measures of participation and satisfaction, we report change in a primary outcome of STBs alongside secondary outcomes (e.g., psychiatric symptoms, emotion regulation).

We hypothesized that PRECISE would be feasible and acceptable and would be associated with reductions in suicidal ideation, with secondary improvements in psychiatric symptoms and coping.

2.2 The PRECISE Intervention

The structure and design of PRECISE is detailed in a prior publication 18 . Briefly, PRECISE is a blended digital health intervention combining EMA with either in-person or telehealth coaching (via a HIPAA-compliant videoconferencing platform). After an initial baseline visit, participants completed six weeks of five-times-daily EMA assessing emotions, emotion regulation strategies, stressors, and STBs. On a weekly basis, idiographic models (vector autoregression and GIMME) were fit to each participant's EMA data to identify person-specific correlates of EMA reported suicidal ideation (e.g., which emotions and regulation strategies most strongly preceded or followed ideation). Participants then met with a trained coach for six sessions. All coaching sessions were delivered by a single coach (K.S.K.), a licensed clinical psychologist with intensive DBT training who also developed the intervention. Each session began with a suicide risk assessment; participants at acute risk completed a suicide risk management protocol. The coach and participant reviewed the participant's idiographic output as the starting point for a functional behavioral analysis, from which specific DBT-consistent skills (drawn from the domains of mindfulness, emotion regulation, and distress tolerance) were selected and practiced. Sessions concluded with development or refinement of an individualized safety plan. EMA continued between sessions to allow idiographic models to be updated prior to each subsequent session.

Before each session, the models were refit to all EMA data collected to date (cumulative), so later models drew on progressively more observations. Models were estimated through an automated R pipeline and reviewed by the coach before the session.

2.3 EMA Platform and Protocol

Following an initial baseline visit, during which participants were oriented to study procedures and trained in EMA completion, participants began a six-week course of EMA. Prompts were delivered via Inclivio (Inclivio, Inc.), a text-messaging-based scheduling platform, which sent participants a custom hyperlink to a brief survey (hosted via REDCap) at semi-random intervals throughout the day. At baseline, participants reported their typical sleep/wake schedule and any unavailable periods, which research staff used to configure individualized prompting windows in Inclivio: the first prompt was scheduled during the participant's waking hours, the second at a midmorning break, and the remaining one to three prompts at random intervals in the afternoon/evening (3:00-9:00 PM on weeknights; 11:00 AM-10:00 PM on weekends), with prompts separated by a minimum of two hours. Participants could dismiss, ignore, or return to a survey within a two-hour response window and received a text reminder 30 minutes after the initial prompt if uncompleted. Participants who missed a full day of EMA received a call from a research assistant to troubleshoot technical difficulties and encourage continued participation.

Suicidal ideation was assessed with a gating item ("Have you thought about hurting or killing yourself since [last night / in the past few hours]?"). Endorsement triggered graded follow-up items assessing intention to self-harm and to die by suicide in the past 30 minutes and in the present moment (each on a 5-point severity scale from "None" to "Severe"), and, if a self-harm or suicide attempt in the past 30 minutes was endorsed, items on method, medical treatment received, and whether the participant had contacted a crisis line, treatment provider, or informal support.

The survey logic included an automated safety response independent of coach review: endorsement of a past-30-minute attempt, self-harm, or moderate-or-greater present-moment suicidal intent immediately displayed an end-of-survey message directing the participant to crisis resources (988 Suicide & Crisis Lifeline, campus Counseling & Psychological Services, campus police, AFSP resources, and DBT provider referrals) and informing them that study staff would follow up, including contacting their emergency contact if they could not be reached within 48 hours.

Following screening and informed consent, participants completed baseline assessments, up to sixweeks of 5x/day EMA, and up to six PRECISE coaching sessions over the subsequent six weeks.

Baseline and follow-up assessments were conducted by a bachelor's-level research assessor who was trained in administering the CSSRS and MSSI, supervised by a licensed clinical psychologist, and not involved in delivering the intervention. Follow-up assessments were completed at 6 weeks (immediately post-treatment) and 12 weeks.

This trial was registered retrospectively at ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT07749391; https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT07749391). Prospective registration was not completed because the trial was conducted as a small pilot feasibility study funded by a private foundation (AFSP); [the NIMH K23 provided salary support for the first author and did not fund trial procedures].

2.5 Measures

Primary outcomes. STBs were assessed with two co-primary outcomes. The Columbia-Suicide Severity Rating Scale (CSSRS 19 ) rates the severity of STBs in the past month on a 6-point ordinal scale (0 = none to5 = active suicidal ideation with specific plan and intent). The Modified Scale for Suicidal Ideation (MSSI 20 ) comprises 18 items rated 0-3 and summed to a total score (range 0-54), providing a continuous index of ideation severity. MSSI items are clinician-rated for the past 1-2 days, whereas the CSSRS covers the past month. We designated both as primary because the intervention targeted suicidal behavior as well as ideation: the CSSRS indexes the severity of suicidal ideation, from passive wish to be dead to active ideation with plan and intent, whereas the MSSI is more sensitive to gradations of change in ideation.

Secondary outcomes. EMA-measured negative affect will be reported in a companion analysis of emotion regulation mechanisms. Emotion regulation strategy use was assessed with the 36-item Cognitive Emotion Regulation Questionnaire (CERQ 21 ), which yields nine 4-item subscales (range 4-20 each); consistent with common scoring practice, we computed an Adaptive composite (acceptance, positive refocusing, refocus on planning, positive reappraisal, and putting into perspective; 20 items, range 20-100) and a Maladaptive composite (self-blame, rumination, catastrophizing, and other-blame; 16 items, range 16-80) as the primary CERQ outcomes, reserving subscale-level detail for a planned companion analysis of emotion regulation as a mechanism of treatment response. General coping self-efficacy was assessed with the 26-item

Coping Self-Efficacy Scale (CSES 22 ), which asks respondents to rate, on a 10-point scale (1 = "cannot do at all" to10 = "certain can do"), their confidence in performing specific coping behaviors; a total score was computed as the sum of all 26 items (possible range = 26-260), consistent with the scale's original scoring rule requiring at least 80% item completion. The original CSES scale ranges from 0 to 10; the study form used 1-10, so totals are 26 points higher than on the original metric. Depressive and anxiety symptoms were assessed with the full-length PROMIS Depression 23 (28-item) and PROMIS Anxiety 24 (29-item) item banks. Scores were computed as IRT-calibrated T-scores (M = 50, SD = 10 in the US general population), which place participants on a common metric that can be compared directly with population norms and with other PROMIS studies. Rather than converting summed raw scores, we estimated each score from the participant's full pattern of item responses using expected a posteriori (EAP) estimation, which weights each item by how informative it is. This approach yields a valid score when some items are missing and, unlike maximum likelihood, produces a finite estimate for participants who endorse the lowest or highest option on every item. Responses were modeled under the graded response model, the model for ordered response categories under which the PROMIS banks were originally calibrated. Item discrimination and threshold parameters were taken from the original PROMIS emotional distress calibration study 24 rather than re-estimated in our sample, so that scores remain anchored to the established PROMIS metric and do not depend on the characteristics of this sample. A standard normal prior was placed on the latent trait, matching the reference population distribution on which the T-score metric is defined. Participants with less than 80% item completion (< 23/28 Depression, < 24/29 Anxiety items) were excluded from that timepoint, ensuring each score rested on enough items to be estimated precisely.

Implementation outcomes.

Feasibility and acceptability were evaluated using well-validated implementation science measures administered at post-treatment: the Acceptability of Intervention Measure (AIM), Intervention Appropriateness Measure (IAM), and Feasibility of Intervention Measure (FIM) 25 , each a 4-item, 5-point Likert scale (item range 1-5; total range 4-20), and the System Usability Scale (SUS) 26 , a 10-item measure of the usability of the EMA/telehealth platform yielding a total score ranging from 0-100.

Safety Monitoring:

Adverse events were monitored throughout the study. Serious adverse events (SAEs) were defined as any unexpected event resulting in death, persistent or significant disability, inpatient hospitalization, or a suicide attempt. EMA responses indicating self-injurious behavior or suicidal intent triggered an automated alert to the study coach, who reviewed the response, assessed current risk, and followed a standardized risk-management protocol. Safety data were reviewed semi-annually by an independent Data and Safety Monitoring Board.

2.6 Statistical Analysis

Change in outcomes assessed at discrete study visits (baseline, 6-week, 12-week) was modeled using Bayesian multilevel (mixed-effects) models fit in R 28 using the brms 29 package (interfacing with Stan 30 ), with time modeled as a categorical fixed effect and a random intercept for participant to account for repeated measures nested within individuals. Models used a Gaussian likelihood with weakly informative priors on the fixed-effect regression coefficients and the between-person standard deviation: Normal(0, 10) and Student-t(3, 0, 5), respectively, for all outcomes except the CSES, for which wider priors (Normal(0, 50) and Student-t(3, 0, 20)) were used to accommodate its larger range (26-260). Models were estimated with 4 chains, 3,000 iterations per chain (1,000 warmup), yielding 8,000 post-warmup draws. Convergence was assessed via R [ (potential scale reduction factor) and effective sample size. Posterior distributions were summarized using the median, 95% credible interval (CrI), the probability of direction (pd), and the percentage of the posterior distribution falling within a region of practical equivalence (ROPE) of ±0.1 standard deviations, following recommended Bayesian estimation and reporting practices 31 . These metrics were used as the primary inferential summaries for all outcomes, preserving effect magnitude in each measure's natural units. We additionally derived a standardized effect size (i.e., the posterior distribution of the fixed-effect estimate divided by the model-implied pooled SD; √[betweenperson variance + residual variance]), the multilevel Bayesian analog of Cohen's d. This was computed to facilitate comparison with effect sizes reported in prior DBT and idiographicintervention trials. Models used all available observations from all 30 enrolled participants, without imputation, which yields unbiased estimates under a missing-at-random assumption. As a feasibility trial, no formal power calculation was conducted; the target of 30 participants was chosen to estimate feasibility, acceptability, and preliminary effect sizes. The analyzed sample comprised 84 assessments from 30 participants (30 at baseline, 28 at 6 weeks, and 26 at 12 weeks); given the within-person correlation of repeated assessments (intraclass correlation ≈ 0.45 for both primary outcomes), this corresponds to an effective sample size of approximately 46 independent observations.

Prior to modeling EMA missingness, we examined whether missed assessments were associated with study week, prompt number within the day, and the participant's negative emotion or suicidal ideation at the preceding prompt, using frequentist logistic mixed-effects models with a random intercept for participant. Missingness increased across study weeks (OR = 1.55 per week, p < .001), consistent with EMA fatigue, and was highest at the morning prompt (OR = 0.89 per later prompt, p < .001). Missingness was not associated with negative emotion at the preceding prompt (b = 0.07, SE = 0.07, p = .30), and prompts were less likely to be missed after participants reported suicidal or self-harm thoughts (OR = 0.58, 95% CI [0.43, 0.77], p < .001). Missed assessments therefore did not disproportionately coincide with periods of heightened distress or ideation.

3. Results

3.3 Feasibility and Acceptability

Implementation outcomes collected at post-treatment indicated high acceptability, appropriateness, feasibility, and usability of PRECISE (Table 2; N = 28). Mean scores on the AIM, IAM, and FIM were all above the scale midpoint (4-20 range; midpoint = 12), with participantlevel scores falling below the midpoint on any of the three measures in only 1 of 28 cases (3.6%).

A high proportion of participants provided the maximum possible rating: on the AIM (9/28; 32.1%), IAM (9/28; 32.1%), and on the FIM 8/28 (28.6%), indicating that a substantial subset of participants rated the intervention as maximally acceptable, appropriate, and feasible. The three implementation measures were strongly intercorrelated (r = .70-.85), consistent with their shared theoretical basis as implementation outcome constructs.

Usability of the EMA/telehealth platform, assessed via the SUS, averaged 75.0 (SD = 17.1), corresponding to a rating of "good" on the standard SUS adjective rating scale. The majority of participants (18/28, 64.3%) scored at or above 68, the established benchmark for above-average usability; 12 participants (42.9%) scored in the "best imaginable/excellent" range. Two participants (7.1%) rated usability as "poor" (scores of 30 and 52.5), which may warrant followup given the platform's central role in EMA-based personalization. SUS scores were only weakly correlated with AIM, IAM, and FIM (r = .35-.39), suggesting that participants' evaluations of the technology platform were relatively distinct from their evaluations of the intervention content itself. 3.3.2 Safety Results

No serious adverse events occurred during the study. No participants attempted suicide, were psychiatrically hospitalized, or died. Eleven adverse events were reported by 5 of 30 participants (16.67%). All were episodes of non-suicidal self-injury (e.g., scratching, cutting, skin picking) rated as mild and judged unlikely to be related to the intervention. None required medical treatment or a change in study participation, and all resolved. One participant accounted for six of the 11 events, five of which occurred during the first week of the study.

Thoughts of self-harm or suicide were reported on 665 surveys (16.4%) by 28 participants. On 11 surveys (0.3%), 5 participants reported having engaged in self-injurious behavior in the past 30 minutes. Suicidal thoughts in the past 30 minutes were reported on 332 surveys (8.2%) by 22 participants; these surveys then asked participants to rate their current suicidal thinking. Current ratings were most often moderate thoughts without intent (149 surveys; 3.7% of all surveys), brief thoughts without intent (87; 2.2%), or no current suicidal thoughts (93; 2.3%). Moderate ratings were concentrated in one participant, who accounted for 129 of the 149. Ratings indicating active planning or a specific plan occurred on 3 surveys (0.1%) from 2 participants, and each triggered the emergency response protocol. All three instances resulted in the coach successfully making contact with the participant, completing a risk assessment, and confirming the participant was at low current risk and denied suicidal intent or a specific plan; each episode was subsequently discussed at the participant's next scheduled coaching session.

4. Discussion

In this open trial, a six-week, idiographically personalized DBT skills intervention was followed by large, sustained reductions in suicidal thoughts. The average participant began treatment with active suicidal ideation that included thoughts of a method, and by 12 weeks reported, on average, little more than a passive wish to be dead (CSSRS 3.2 → 0.95); MSSI scores fell by roughly twothirds. Depressive symptoms fell from the moderate range to the threshold of the mild range, anxiety symptoms declined more modestly, and confidence in coping rose by a quarter and was still climbing after treatment ended. These gains came with high engagement: 93% of participants were retained through treatment, most attended all six sessions, and nearly all rated the intervention as acceptable, appropriate, and feasible. These preliminary findings are consistent with the study's primary hypothesis that a brief, personalized, emotion-regulation-focused intervention would produce clinically meaningful reductions in suicidal ideation, and they extend prior nomothetic DBT findings by demonstrating that similar benefits may be achievable within a substantially briefer, idiographically tailored format.

Because PRECISE delivers a subset of DBT skills in a fraction of the usual time, a natural benchmark is the magnitude of change observed in full DBT protocols. In the most comprehensive meta-analysis to-date of DBT-Adolescents (DBT-A; 21 studies, N = 1,673), pre-post reductions in suicidal ideation were large (Hedges' g = -1.16) and self-harm reductions were similarly large (g = -0.98), while effects versus active control conditions were more modest (SI: g = -0.31; self-harm: g = -0.44), with longer treatment duration predicting larger ideation reductions 32 . Standard DBT-A protocols typically span 16-24 weeks of weekly individual therapy, concurrent skills group, and family sessions. That PRECISE produced ideation reductions of comparable magnitude within a six week format suggests that idiographic personalization (i.e., tailoring skill selection to each participant's own EMA-derived emotion-regulation patterns rather than delivering a fixed sequence of modules) may reduce the need for lengthier treatments, though this comparison is necessarily indirect given differences in sample (adult vs. adolescent), design (open trial vs.

controlled), and outcome measurement (Bayesian posterior probabilities vs. frequentist effect sizes). Consistent with this, adult DBT trials that have not incorporated idiographic personalization have shown more limited effects specifically on suicidal ideation even as they reduce self-injury 8 , suggesting that personalization could contribute to the treatment effect beyond treatment dose alone.

The results of the present study also extend a small but growing literature applying idiographic, EMA-derived personalization to brief interventions outside the STB domain. Depressive and anxiety symptoms improved substantially and precisely, placing general psychiatric symptom relief among the clearest findings in the study alongside the primary STB outcomes. Emotion regulation strategy use, however, showed a more selective pattern: maladaptive strategies (self-blame, rumination, catastrophizing, other-blame) declined with weak-to-moderate and strengthening evidence, while adaptive strategies (acceptance, positive refocusing, refocus on planning, positive reappraisal, putting into perspective) showed no credible change. This pattern may partly reflect a mismatch between measure and intervention: the CERQ indexes general cognitive strategies such as reappraisal, whereas PRECISE taught each participant only the DBT skills (predominantly distress tolerance, emotion regulation, and mindfulness) matched to their own idiographic drivers. Because the targeted strategies differed across participants, a group-level composite would be expected to change little even if each participant improved on the strategies they were taught. Examining change in each participant's targeted strategies is a more appropriate test and will be addressed in a planned companion analysis.

Notably, coping self-efficacy also diverged temporally from the primary ideation outcomes and other visit-based measures, which showed their largest gains by the end of active treatment: coping self-efficacy instead continued to accrue after treatment ended, suggesting participants may have kept consolidating or applying skills (or generalizing a sense of self-efficacy from treatment gains) even without ongoing intervention contact. This interpretation is speculative given the preliminary, single-arm design and should be tested directly in future work, potentially in the planned companion analysis of emotion regulation as a treatment mechanism.

4.1 Limitations

This open trial has several limitations consistent with its preliminary, uncontrolled design. The sample size (N = 30) was small, and the design lacked a comparison condition, precluding causal inference or efficacy testing; observed changes cannot be attributed to PRECISE's personalized components specifically, as opposed to nonspecific factors such as treatment engagement, repeated assessment, or coach support. Relatedly, the study design does not permit a direct test of the incremental value of personalization over non-personalized DBT skills training. The six-week EMA protocol, while longer than typical EMA studies, was associated with an overall compliance rate of 64.7% (below the >80% benchmark achieved in prior work using the same procedures) and appears to have contributed to participant fatigue, with missed prompts becoming more frequent later in the study and earlier within each day; and reliance on self-report measures introduces the possibility of measurement error and demand characteristics. Sample size available for analysis declined across follow-up (30 at baseline, 28 at 6 weeks, 26 at 12 weeks), and several secondary outcomes were likely underpowered to detect anything but large effects; null or inconclusive secondary findings should not be interpreted as evidence of no effect. Because participants were enrolled on the basis of elevated suicidal ideation, some improvement may reflect regression to the mean. Outcome assessments were not blinded, and fidelity was rated by the coach, who also developed the intervention. Participants were not restricted from concurrent mental health treatment, which was not systematically accounted for in the analyses. The sample consisted almost entirely of university students (97%). Finally, all coaching sessions were delivered by a single coach who also developed the intervention, which introduces the possibility of allegiance bias and limits the generalizability of findings to other providers.

4.2 Future Directions

Building on these preliminary findings, a logical next step is to compare PRECISE against an active, non-personalized treatment condition. For example, a randomized trial comparing PRECISE with a non-personalized version delivering the same number of sessions (e.g., skills selected by a standard sequence rather than idiographic models) could isolate the incremental benefit of idiographic personalization.

4.3 Conclusions

A brief, adjunctive, personalized intervention informed by idiographic modeling of ecological momentary assessment data was associated with meaningful and largely sustained reductions in STBs and psychiatric symptoms among young adults at elevated suicide risk in this open trial.

These preliminary, uncontrolled findings support further evaluation of PRECISE in a randomized controlled trial.
