Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard), also Internal Review Board - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term); not IEC followed by a number, which is a technical standard - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee/Commission, in the singular or plural - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee, Bioethics Committee/Commission, Ethikkommission - Ethical Review Authority/Unit/Office/Agency - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Human Research Protection Office/Program, Office for Human Subjects Protection - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee(s) on/for (Medical and Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

When a statement is expected

The module sorts each paper by where its data came from (the internal .data_source(); the signs and how the rule was tested are described in R/data-source.R): - own: the authors collected data from people or animals, for this paper, in an earlier study of their own, or (a protocol) will collect them; - existing_records: records about individual people that the authors obtained for this study, such as hospital records or a cohort; - existing_unclear: existing data about people from a source the module could not identify; - open_resource: an open resource any researcher can use, such as UK Biobank or NHANES; - none: no data about people or animals (a review, simulation, commentary, theory, tutorial, coding of published papers, or figures about groups); - uncertain: the signs were missing or pointed both ways; - no_text: the paper has no text.

A statement is expected for own, existing_records and existing_unclear. A paper without a statement in these classes, or in uncertain, is flagged. The module reads the text, and the results of power, stat_p_exact, stat_check, repo_check, data_check and prereg_check when they ran before it (run it after them); without stat_p_exact, it finds the p-values in the text.

On 199 PsyArXiv and medRxiv preprints labelled after the rule was fixed, the module decided right whether a statement is needed for 86.9% (the earlier live-data search: 83.4%). Its main errors: other people’s studies described in the words of a new study, records and specimens described only as counts, and open resources the paper does not call open.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download information from other preregistration sites, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. A TRO uses them for each trov:TransparentResearchPerformance. * run_results$exit_status: the exit status of the process that ran the script (0 = ended normally); NA when the process did not report one (a time limit, or Docker failing to start the container). * artifacts: one row per file, with stage (deposit: as downloaded from the repository, hashed from the downloaded file; before_run: the run folder just before the first script ran, after metacheck’s own changes, such as rewritten paths and unpacked archives; after_run: only the files the scripts made (status new), changed (changed) or removed (removed), as an unchanged file is the one listed before the run), path (relative to the run folder, as valid UTF-8, a byte of another encoding written as <xx>), size (bytes), sha256, mime_type (by extension, NA when unknown), source (the file’s address in the repository, for deposit). The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). Files metacheck itself copied or made between passes (see modifications, output_copied, file_placed) show in after_run too. A TRO uses the rows as trov:ResearchArtifacts and each stage as a trov:ArtifactArrangement. * modifications$sha256_before, modifications$sha256_after: the hash of the changed script as downloaded and as it ran; NA when the script is not at the same path in both (a script made from an R Markdown file). * environment: the system that ran the code (trov:TransparentResearchSystem in a TRO): metacheck_version, started_at, ended_at, host (R version, platform, operating system), sandbox, network_isolated (TRUE only when every Docker run had --network none; the process sandbox does not stop the network), internet, timeout_per_script, total_timeout, python_local (the Python run outside Docker and its version), and docker: runs (for each kind of run, r_run, r_install, python_run, python_install, the image and the docker run arguments, such as --network none, --read-only, --cap-drop ALL, no-new-privileges, the process limit and the memory and processor limits) and images (for each image, its ID and digests from docker image inspect, its creation time and the R and Python versions its environment declares).

run_results$checked says what the run of each script shows about the code, in five groups, so that a script that does not reproduce is kept apart from one the check could not test: ran (it ran to the end); code_error (it did not reproduce: it stopped with an error of the code or the deposit, could not be read, or needs a file the deposit does not hold); dependency (a package or program was not available: a package that could not be installed or was not loaded, a program outside R and Python, a system library, or R crashing in a package’s compiled code); time (it did not finish within the time limit for one script, or was not started because the time for all scripts had run out); and not_checkable (it could not be checked automatically: it needs a person, the internet, a key or another setting of the authors’ computer, one platform such as Google Colab, Windows or OpenSAFELY, more memory than the run allows, a file that was not downloaded, a screen, or a program that runs it, or it is a file of a package of the code that Python runs only as a module of that package). It is NA for files run inside another script, files that are not an analysis, files not read, and a Docker failure. summary_table counts each group (repro_ran_n, repro_code_error_n, repro_dependency_n, repro_time_n, repro_not_checkable_n), and the report’s first sentence gives them. The traffic light reflects the static readiness signals by default; a script in code_error that stopped with an error makes it red, as a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure, and a script in one of the other three groups keeps it from being green. Calls that need a screen, which the run does not have (dev.print(), dev.copy2pdf(), dev.copy2eps(), savePlot(), windows(), win.graph(), x11(), quartz(), View()), are replaced before the run by calls that need none, which modifications records as screen_call_replaced. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

Personality disorders have been traditionally framed as enduring dispositional conditions. Yet accumulating evidence suggests that they also reflect dynamic patterns of contextual adaptation. In this paper, we develop a formal framework that partitions personality stability into two components: an enduring portion (which is retained perfectly over time) and an adaptable portion (which dissipates over time, at either a fast or slow rate). After validating the model in computer simulations, we conduct a meta-analysis of 12 longitudinal investigations (N=17,785), yielding 145 test-retest correlations that quantify the stability of all personality conditions over 18 years. We then apply our formal model on these meta-analytic stability estimates to quantify the extent to which personality disorders are enduring vs. adaptable over time.

Results indicate that most of personality pathology (60-70%) is adaptable over 18 years' time, though it dissipates at a moderate rate (R≈55%). We conclude by suggesting that there is no formal distinction between personality pathology and psychopathology and outline a program aimed at studying all mental disorders as personality disorders.

In this section, we provide a brief historical tour on the two core, yet somewhat implicit, assumptions on the stability of personality conditions: namely, the assumptions that these conditions tend to be preserved over time (and are thus relatively enduring) or they tend to be revised over time (and are thus relatively adaptable). In outlining these positions, we note that it is unlikely that any researcher fully endorses either assumption to the exclusion of the other, as such a stance would over-simplify the dynamics of personality disorder. Instead, we acknowledge that researchers likely draw from both assumptions, though often emphasizing one over the other without clearly outlining the implications for theoretical and empirical research. Our aim with the present and subsequent parts of the paper, then, is to both theoretically clarify and empirically examine the dynamics of each assumption in order to determine the extent to which personality conditions are enduring vs. adaptable over time---as well as what it means to say that they are "enduring" versus "adaptable" over time. With that being said, we proceed by outlining each perspective on the stability of personality pathology, after which we draw a conclusion on how these perspectives speak about three different topics (namely, dynamics, mechanisms, and content), with only the first of them (dynamics) being of primary interest to our paper.

INTRODUCTION

Since their diagnostic origins, disorders of "personality" have been defined as "ingrained", "enduring", and "lifelong" (e.g., APA, 1952, p. 34; 1968, p. 41; 1980, p.305). This framing of personality pathology as chronic has profoundly shaped clinical theory and practice: For the longest time, disorders of "personality" have been seen as intractable and untreatable, perpetuating clinical pessimism regarding the prospect of meaningful therapeutic change (Bateman & Fonagy, 2000; Livesley & Larstone, 2018).

Today, this view of personality pathology is recognized as invalid. Several major longitudinal studies, including the Chestnut Lodge study (McGlashan, 1986), the PI-500 study (Stone et al., 1987), the Children in the Community Study (Johnson et al., 2000), the McLean Study of Adult Development (Zanarini et al., 2003), the Collaborative Longitudinal Personality Disorders Study (Gunderson et al., 2011) and the Nottingham Study of Neurotic Disorder (Yang et al., 2022), have shown that personality pathology can and often does ameliorate even after moderate periods of time (like 6 years). Importantly, developmental evidence has also illustrated that personality (pathology) is not "set like plaster," but shows considerable changes in response to significant life experiences, like entering a romantic relationship or undergoing psychotherapy (Bateman & Fonagy, 2000; Bleidorn et al., 2022; Bühler et al., 2024; Roberts et al., 2017). In the wake of these findings, several researchers have drawn the conclusion that "a formal definition of personality disorder should no longer include an indication of long-term stability" (Yang et al., 2022, p. 268).

Nevertheless, despite these influential findings, at least three uncertainties remain regarding the stability of personality pathology. First, it is unclear how stable personality disorders are across di#erent timeframes. Indeed, although a recent meta-analysis concluded that personality disorders are "moderately stable over time" (d 'Huart et al., 2023, p.15), it did not specify exactly how much time. This is a major shortcoming because a 'moderate' coeficient of test-retest stability (e.g., r=.50) over the short-term implies only brief stability; but the same coeficient over the long-term implies more enduring stability (see Bleidorn et al., 2022; Morey & Hopwood, 2013). Without stratifying meta-analyses by specific timeframes (short-vs. long-term), it is impossible to know whether personality disorders are "moderately stable" for a year or for a lifetime.

Second, it is currently not known what mechanisms are responsible for stability in personality pathology. This is another important uncertainty because even though two psychological phenotypes might exhibit the same long-term stability (e.g., a test-retest correlation of r=.40 over 10 years), the ways by which they arrive at this stability may di#er. In one case, for instance, test-retest coeficients may steadily decline over time (e.g., r=.80, .70, .60, .50, .40), implying that a phenotype is subject to revision. In another case, however, test-retest coeficients may remain relatively consistent over time (e.g., r = .46, .44, .42, .40, .40), implying greater resistance to revision and a more enduring disposition. Although other areas in psychology have developed formal frameworks for modeling these patterns of stability (e.g., Fraley, 2002; Fraley et al., 2004; Fraley & Roberts, 2005), the field of personality pathology lacks a comparable formal framework for inferring whether personality pathologies tend to be gradually revised over time (as in the former case) or tend to preserve their properties (as in the latter case).

Finally, because the field lacks a comprehensive formal theory to understand the dynamic mechanisms involved in stability and change in personality pathology, diagnostic debates persist over the very nature of this pathology. Indeed, at the time of writing, the field is split in two competing camps. The first camp endorses a more "adaptable" perspective, viewing personality pathologies as adaptations to broader life conditions (Lilienfeld et al., 2019; Luyten et al., 2020 Luyten et al., , 2021;; Sharp & Wall, 2021; A. G. Wright et al., 2022; Zavlis, 2024; Zavlis et al., 2025). The second camp, however, endorses a more "enduring" perspective, viewing personality pathologies as entrenched deviations in core personality dispositions (García et al., 2024; Miller et al., 2001; Sleep et al., 2019; Widiger et al., 2012a). In the absence of a framework that formalizes and empirically scrutinizes these views, it remains unclear to what extent personality pathology is best understood as an ephemeral contextual adaptation or as a more enduring personality disposition. 1 In this pre-registered investigation (see https://osf.io/yrxdv/overview), we aim to address this issue by developing a formal framework that partitions the stability of personality pathology into two components: an enduring portion (which is perserved perfectly over time) and an adaptable portion (which dissipates over time at a particular rate). We then apply this formal framework to all available test-retest correlations on the stability of personality conditions to estimate the extent to which these conditions are enduring versus adaptable over time. Before we proceed with our formal framework, though, we first clarify how we conceptualize personality stability and then outline the historical origins of our two examined perspectives on personality stability vs. change. 1 Throughout the paper, we use the terms "personality pathology" and "personality conditions" to refer rather abstractly to all sorts of psychological phenotypes that have been historically classified under the rubric of personality (e.g., "depressive personality pathology"). We further use the term "personality disorders" to refer to the traditional categorical personality disorders (e.g., borderline personality disorder), which are no longer part of recent diagnostic systems, as well as the term "personality disorder" (singular) to refer to the modern diagnostic category of dimensional personality disorder (defined by criterion A, self-interpersonal functioning, and criterion B, maladaptive personality traits). Finally, we refrain from providing a definition of the broader idea of "personality pathology" as we believe that such a definition is contingent on the results of our analyses. We discuss possible interpretations of "personality pathology" in our Discussion, and choose the one that we believe is in most agreement with both the results of this paper specifically, and the evidence from personality-related fields, more broadly.

What do we mean by stability?

Contemporary psychometrics recognises various types of stability, including structural stability (the persistence of the covariance structure of a construct over time), ipsative stability (the continuity of the configuration or profile of attributes within a person across time), and individual-level change (the magnitude and direction of change exhibited by each person over time; De Fruyt et al., 2006; Roberts & Mroczek, 2008). In this section, however, we focus on two other types of stability that are most commonly applied in the context of personality pathology, namely, mean-level stability and rank-order stability. Upon outlining each type, we then situtate our study within the latter rank-order stability.

To begin with, we consider mean-level stability, defined as the overall proportion of pathology that changes over a certain timeframe (Morey & Hopwood, 2013). Overall, there are two types of mean-level stability: categorical (aka diagnostic) and dimensional (aka severity-based). The first type, categorical stability, refers to the proportion of patients that retain their diagnostic status over a certain follow-up timepoint (see Morey & Hopwood, 2013). For instance, if 80 out of 100 patients retain their diagnostic status of personality disorder at a particular follow-up timepoint, then their diagnostic stability will be 80%. This kind of stability is interesting insofar as the diagnosis in question has clinical utility (e.g., it can be leveraged for prognosis or treatment planning). However, as various studies have indicated, diagnostic stability for personality pathology can be problematic precisely because it lacks clinical utility: patients can 'lose' their personality diagnoses without experiencing meaningful therapeutic change (see Gunderson et al., 2011; Zanarini et al., 2010). Moreover, the cut-of points upon which personality diagnoses are based are rather arbitrary---and, historically, were only established to enable assessments of reliability rather than to reflect natural discontinuities in psychopathology (Markon et al., 2011). In that sense, while categorical stability can be useful descriptively, it is limited theoretically because its arbitrary thresholds obscure the natural continuity of personality pathology.

The second type of mean-level stability addresses this theoretical shortcoming by directly quantifying the degree to which the average severity of (personality) psychopathology changes from one time point to the next (Morey & Hopwood, 2013). As an example, consider the same sample of 100 patients: If at baseline, they endorsed an average of 12 PD criteria but at a follow-up endorsed only 6 criteria, then they experienced a 50% reduction in their symptoms. This conception of stability is powerful because it directly captures the magnitude of change in personality pathology over time. Nonetheless, this approach sufers from one other problem: group-level changes may obscure what happens at the individual level, as most of the change observed may be due to a small number of patients showing dramatic improvements (and most of the persistence could be attributed to either a balance between improvements in some patients and deterioration in other patients; or a genuine lack of improvement in all patients). Thus, although providing a useful snapshot of aggregate symptom change, mean-level stability obscures individual diferences in change (typically captured via growth curve modeling) and fails to indicate whether individuals maintain their relative positions over time. This leads us to the other type of stability: namely, rank-order stability. Rank-order stability is about the extent to which individuals maintain their relative positions on the personality pathology continuum over time (Morey & Hopwood, 2013). This type of stability is typically quantified based on correlations between measures of personality pathology over time. If these correlations are high (e.g., r=.60), then they imply that the relative ordering of patients in terms of personality disorder severity remains preserved over time (i.e., those scoring high tend to remain high and those scoring low tend to remain low). Conversely, if these correlations are low (e.g., r=.30), then they imply that the relative ordering of patients in the severity continuum has changed dramatically, either for better (those who scored high now score low) or for worse (those who scored low now score high). A key limitation of this kind of stability is that it does not consider mean-level changes, meaning that it only ofers an index of personality consistency without telling us whether patients systematically improve or worsen over time.

In this paper, we focus on rank-order stability because we are interested in how people maintain their relative position on the personality pathology continuum over time. That is, we are interested in the extent to which people's personality pathology remains consistent relative to others over time (e.g., consistently high vs. consistently low). To the extent that people's personality pathology is not consistent over time, we are interested in the overall magnitude of change (e.g., high, moderate, or low change?). In the following section, we situate this question historically by outlining two perspectives that have tended to emphasise greater versus lesser stability in personality and its pathology.

Personality Disorders as Enduring Disorders

To begin with, we consider what we term the "enduring" assumption of personality conditions: that is, the view of these conditions in terms of largely stable "dispositions". Although the history of this viewpoint is long and multifaceted, we consider four periods of particular relevance to our paper: namely, the constitutional accounts of the ancient times, the early clinical accounts of the 19-20th centuries, the trait accounts of the twentieth century, and the contemporary accounts of the twenty-first century.

Starting with ancient accounts, it is noteworthy that they treated personality as an enduring phenotype that was fixed by the constitution of the body. For instance, in ancient Greece, Hippocrates (400 B.C.E./1931) and later Galen (190 C.E./2020) held that human personality is rooted in four bodily fluids (blood, yellow bile, black bile, and phlegm), with imbalances in such fluids leading to particular temperamental traits (e.g., an excess of blood leading to cheerful, sociable traits, now known as "extraversion", and an excess of black bile leading to sad, withdrawn traits, now known as "neuroticism"). Likewise, in ancient India, Ayurvedic medicine traced enduring individual differences to three bodily systems, called doṣas (which governed movement and anxiety; ambition and irritability; and calmness and stability), with again the relative proportions among them determining a person's broad way of being (Caraka, ca. 100 C.E./1981). Finally, ancient China echoed the same sentiment, with its classical medicine grounding temperament in the movements of qi as shaped by the balance of yin and yang, the predominance of the Five Phases (wood, fire, earth, metal, and water), and the functional state of their corresponding organs (the liver, heart, spleen, lungs, and kidneys; Huangdi Neijing, ca. 2nd Century B.C.E./2011). From these perspectives, then, personality was understood as an enduring phenotype, fixed by the constitution of the organism and preserved over time precisely because that constitution was thought to be invariant over time.

Although there were other interesting developments in the intervening centuries (most notably, philosophical perspectives that psychologised the idea of "the self" and drew clearer distinctions between "temperament" and "character"; Berrios, 1993 Berrios, , 1996)), the most relevant theoretical development for our purposes came from early psychiatry in the 19-20th century. During this time, Morel (1857) first introduced the clinical idea of a "temperamental pathology", holding that certain enduring pathological phenotypes are rooted in the constitutional and heritable makeup of the individual. Building on this, Koch (1891) then introduced the idea of "psychopathic inferiority" to make sense of a range of enduring but non-psychotic abnormalities (like chronic emotional excitability, impulsivity, and eccentricity) that were neither forms of insanity nor of intellectual deficiency, yet nonetheless caused persistent difficulties across life. More broadly, Ribot (1896/1897) developed a clinical framework to partition personality into various biological types, like the "sensitive", "active", and "apathetic" types, which loosely mirror contemporary ideas of "borderline", "narcissistic", and "schizoid" personalities. Finally, this period saw the first systematic attempts to classify these enduring types, with Kraepelin (1913 Kraepelin ( /1919) ) defining disordered personalities as inborn constitutional predispositions to the major mental illnesses, (Schneider, 1923 (Schneider, /1958) ) defining them as constitutionally abnormal personalities that cause sufering to the self or others, and Spitzer eventually classifying them formally in DSM-III as "enduring" and "inflexible" ways of being (APA, 1980, p.305). Across these psychiatric accounts, then, personality and its pathology remained largely enduring and constitutional phenotypes, though now recast in more clinical language---langauge, which as we will see later contrasts sharply some earlier "reaction-based" and "adaptable" framings of personality pathology.

A third major development in this enduring viewpoint came in the twentieth century, with the rise of psychometric perspectives on personality. The pivotal figure here was Gordon Allport (1937), who was the first to define the idea of "personality trait" in a scientific way, namely, as an enduring disposition to perceive and respond to particular classes of stimuli in a consistent way (e.g., extraversion disposing a person to approach social stimuli). This trait concept was subsequently operationalised by Eysenck (1947) and Cattell (1956), who used factor analysis to reduce various adjectives to a small number of dimensions of personality (as well as to define psychopathology as an extreme standing on those dimensions of personality). Over the following decades, a recurrent set of five such dimensions emerged across analyses in various societies (Goldberg, 1990; Norman, 1963; Tupes & Christal, 1958), culminating in the famous five-factor view of personality: that is, the view that five broad ways of being (indicating ways of "thinking, feeling, behaving, and relating"; Widiger, 2011, p.103) are the "universal" and "evolutionarily-developed" traits of personality (e.g., Costa & McCrae, 1992). Importantly, while more data-driven (and thus evidence-based), this perspective still held that the five-factor traits are "endogenous basic tendencies": that is, biologically-rooted dispositions that remain largely stable across the life course and are, at least in principle, uninfluenced by environmental experience (Costa & McCrae, 1992 , 1994).

Finally, we reach the contemporary research period, which has made at least three contributions that both clarify and extend the "enduring" view of personality (and its pathology). The first of these contributions is the idea that personality may not be as stable as once thought. Indeed, recent meta-analytic evidence shows that while the five-factor traits are moderately stable over time (with rank-order correlations averaging around r = .60 across the five-factor dimensions; Bleidorn et al., 2022), this stability is considerably weaker than the trait tradition had originally suggested (Bleidorn et al., 2022; Wright et al., 2025). Although this finding does not imply that personality is devoid of stability, it does suggest that personality (and its pathology) comprise both enduring aspects (that tend to persist over time) and adaptable aspects (that tend to be revised over time) (Cloninger et al., 1993; DeYoung, 2015; Fleeson, 2001; D. P. McAdams & Pals, 2006).

The second contribution adds to this pattern of stability by suggesting that the enduring parts of personality may persist for a variety of reasons, not only due to constant biological factors alone. Specifically, beyond the constant biological factors that reinforce some aspects of personality (most notably, neurodvelopmental traits like callous-unemotional traits, autistic traits, and attention-deficit hyperactivity traits; Faraone & Larsson, 2019; Tick et al., 2016; Viding et al., 2005), personality science has identified at least two additional sources of personality stability. The first source is the environment itself, which could entrench various personality aspects---for instance, adversity entrenching neuroticism (Rosenek et al., 2026) and caregiving relationships entrenching attachment styles (Fraley, 2002). The second source is the so-called person-environment relationship: specifically, the tendency of human beings to select, evoke, and shape environments consistent with their traits, which in turn reinforce those very traits (Buss, 1987; Caspi & Roberts, 2001; Roberts & Caspi, 2003; Scarr & McCartney, 1983; Wrzus & Roberts, 2017). Together, these lines of evidence suggest that several aspects of personality may become "enduring" (or, as we will later on see, "rigid") due to a variety of reasons.

Finally, the last key contribution of contemporary personality science is the explicit definition of personality disorders as "extreme and maladaptive variants of common personality dimensions" (e.g., see Miller et al., 2001; Samuel et al., 2013; Suzuki et al., 2015; Widiger et al., 2002). According to this view, personality pathologies can be defined as specific configurations of extreme and maladaptive personality trais----for example, borderline personality disorder as a configuration of extreme emotional, impulsive, and antagonistic traits (Mullins-Sweatt et al., 2012). Empirically, this assumption appears intuitive because, when pushed to their extreme, most psychological phenotypes lead to maladaptive ways of being (e.g., maladaptive ways of "thinking, feeling, behaving, and relating"; Widiger, 2011, p.103). Conceptually, however, this assumption proved to be rather problematic for at least two reasons: first and foremost, all mental disorders inherently ential extreme personality traits (e.g., emotional disorders inherently entail extreme neuroticism, psychotic disorders entail extreme psychoticism, and so on), implying that personality traits may define not only the personality disorders but all mental disorders, more broadly (e.g., DeYoung & Krueger, 2025; Zavlis & Fonagy, 2024; see also Discussion for details). Second, the view of "personality disorders" as "extreme variants" of personality traits suggests, at least implicitly, that "disorders of personality" must have the same stability as "traits of personality" (e.g., see Sleep et al., 2019 Sleep et al., , p.1203)). Although the jury is still out on this hypothesis, it is worth noting that a growing corpus of empirical evidence appears to contradict it by showing that the maladaptive features of personality are not as stable as adaptive features of personality (Bleidorn et al., 2022; Hopwood, 2022).

To summarise, therefore, the enduring perspective has evolved considerably over the centuries, starting with the view of personality (pathology) as a constitutionally-fixed phenotype in ancient times; moving to a similarly biological though less "fixed" view of personality (pathology) in the nosological and research times of the 19-20th century; and reaching contemporarily to a more nuanced viewpoint that acknowledges how personality contains both enduring and adaptable aspects. With respect to the disorders of personality, contemporary trait theorists define them using the more enduring aspects of personality (i.e., "personality traits"), thereby suggesting, at least implicitly, that personality disorders should be at least as enduring as personality traits. Although some evidence contradicts this viewpoint, it is worth noting that no study to date has used formal modeling to estimate the extent to which personality pathology is stable at different timeframes. Our stratified meta-analytic formal test attempts to address this aim later by explicitly estimating the proportion of personality (pathology) that endures vs. dissipates over 18 years' time.

Before we proceed with this test, however, we continue by explicating an alternative assumption on the stability of personality disorders: namely, the assumption that these disorders are adaptable, not enduring, over time.

Personality Disorders as Adaptable Disorders

The second assumption on the stability of personality conditions is what we term the "adaptable" assumption: that is, the view of these conditions as largely adaptable as opposed to durable over time. Although this view does not reject the presence of enduring dispositions, its main contribution is the emphasis that personality pathology is expressed in a largely dynamic and context-driven way. As with the enduring assumption, we trace this notion of adaptability across four historical time points: the ancient moral, 18-19th century clinical, 20th century psychobiological and psychoanalytic, and, finally, the contemporary accounts on the so-called "character" problems (which as we will later see are distinct from the "personality" problems in both their assumed stability and nature as well).

Starting with the ancient accounts, it is worth noting that the adaptable viewpoint is rooted less in medicine than in moral philosophy. According to moral philosophy, "character" is distinct from "personality" in that it is not fixed by one's biology ("temperament") but is rather something acquired through one's environmental landscape (and can thus be revised over time). An early example of this dichotomy comes from ancient Greece, where Aristotle (350 BCE / 1925) held that character (ēthos) is formed via habit such that one could become "just" by performing "just acts" and "temperate" by performing "temperate acts". Likewise, a parallel intuition appeared in ancient China, where (Confucius, ca. 5th-3rd Century BCE/1893) suggested that "by nature men are nearly alike; by practice they get to be wide apart" (Analects, 17.2), suggesting, much like Aristotle did, that character is the product of habit and cultivation rather than temperament alone. Finally, these positions took their most clear form in a book written by Theophrastus (319 BCE./1870), a pupil of Aristotle's who outlined thirty "character types" that represented "maladaptive ways of relating to others" within ancient Athens. Example types here include the "Distrustful" man (who holds "a presumption that all men are unjust") and the "Arrogant" man (who holds "a certain scorn for all the world beside oneself") which bear striking resemblence to the contemporary "paranoid" and "narcissistic" personality pathologies, respectively (Theophrastus, 319 BCE./1870). Thus, these early perspectives appeared to suggest that there are at least some aspects of personality (i.e., "character traits") that are acquired and revisable, and therefore distinct from the fixed "temperament" of the medical tradition---a dichotomy later formalised by Kant (1798 Kant ( /1978)), who defined "temperament" as what nature makes of a person and "character" as what a person makes of themselves.

Although the concept of character as acquired and revisable continued to be developed in the intervening centuries (most notably by empirical philosophers, like Locke (1690), who famously but falsely took this environmental viewpoint to the extreme conclusion that the mind is "blank slate"), the most relevant development for our purposes came, once again, from early clinical psychiatry. Specifically, around the turn of the nineteenth century, the humane reform of asylum care known as "moral treatment" recast mental disturbance as something shaped by, and re-mediable via, a person's circumstances rather than fixed by their temperament (with "moral" here denoting the psychological and social, rather than the ethical). This reform found its clearest expression in the work of Pinel (1801, p.151) in France (who held that personality pathologies stem from "deficient and ill-directed upbringing") and Tuke (1813, p.23) in England (who further noted that "the influence of a well-regulated moral discipline" could mitigate them). Together, these reforms emphasised the role of environmental conditions in shaping "character" and "personality", and were among the first to intuit that their "pathologies" might respond to psychosocial therapies.

The third major development in this adaptable viewpoint came during the twentieth century, when the environmental intuitions of "moral treatment" were elaborated into two seminal theories on "personality" and "character" development: namely, the "psychobiological" and "psychoanalytic" theories, respectively, The first theory of "psychobiology" was developed by Adolf Meyer, who was among the first psychiatrists to state explicitly that all mental disorders (including personality disorders) are not fixed "diseases" but rather natural "reactions" to biological and social stress (Meyer, 1931 (Meyer, /1957)). Importantly, Meyer also suggested that these "reactions" are maladaptive habits that were learnt (and could thus be un-learnt), an emphasis that made him a founding influence on occupational therapy, which sought to remediate such maladaptive habits through structured activities (Meyer, 1983). Beyond "psychobiology", the second theory, "psychoanalysis", was developed by Sigmund Freud, who was the first to define the idea of "character" psychologically (as the amount of energy a person invests in themselves vs. others) and to also suggest that early caregiving relationships are essential in the formation of "character" (Freud, 1914 (Freud, /1957 (Freud, , 1923 (Freud, /1961 (Freud, , 1931 (Freud, /1961)). This relational emphasis was subsequently deepened by the object-relations analysts, such as Klein (1946 Klein ( /1975)), Fairbairn (1952), Bion (1962), and Winnicott (1965) and by the attachment theorists, like Bowlby (1969), Ainsworth et al. (1978), and Main et al. (1985), who together established that the quality of one's earliest relationships are a central determinant of later psychological functioning (Fonagy et al., 2018). Taken together, these developments solidified the view that all psychopathology (and, particularly, "personality" or "character" pathology) is acquired reactively in response to one's largely relational circumstances as opposed to arising from their temperament.

At this point, it is worth noting a distinction that has been rather implicit across the two traditions we have traced: namely, the distinction between "character" and "personality". On the one hand, the term "personality" (as well as "temperament") was preferred by theorists who viewed various psychological characteristics in a broad and enduring way (i.e., personality as a relatively stable way of being, like way of thinking, feeling, behaving, relating, and so on; e.g., Widiger, 2011). On the other hand, as we have now seen, the term "character" was preferred by theorists who discussed specific aspects of personality in a more adaptable way (i.e., specific parts of personality on how humans relate to themselves as well as others, which are learnt and can thus be un-learnt; Freud, 1923 Freud, /1961)). This split in emphasis is interesting because it metastised into the first edition of the DSM, which defined some personality disorders as "lifelong pattern of action or behaviour" (APA, 1952, p.34) while others as an "exaggeration of certain character and behavior patterns" that represent "regressive reactions due to environmental or endopsychic stress" (APA, 1952, p.36). In other words, one class of personality disorders was defined as more "constitutional" (p.35) while another as more "reactive" (p.34) in nature.

Although this split appreciated how some "personality-related" phenotypes were enduring while others were reactive, it was eventually flattened when DSM-II and DSM-III re-defined all personality pathologies as uniformly "enduring" and "inflexible" ways of being (APA, 1980, p.305). As intellectual historians have since noted (Lane, 2009, p.60): "This bold stroke [i.e., removing the term 'reactions'] amounted to a major shift in approach: it changed the very meaning of illnesses for clinicians and patients and eliminated the dynamism or struggle of the patient's reaction. Illnesses began to define people rather than surfacing as an aspect of their personality, and symptoms were not merely an expression of unease tied to specific conditions. They were maladaptive problems that psychiatrists must treat and that society in general should fear." (parentheses [] ours).

Against this background knowledge, it is notable that contemporary researchers have attempted to revive traces of this traditional adaptable way of view personality. Early revivals came from the social-cognitive theorists, who, in the so-called person-situation debate, emphasised the dynamic and context-driven aspects of personality (e.g., Mischel and Shoda, 1995). Today, this dynamic view of personality has moved beyond theoretical debate and onto firm empirical footing---giving rise to the field of "personality dynamics" that has since been applied to both personality and its pathology. Below, we outline three key empirical contributions from this field of personality dynamics.

The first contribution is the well-replicated observation that both personality and its pathology change considerably over time---with this change being apparent regardless of how stability is operationalised. Specifically, at the level of mean-level stability, personality traits tend to move in a direction of increasing maturity over adult life (with individuals becoming more emotionally stable, agreeable, and conscientious on average; (Bleidorn et al., 2022; Roberts et al., 2006) and symptoms of personality indicate a parallel remission during long-term follow-ups (Gunderson et al., 2011; Zanarini et al., 2003 Zanarini et al., , 2010)). Likewise, at the level of rank-order stability, the relative ordering of individuals on measures of adaptive and especially maladaptive features of personality is only moderately perserved over time (Bleidorn et al., 2022; Roberts & DelVecchio, 2000; Wright et al., 2025). Thus, however conceptualised, stability indices consistently indicate that personality contains both adaptable elements (that diminish substantially over time) and enduring elements (that remain more stable over time), implying that the most productive question facing the field is determing the relative proportion of these parts of personality pathology.

Beyond this general question on personality stability, a second contribution from contemporary science on personality is the recognition that the adaptable elements of personality (pathology) shift not only due to environmental but also biological influences over time---an intuition already anticipated, as we have seen, by Meyer's psychobiology, which conceptualised all mental disorder as a reaction to biological and social stress. On the environmental side, personality (pathology) can be revised by significant life events (such as entering the workforce; Bühler et al., 2024) and psychological interventions (such as psychotherapy; Roberts et al., 2017). On the biological side, personality (pathology) fluctuates due to endogenous physiological changes (such as ovarian-hormone fluctuations across the menstrual cycle; Eisenlohr-Moul et al., 2018). Together, this evidence indicates that much like personality can be maintained by both biological (e.g., genetic) and environmental forces (e.g., person-environment transactions), so too can personality can be revised by both biological (e.g., hormonal) and environmental (e.g., relational) factors as well.

Finally, the last and arguably most important contribution is the introduction of the idea of "self-interpersonal functioning": that is, the view, promoted within the alternative model of DSM-5 and the dimensional model of ICD-11, that personality pathology consists mainly of impairments in how a person relates to themselves (i.e., how they establish an identity and direction in life) and to others (i.e., how they build empathic and intimate relationships; see Bender et al., 2011; Skodol et al., 2011). This idea is rooted in the psychoanalytic traditions of the previous century, which, as we have seen, located personality pathology in people's characteristic ways of relating to self and others (i.e., their revisable character traits; see Freud, 1914 Freud, /1957 Freud, , 1923 Freud, /1961 Freud, , 1931 Freud, /1961)). While this idea has been influential in terms of shifting toward a dimensional classification system of personality disorder, it is currently impeded by two uncertainties. First and foremost , it is unclear whether it can demarcate a unique disorder of personality given that it is linked transdiagnostically with various forms of psychopathology (e.g., see García et al., 2024; Kerber et al., 2024; Widiger et al., 2019). Second, it is further unclear what kind of temporal dynamics are predicted by this position: on the one hand, several theorists have suggested that personality disorder is largely an adaptation to broader life conditions; yet, on the other hand, the same theorists have also highlighted that such adaptations could be held with extreme rigidity over time and space (Luyten et al., 2020; Sharp & Wall, 2021). As we shall see subsequently, both observations are compatible with the adaptable viewpoint since although a phenotype may be adaptable in the sense that it is ultimately revisable, it may nonetheless be retained at a high rate and thus change only gradually over time.

To summarise, just like the enduring viewpoint, the adaptable viewpoint has evolved considerably over the centuries, starting out as an environment-based description of "character" in ancient times, moving on to the incorporation of both environmental and biological stress factors in the development of character and personality during the early psychiatric and psychoanalytic times, and arriving today, almost full circle, to the view of personality disorders as adaptable disorders in self-interpersonal functioning. Currently, several theorists embrace this view of personality pathology by viewing it as, by and large, adaptable. However, although there is evidence that personality pathology is adaptable over time, there is also evidence that some specific symptoms of personality (like "chronic feelings of emptiness" or "sensitivity to interpersonal events") are also durable over longer time periods (Gunderson & Lyons-Ruth, 2008; Keuroghlian & Zanarini, 2015). On that basis, the most productive question facing the field today is not whether personality disorder is adaptable, but the extent to which it is adaptable versus enduring over time.

Before we proceed with our own empirical eximination of this question, though, we first summarise the key lessons learnt from the historical outline above and then situate our own empirical investigation within one of these lessons.

Summary of enduring vs. adaptable personality dynamics

The historical outline above illustrates that there are at least three inter-related questions facing the field in regard to the nature of the disorders of "personality". First and foremost, there is the question of dynamics, with theorists disagreeing, at least implicitly, on the extent to which they view personality disorders as largely adaptable versus enduring over time. Second, there is the question of the source of these dynamics, with research consistently showing that both personality stability and change come either from environmental or biological influences (but being unclear as to which of these is most relevant for the disorders of personality). Finally, there is the question of the content of personality pathology, with researchers either defining personality pathology in a largely specific way (i.e., as a disorder in self-interpersonal functioning) or in general way (i.e., as a disorder in the ways of "thinking, feeling, behaving, and relating").

Although our paper speaks theoretically about all of these questions, its core empirical contribution targets only the first of them: namely, the question about the extent to which personality disorder is adaptable versus durable over time. This focus of our analyses implies that we cannot empirically discern, at least within this paper, the source of these dynamics (i.e., whether personality stability vs. change comes mainly from environment or biology) or the content of personality pathology (i.e., whether most personality disorder symptoms are general or specific in nature). While this approach is limited in scope, we believe it is a necessary first step, since it enables us to first address the broader question over the extent to which personality disorder is adaptable versus durable, before future work turns to the more specific questions over the source and content of this disorder. Indeed, having first addressed the overall empirical question of the adaptable vs. enduring proportions of personality pathology, we later on use our formal framework to discuss the other two questions theoretically and draw novel conclusions about the nature and definition of not only personality pathology but all psychopathology broadly. Before we turn to these theoretical implications of work, though, we first introduce our formal framework.

DISCUSSION

In this study, we developed a formal model that distils the stability of personality disorder into two parts: an enduring part (that persists over time) versus an adaptable part (that dissipitates over time). Simulating data from this model, we have shown that the enduring part manifests as a long-term asymptote toward which personality stability settles; whereas the adaptable part decays toward that asymptote at a rate determined by how consistent it is with person-authored vs. stochastic influences.

An important aspect of our model is that it not only formally defines these aspects of personality disorder but can also empirically estimate them assuming it is applied to at least 4 test-retest coeEicients that indicate the stability of a particular phenotype over at least 8 years. Based on this property, we systematically reviewed and meta-analysed extant rank-order coeEicients on the stability of categorical personality disorders, showing that there exist enough of them to estimate the parameters from our model: specifically, there exist 145 test-retest coeEicients, quantifying the rank-order stability of categorical personality disorders over a period of up to 18 years (N = 17,785).

As a final step, then, we applied our model on these stability coeEicients, revealing that around 60-70% of overall personality disorder is adaptable, while around 30-40% is durable. Although some personality disorders (notably, antisocial and narcissistic personality disorders) exhibited greater uncertainty in their parameter estimates (due to a lack of suEicient test-retest coeEicients), our general result was preserved at the level of overall personality disorder, implying that dimensional personality disorder is mainly adaptable over time (despite also exhibiting a non-zero enduring baseline).

These findings have strong implications for how both personality pathology and psychopathology, more broadly, are conceptualised and examined empirically. In the discussion that follows we expand on each of these implications by suggesting that (a) personality stability (enduring or otherwise) defines all psychopathology, (b) personality content (however defined) also defines all psychopathology, and, finally, (c) all "mental disorders" should be studied as "personality disorders".

The stability of personality defines all psychopathology

Historically, one of the most influential and theoretically coherent ways of defining personality disorders was not as a distinct class of mental disorder, but rather as longterm variants of other "mental state disorders". This logic was first formalised in DSM-III, which defined personality disorders as "enduring patterns" that are "inflexible across a broad range of situations" (APA, 1980), and was later on extended by personality trait models that conceptualised personality disorders in terms of extreme and maladaptive personality traits (e.g., Samuel et al., 2013; Widiger et al., 2012). Importantly, although rarely recognized, the same logic persisted until recently---specifically, until the construction of DSM-5, during which it was proposed that personality disorders be reclassified as "early-onset, chronic variants of other Axis I disorders" rather than as categorically distinct forms of mental disorder (First et al., 2002). Thus, across history, one of the most notable ways of defining personality and its pathology was in terms of relatively stable and broad ways of being that persist across time and space.

In this study, we have examined this intuition by quantifying the "enduring" versus "adaptable" components of personality disorder stability over 18 years' time. In our preregistration, we had suggested that if personality disorder was mainly "enduring", then its stability coeEicients should asymptote above 0.5 indicating above-average eEects of an enduring influence. However, when we applied our model on stability coeEicients of traditional personality disorders, we found no evidence for such a strong enduring influence. What we found instead was that personality disorders contain mainly adaptable elements (60-70%) that subside moderately over time (R=0.55), implying that they do not behave as enduring disorders in the traditional sense outlined earlier.

Importantly, it is worth noting that there are strong reasons to expect that this result would be the invariant (if not even more robust) if personality pathology was operationalised using contemporary dimensional constructs (such as "selfinterpersonal functioning"), since these constructs have been explicitly birthed to be even more context-and developmentally sensitive than the traditional, cateogrical personality disorders (Bender et al., 2014; Sharp & Wall, 2021). In that sense, it may actually be that, however defined, personality pathology is not as enduring as once assumed to be.

Still, even if personality pathology could somehow be operationalised so as to be enduring (e.g., operationalised via the adaptive five-factor traits, which appear to be more "enduring" than the maladaptive variants of the five-factor traits), this would still not be enough to define personality pathology by the criterion of durability (or, formally, an attractor-like setpoint toward which a phenotype returns to over time). The reason why personality pathology could never be defined as an enduring pathology in this setpoint-like way is because there are at least two other psychopathologies that exhibit similarly enduring setpoint-like dynamics. The first of these is neurodevelopmental phenotypes (like autism), which are characterised by neurobiological setpoints toward which they are drawn to over time (Faraone & Larsson, 2019). The second of these is specific trait-like diEiculties (like rumination or habitual self-criticism), which may be characterised by experience-shaped set-points toward which their defining phenotypes likewise repeatedly return over time (Nowak et al., 2005). In that sense, setpoint-like endurance does not uniquely define "personality pathology," since it also typifies diEiculties characterised by neurobiologically anchored set-points, experience-shaped set-points, or some combination of the two.

Beyond endurance alone, however, another intricate point worth noting about personality stability is that it may alternatively be based on the adaptable parts of personality. Indeed, our results indicated that while personality disorders are mostly adaptable over time, they only fade at a moderate rate (R=0.55), with some personality disorders (like histrionic and antisocial) fading at even a lower rate (R=0.71 and R=0.80) ---implying that a considerable proportion of their adaptable elements is retained from one timepoint to the next (average span of 3.5 years). This pattern, which we failed to pre-register as a possible outcome, suggests that personality disorders may be stable not because they are largely enduring, but because their adaptable elements are only slowly revised over time. Interestingly, as our simulations have shown previously, such rigidity (or high retention rate) may arise when a phenotype keeps reproducing itself by transacting with the environment in ways that continually reinstate its existing state.

Thus, an alternative (and perhaps less restrictive) view of personality disorder stability may be that it reflects not whether a maladaptive phenotype is enduring per se, but rather the extent to which its adaptable elements resist revision over time.

Alas, however, this argument also fails as a unique definition of personality psychopathology for at least two reasons. First and foremost, some suEiciently high degree of stability is needed for any psychological diEiculty to be recognised as a mental disorder in the first place. To illustrate, consider how the DSM distinguishes mental disorders from transient reactions to adversity by requiring the former to persist beyond a specified period of time---with post-traumatic symptoms required to persist for more than one month to qualify as PTSD, psychotic symptoms for at least six months to qualify as schizophrenia, and even grief required to remain impairing for at least twelve months before qualifying as prolonged grief disorder (American Psychiatric Association, 2022). The only partial exception to this rule may be episodic disorders (such as single episodes of mania or depression); however, even these tend to recur over time and, when they do not, must at least produce a sustained period of impairment before being recognised as mental disorders (American Psychiatric Association, 2022; Hardeveld et al., 2010; Kessing et al., 2018). These examples imply that all mental health problems must become suEiciently persistent and resistant to change (and thus be part of one's personality) in order to be recognized as disorders at all. Indeed, to the extent that such mental health problems are only transiently present (and thus do not produce clinically significant or sustained impairment), they will typically fail to cross the threshold from a transient pathological mental state into a clinically significant psychopathology (American Psychiatric Association, 2022).

Second, and most importantly, there is now a wealth of research showing how all forms of psychopathology are typified by at least one type of person-environment transaction that stabilises them over time, namely, "stress-generation" (defined as the tendency of people to generate stressors that sustain or exacerbate their mental health conditions; see Hammen, 1991 Hammen, , 2006 Hammen, , 2015)). Indeed, a recent meta-analysis of 95 studies by Rnic and colleagues (2023) indicated that people experiencing all sorts of psychopathologies have a tendency to generate stressful events that partly depend on their own conduct (like interpersonal conflicts) while showing no comparable tendency to generate fateful events that occur independently of their conduct (such as the death of a loved one). Importantly, this pattern of person-dependent stress was also demonstrated to stabilise psychopathology (rendering it rigid in the manner outlined previously) by mediating the association of past with future psychopathology symptoms (see Dang & Xiao, 2025; Rnic et al., 2023). Finally, two recent meta-analyses have replicated these eEects and extended them by showing how the strongest predictors of this stressgeneration process are not specific mental health diagnoses, but rather transdiagnostically-occuring personality traits, such as rejection sensitivity, reassurance-seeking, dependency, perfectionism, antagonism, and disinhibition (Liu et al., 2024; Santee et al., 2023). Together, this large corpus of findings suggests that all psychopathologies may become resistant to change precisely because some of their characteristic aspects generate the very environmental contexts that continually reinforce them. 3Altogether, then, the evidence suggests that there might be two kinds of "personalityrelated" stability. On the one hand, there is the setpoint kind, which acts by pulling a dimension of psychology to a specific biologically-set or environmentally-set fixed point. On the other hand, there is the rigidity kind, which acts by rendering the adaptable elements of a dimension resistant to revision. Crucially, neither of these kinds of stability is unique to personality pathology: the setpoint kind clearly characterises diEiculties sustained by enduring neurobiological or environmental fixed points, while the rigidity kind is found across all psychopathology (insofar as psychopathology is defined by characteristic ways of being). Together, these pieces of evidence suggest that even if personality disorders were more stable than our findings suggest, that stability would not be enough to define them in a way that separates them from other mental disorders.

In the next section, we extend this argument to the level of personality content, showing that much like personality stability, personality content is also, paradoxically, unable to uniquely define personality disorder because it defines all forms of mental disorder.

The content of personality defines all psychopathology

From its origins, the idea of "personality" was defined not only by its "stability" but also by its "content", which was considered to span various dimensions of human existence (such as the cognitive, aEective, behavioural, and interpersonal dimensions of human existence; Allport, 1937; DeYoung, 2015; McAdams & Pals, 2006). Accordingly, the idea of "personality pathology" has been used to outline stable and broad impairments across various domains of functioning (e.g., like the domains of "thinking, feeling, behaving, and relating"; Kernberg, 1967; Widiger, 2011). As increasing evidence (including the present), however, begun suggesting that personality disorders cannot be defined by stability, the definition of personality pathology begun to shift toward the content rather than the stability of personality. Today, this shift toward content is largely complete, with the field defining personality disorder by the nature of its impairments rather than by how long those impairments last (Bender et al., 2014; Tyrer et al., 2019).

Although our study cannot directly speak about the content of personality, by virtue of showing that stability cannot uniquely define a "disorder of personality", it does beg the contemporary question: could the content of personality define the so-called "disorder of personality"? In this section, we address this question theoretically by arguing that the content of personality is unable to define a unique "disorder of personality" since much like stability, it appears to instead define all forms of psychopathology.

To make this case, we consider first the five-factor personality traits (also known as criterion B in the DSM-5 Alternative Model of Personality Disorders; García et al., 2024). These traits outline maladaptive ways of thinking (psychoticism), feeling (negative aEectivity), behaving (disinhibition), and relating (detachment and antagonism).

Although introduced to define the stylistic expression of personality pathology (e.g., Krueger et al., 2012), these traits have since been shown to be isomorphic to established dimensions of all psychopathology: namely, negative aEectivity with emotional psychopathology (Watson et al., 2022), psychoticism with cognitiveperceptual psychopathology (Kotov et al., 2020), disinhibition with impulse-related psychopathology (Krueger et al., 2021), and detachment and antagonism with interpersonal psychopathology (Wright et al., 2022 (Wright et al., , 2023)). Importantly, each of these dimensions of psychopathology has been shown to organise specific classes of mental disorder, with the emotional dimension organising the 'mood disorders' (like depression and anxiety; Watson et al., 2022), the cognitive-perceptual dimension the 'thought disorders' (like psychosis; Kotov et al., 2020), the behavioural dimension the 'impulse disorders' (like substance abuse; Krueger et al., 2021), and the interpersonal dimension various 'relational disorders' (like attachment disorders; see Wright et al., 2022 Wright et al., , 2023)). These findings have led a large consortium of researchers to suggest that personality and psychopathology reflect two sides of the same psychological coin--such that the content of (personality) dimensions defines all kinds of psychological functioning (and 'malfunctioning'), implying that it cannot define a specific "disorder of personality" (DeYoung & Krueger, 2025; Zavlis & Fonagy, 2024).

Interestingly, the same conclusion is reached when one considers the more recent construct of "personality" functioning (also known as criterion A in the DSM-5 AMPD; Sharp & Wall, 2021). Indeed, while this criterion was introduced to delineate "what is personality disorder from what is not" (Morey, 2019 (Morey, , p.1195)), it also came to be a transdiagnostic marker of all mental health problems (rather than as a unique marker of a putative personality disorder). To illustrate, consider first how the various facets of criterion A map across various domains of personality-psychopathology, with "identity" mapping on neuroticism (emotional functioning), "self-direction" on disinhibition (behavioural functioning), "intimacy" on detachment (interpersonal functioning) and "empathy" on antagonism (interpersonal functioning; see Thimm, 2025 for a metaanalysis). Consider moreover how factor analyses at both the cross-sectional and longitudinal levels have shown that these self-interpersonal aspects are "pure markers" of the general factor of psychopathology (suggesting how they are relevant for understanding all kinds of mental health problems invariantly; Kerber et al., 2024; Widiger et al., 2019). Finally, consider how several clinical researchers have indeed noted that these features define "what it means to be human" (Bender, 2019; Sharp & Wall, 2021), implying once again that they are relevant for understanding all psychological functioning (and thus malfunctioning) equally. Together, these findings suggest that "personality functioning" is equally relevant for all mental health diEiculties, leading some researchers to go as far as to say that the general ("p") factor of "psychopathology" may, in fact, be a general factor of "personality" (e.g., Bender, 2019; Tackett & Katz, 2026).

Finally, one last thing worth noting is that the same definitional diEiculties extend to virtually all other psychiatric diagnoses, not only the "personality disorder" diagnosis. To briefly illustrate, consider first how archetypal emotional, cognitive-perceptual, behavioural, and interpersonal symptoms are not confined to the diagnostic families after which they are named, but recur across the full range of psychopathologies (Kotov et al., 2017 (Kotov et al., , 2020 (Kotov et al., , 2021;; Krueger et al., 2021; Watson et al., 2022; Wright et al., 2022). Consider further how, when the individual symptoms of various mental disorders are factor-analysed, they fail to reveal coherent diagnostic entities with clear boundaries, but rather re-organise themselves into the well-known dimensions of personality, as outlined previously (that is, the dimensions of negative aEectivity, psychoticism, disinhibition, detachment, and antagonism; e.g., Forbes, Baillie, et al., 2025; Forbes et al., 2021 Forbes et al., , 2026;; Forbes, Watts, et al., 2025; Ringwald et al., 2023). Finally, consider how these dimensions of personality pathology are themselves so highly correlated such that people who experience one kind of mental health problem are more likely to experience others as well, typically shift between diEerent diagnoses of such problems, and transmit broad rather than diagnosis-specific liabilities to their children (Caspi et al., 2026a; Caspi et al., 2026b). Together, this evidence suggests that mental disorders are not discrete "natural" entities, but rather are heterogenous and shifting constellations of the same kinds of dimensions that make "personality" and its putative "pathology": namely, dimensions indicating ways of "thinking, feeling, behaving, and relating" (Widiger, 2011).

In the next section, we extend this line of reasoning even further by arguing how all "mental" disorders can be equally understood as "personality" disorders.

All mental disorder as personality disorder

Since its beginnings in DSM-III, scientific psychiatry has sought to define discrete diagnostic categories of mental disorder (Kendler et al., 2011). Decades of later research, however, have indicated that these categories are not only not discrete entities, but rather are so overlapping, so heterogenous, and so unstable that a single person can meet one diagnosis in thousands of diEerent ways and could also transition across diEerent diagnoses over their lifetime (Caspi et al., 2026a; Caspi et al., 2026b; Fried, 2026; Haslam et al., 2020; Kendell & Jablensky, 2003; Kotov et al., 2017 Kotov et al., , 2021)).

On that basis, a large consortium of researchers have proposed abandoning discrete categories altogether and focusing instead on a set of transdiagnostic dimensions that characterise all kinds of mental health conditions (e.g., Cicero et al., 2024; Conway et al., 2019a Conway et al., , 2023;; Kotov et al., 2017 Kotov et al., , 2021 Kotov et al., , 2022)). In this final discussion section, we argue that our model is consistent with this dimensional approach to psychopathology and can further clarify its implications for empirical inquiry by showing how all mental disorder should be studied as personality disorder: that is, as a set of diEiculties that involve dimensions of personality.

To make this case, we extend our model from its descriptive between-subjects formulation to a more mechanistic within-subjects formulation in seven key steps.

First, we re-emphasise what was also stated previously: namely, personality and psychopathology share the same content space. Specifically, both personality and psychopathology encompass emotional dimensions (indicating characteristic ways of feeling), behavioural dimensions (indicating characteristic ways of acting), cognitiveperceptual dimensions (indicating characteristic ways of thinking and perceiving), and interpersonal dimensions (indicating characteristic ways of relating; Conway et al., 2019; DeYoung & Krueger, 2018; Kotov et al., 2017 Kotov et al., , 2021 Kotov et al., , 2022;; Ringwald et al., 2023; Tackett & Katz, 2026). Moreover, all of these aspects are inherent to all mental disorders, with, for instance, "internalising disorders" expressing maladaptive ways of thinking (rumination), feeling (anhedonia), behaving (amotivation), and relating (social withdrawal) (Watson et al., 2022), "psychotic disorders" likewise expressing maladaptive ways of thinking (delusions), feeling (flat aEect), behaving (catatonia), and relating (interpersonal detachment) (Kotov et al., 2020), and "externalising disorders" expressing maladaptive ways of thinking (poor consideration of consequences), feeling (sensation-seeking), behaving (impulsivity), and relating (frequent conflicts with others) (Krueger et al., 2021). Together, these patterns suggest that personality and psychopathology are two sides of the same psychological coin, with personality outlining value-neutral ways of thinking, feeling, behaving, and relating, and (personality) psychopathology outlining the maladaptive expression of these ways of thinking, feeling, behaving, and relating.

In this sense, all mental disorder can be viewed as personality disorder as well, because it is represented by characteristic impairments that emerge from the same dimensions of "thinking, feeling, behaving, and relating" that constitute personality.

But if all mental disorders are configurations of characteristic impairments arising from the same dimensions of psychological experience, then psychiatric research should no longer organise itself around diagnostic categories (see Caspi et al., 2026a; Caspi et al., 2026b). Instead, it should study the individual dimensions (and their specific signs and symptoms) that cut across those categories, asking for each dimension at least three questions: namely, what is its content (i.e., the non-impairing vs. impairing outcomes via which it is expressed), its dynamics (i.e., the extent to which it is enduring versus adaptable), and the sources of those dynamics (i.e., the biological, environmental, and transactional processes that sustain or revise them).

The first question is already being addressed by dimensional research on psychopathology (most notably, the hierarchical taxonomy of psychopathology) and involves identifying the universe of characteristics that typify mental health and illness, including specific signs and symptoms (e.g., insomnia, anhedonia, etc), intermediate syndromes and subfactors (such as fear vs. distress), and broader spectra (such as internalising vs. externalising) and superspectra (i.e., "general psychopathology"; (Conway et al., 2019; Kotov et al., 2017 Kotov et al., , 2021 Kotov et al., , 2022)).

The second question involves understanding the dynamics of each of these dimensions of human experience, something that could be addressed using state-trait models (like the one we empirically used here) and state-space models (like the attractor-based formulation we developed in our discussion). Applying these models on various dimensions of human existence could establish the extent to which each of them is enduring (in the sense that they tend to return to a particular equilibrium or attractor state) versus adaptable (in the sense that they tend to depart from the equlibrium and be revised in response to environmental and biological influences) (Nowak et al., 2005).

The third question involves moving beyond this descriptive level of analysis and examining the sources of these dynamics over time. Existing genetically informed, longitudinal, and stress-generation designs already isolate biological (McAdams et al., 2023), environmental (Bühler et al., 2024), and person-driven (Liu et al., 2024) influences on psychological functioning, but they typically estimate these eEects at short-term intervals, making it diEicult to determine how such influences shape psychological dimensions over the long term. Our model could address this limitation by linking these influences directly to the temporal architecture of each dimension, distinguishing whether they contribute to enduring equilibria (parameter 𝐷), adaptable deviations (parameter 𝐴), or the retention of those deviations over time (parameter 𝑅).

Finally, the last question is how these dynamics should be mapped onto intervention.

To briefly illustrate, dimensions with low retention rate may benefit from low-intensity, preventive work, while that they are more revisable (McGorry & Mei, 2021). By contrast, dimensions with a high retention rate may require higher-intensity intervention to break the person-environment cycles that sustain them (Rnic et al., 2023). Likewise, dimensions with environmentally-set equilibria may require longer-term treatment to revise the deeply internalised patterns underlying severe problems, such as the mistrust that follows complex relational trauma (Fonagy et al., 2015). Finally, and conversely, dimensions with biologically-set equilibria may call for more acceptance, accommodation, and improved management given that their fixed points may be diEicult to directly alter (Pellicano et al., 2022). Together, these distinctions suggest that treatment should not target putative diagnostic categories, but the particular dynamics that sustain the person's specific diEiculties.

Taken together, these points suggest that all "mental disorders" should be studied as "personality disorders": not only because they exhibit the same characteristic stability and temporal dynamics, but more fundamentally because they are composed of the same dimensions of personality experience. Figure 4 summarises this research programme by depicting psychopathology not as a landscape of discrete disorders, but as a hierarchy of psychological dimensions, which may produce impairing or nonimpairing outcomes, exhibit enduring, rigidly retained, or readily adaptable dynamics, and be sustained or revised by biological, environmental, and transactional processes. Accordingly, rather than studying unstable and reified diagnostic categories as though each had a single course and cause, researchers may do better by identifying the broad dimensions and narrow symptoms that constitute the entire architecture of personality and psychopathology and establish for each, its temporal dynamics, the source of those dynamics, and how its possible impairments can be ameliorated in therapy.

Conclusion

To conclude, then, we have outlined a formal approach for studying personality retention versus revision. Applying this approach to 145 test-retest coeEicients spanning up to 18 years, we revealed that categorical personality disorders are primarily adaptable rather than durable--though their adaptable elements are only gradually revised over time. We then argued that neither personality stability nor content can define a unique "personality disorder" because both of these criteria define the broad architecture of all mental disorders. Together, these patterns suggest that psychiatric research should move beyond traditional diagnostic categories altogether and recognise that all mental disorders are best studied as personality disorders: that is, as disorders involving various dimensions and dynamics of personality experience.

By "characteristic", we mean any aspect of personality that is stable enough to be considered "characteristic" of the person. This includes both "personality traits" (which are more general and enduring) and characteristic adaptations (which are more specific and rigid, though could also be enduring at times; DeYoung, 2015).
