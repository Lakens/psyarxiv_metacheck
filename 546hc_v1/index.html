What we found

Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine, Daniel Lakens and Cristian Mesquida

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 128 papers with 246 instances of power statements, 203 were correctly detected (true positives), 22 were missed (false negatives) and 21 were incorrectly detected (false positives). Overall, among all instances flagged as power statements, 90.6% were correct (positive predictive value).

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download information from other preregistration sites, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Read more in the metacheck manual.

How Marginal Significance works

List all sentences that describe an effect as ‘marginally significant’.

The marginal module searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The list of terms is a subset of those listed in a blog post by Matthew Hankins. The module returns all sentences that match terms describing ‘marginally significant’ results.

Some of the terms identified might not be problematic in some contexts, and there are ways to describe ‘marginal significance’ that are not detected by the module.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Full text

Abstract

Cognitive pupillometry guidelines have been developed and validated almost exclusively on young, healthy participants recorded at high sampling rates, leaving open whether their preprocessing and statistical defaults transfer to harder recording contexts. Older adults with mild cognitive impairment (MCI) illustrate this problem directly: senile miosis, elevated blink rates, and pharmacologically induced pupillary constriction depart from the assumptions existing pipelines were built on. Lower-cost, lower-sampling-rate eye trackers, a translationally important target for scalable clinical screening, compound this further. Using a pooled sample of 67 older adults with MCI from two independent cohorts, recorded at 33 Hz during a visual oddball task, we adapt and empirically justify, decision by decision, a cognitive-pupillometry pipeline for this harder context, evaluating each decision against the well-established Target versus Distractor effect. Some literature defaults held up unchanged once tested directly against this dataset; others did not transfer; and others had no applicable default at all and were derived empirically. The Target versus Distractor effect was recovered consistently across every preprocessing alternative tested and across complementary statistical approaches, generalized additive and linear mixedeffects models and trial-level features, whose choice we show depends on whether the question concerns the timing, the average size, or specific components of the effect. This paper's contribution is not a new pipeline or a clinical marker, but a transparent, reproducible account of which standard assumptions and defaults hold in a harder recording context, which require empirical re-derivation, and which have no existing literature anchor at all, illustrated with accompanying code.

1. Introduction

Pupillometry, the measurement of pupil size to study cognitive and emotional processes, is a wellestablished tool in psychology and neuroscience (Beatty & Lucero-Wagoner, 2000; Mathôt, 2018), with applications spanning attention, working memory, arousal, mental effort, and emotion (Bradley et al., 2008; Joshi & Gold, 2020). Despite this widespread use, there is no single agreedupon methodology for designing, preprocessing, and analyzing cognitive pupillometry experiments, which limits comparability across studies and complicates replication and metaanalytic synthesis of findings. In response, comprehensive guidelines have been proposed, most notably by Kret and Sjak-Shie (2019) and Mathôt and Vilotijević (2023) for cognitive pupillometry, complemented by expert consensus standards for pupillography more broadly (Kelbsch et al., 2019), covering experimental design, preprocessing, and statistical analysis, and substantially improving transparency and reproducibility in the field. These guidelines, however, were largely developed and validated on young, cognitively healthy participants tested under controlled laboratory conditions, and whether the same preprocessing defaults generalize to noisier, less well-behaved signals remains untested. Older adults with cognitive decline, such as those with Mild Cognitive Impairment (MCI), illustrate this problem directly. Aging alone is associated with a well-documented, luminance-dependent reduction in pupil diameter, called senile miosis (Kelbsch et al., 2019; Winn et al., 1994). This brings baseline pupil sizes in this population closer to the 2 mm threshold conventionally used as the lower bound of the "normal" pupillometric range. This 2 mm threshold is not a hard physiological floor, particularly in MCI. Pharmacological treatments common in this population can reduce pupil size further: acetylcholinesterase inhibitors, the standard pharmacological treatment for MCI and Alzheimer's disease, increase cholinergic tone at the iris sphincter (Nuzzi et al., 2020), and opioids, frequent in pain management in this age group, can constrict the pupil well below 2 mm (Larson, 2008). MCI further adds elevated spontaneous blink rates (D'Antonio et al., 2021; Ladas et al., 2014), and aging a higher prevalence of dry eye (Sharma & Hindman, 2014), which itself shortens the interval between blinks (Johnston et al., 2013); both increase missing or unreliable samples, so preprocessing defaults developed on young, healthy samples cannot simply be assumed to transfer to this population.

These physiological departures from the standard pupillometric profile are compounded by a second, independent source of difficulty: the recording technology itself.

In recent years, interest in pupillometry and eye tracking as tools for characterizing cognitive decline in MCI and dementia has grown substantially, driven by an explicit translational goal: moving these measures out of specialized laboratories and into scalable, accessible clinical screening tools (Verón et al., 2026; Wolf et al., 2023). This goal runs into a concrete practical obstacle. The infrared eye trackers most commonly used in research to develop and validate cognitive pupillometry pipelines, such as EyeLink systems or the Tobii Pro Fusion, are expensive and require dedicated laboratory infrastructure, which by itself excludes most clinical settings from adopting them at scale. Low-cost eye trackers, such as those in Tobii's entry-level range, are considerably more feasible for broad clinical deployment (Cuve et al., 2022; Romeo et al., 2024), but do not provide research-grade timing or accuracy: the device used here samples at 33 Hz with an accuracy of approximately 0.65° (Housholder et al., 2022), against EyeLink's typical 1000 Hz sampling and 0.25-0.50° accuracy. This raises an important methodological question. Do standard preprocessing and analysis pipelines, developed and validated almost exclusively at higher sampling rates, remain valid once applied to the coarser, noisier signal that lower-frequency, lower-accuracy recording produces? Because cognitively driven pupil dynamics are slow, with most of their power below approximately 4 Hz (Mathôt & Vilotijević, 2023), a lower sampling rate need not by itself prevent the response from being captured; the open question is rather whether preprocessing parameters defined in samples or tuned on high-frequency recordings, such as artifact-edge windows, gap limits, and smoothing kernels, transfer to it. Prior work using the same attention paradigm adopted here reported an altered target-specific pupil response in MCI and Alzheimer's disease patients compared to healthy controls, peaking progressively later from controls to MCI to Alzheimer's disease and reaching its largest amplitude in Alzheimer's disease (Jiménez et al., 2021). Similar alterations have been reported with other paradigms applied to this population, such as working memory span tasks (El Haj et al., 2022; Granholm et al., 2017). Like most of the field, these studies did not report or justify their underlying preprocessing decisions in detail.

The present study addresses both gaps in this population and recording context: the lack of a standardized, comparable pupillometry methodology, and the lack of detailed, empirically justified preprocessing decisions in prior pupillometry work with older adults with MCI. Using a pooled sample of 67 older adults with MCI from two independent cohorts, we recorded pupil responses with the same low-cost eye tracker described above during a visual attention task.

Because such trackers are the practical route toward scalable clinical screening, their output must be held to the same preprocessing rigor as research-grade systems. We therefore adapt and empirically justify, decision by decision, a cognitive pupillometry pipeline for this population and recording context, extending the same logic from preprocessing to the statistical models applied to the resulting signal, since the simplifying assumptions these models make about the form of the effect over time are themselves untested here. Existing guidelines, principally Kret and Sjak-Shie (2019) and Mathôt and Vilotijević (2023), provide the starting point: where no applicable default exists, we derive and justify thresholds directly from this dataset, reporting transparently which decisions are literature-anchored and which are dataset-specific. Our aim is methodological rather than diagnostic, asking whether the expected pupil responses can be reliably recovered within an MCI population once every preprocessing and analytic decision is made explicit and justified.

2. Methods

Data reported here were drawn from two studies conducted by our research group. The first (Martínez-Flores et al., 2026b , 2026c) was a cross-sectional study examining the association between the task-evoked pupil response and cerebrospinal fluid biomarkers of Alzheimer's disease pathology, approved by the Ethics Committees of the University of Barcelona (HCB/2021/0668) and Hospital del Mar. The second (Martínez-Flores et al., 2026a) was a longitudinal study examining the effects of an oculomotor training intervention on oculomotor metrics, including the task-evoked pupil response, and plasma biomarkers of neurodegeneration, approved by the Ethics Committee of Hospital Universitari de Bellvitge (PR257/24) and prospectively registered at ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT06963879). Both studies were conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki, and written informed consent was obtained from all participants, directly or through a legally authorized representative, prior to enrollment.

2.1 Participants

The pooled sample comprised 67 older adults with a clinical diagnosis of MCI, drawn from the two cohorts described above, collected at different points in time using the same recording device and paradigm.

The first cohort (n = 38, cross-sectional study) was recruited at two clinics in Barcelona, Spain (Hospital del Mar and Hospital Clínic de Barcelona) (age 72.9 ± 5.83 years; 12 men [31.6%], 26 women [68.4%]; MMSE 22.4 ± 4.04). Other biomarker data beyond pupil size were also collected in this cohort; these are outside the scope of the present study and are reported elsewhere (Martínez-Flores et al., 2026b , 2026c).

The second cohort (n = 29, longitudinal study) was recruited from two residential care facilities under shared administration (Barcelona, Spain). Of the 30 individuals initially screened, one was excluded prior to baseline assessment due to a medical condition unrelated to the study, yielding a final sample of 29 participants (age 77.5 ± 8.59 years; 13 men [44.8%], 16 women [55.2%]; MoCA 16.2 ± 5.45). Only the baseline, pre-intervention data from this cohort are used here; the intervention, its outcomes, and the associated plasma biomarker data are outside the scope of the present methodological work and are reported elsewhere (Martínez-Flores et al., 2026a).

Across both cohorts, all participants had received a clinical MCI diagnosis from their treating physicians, in accordance with core clinical criteria (Albert et al., 2011; Petersen, 2004).

Eligibility additionally required participants to be aged 65 years or older, to have sufficient visual function (corrected or uncorrected) to discriminate on-screen stimuli, and to be able to provide informed consent. Participants were excluded for severe cognitive impairment as judged by the treating physician, a neurological condition resulting in cognitive deficits other than MCI, a major psychiatric diagnosis, structural brain lesions judged to be a primary contributor to cognitive deficits, ophthalmological pathology precluding reliable task performance, or insufficient proficiency in Spanish.

The two cohorts used different cognitive screening instruments (MMSE and MoCA, respectively) and were collected under separate protocols without a shared diagnostic staging framework. Because of this, and because no healthy control group was collected alongside either cohort, the two samples were pooled exclusively for the methodological validation described here, and no clinical group comparison is attempted.

2.2 Paradigm

Both cohorts completed the same visual attention task (Figure 1), following the protocol described by Jiménez et al. (2021). Visual stimuli were presented using the BGaze system (Braingaze SL, Mataró, Spain). Testing took place in a dimly lit room with curtains drawn and overhead lights off, so that the display screen was the dominant source of ambient illumination throughout each session. The same recording equipment was used across both cohorts. Participants were seated approximately 50 cm from the display screen, with a remote binocular eye tracker (Tobii 5L, Tobii Technology AB, Sweden) positioned below it; a chinrest was used throughout to minimize head movement, and corrective lenses were permitted. Binocular eye position and pupil diameter were recorded continuously at 33 Hz. The eye tracker was calibrated using a standard five-point binocular procedure before each session.

The task consisted of 120 trials presented in pseudorandom order. Each trial began with a 2000 ms grey mask screen, followed by a centrally presented stimulus displayed for 2000 ms: an 11-character string of randomly selected upper-or lower-case alphanumeric characters, constructed to avoid meaningful words or acronyms (Figure 1). Stimuli were identical in all physical properties except color: 80% were presented in blue font (distractors) and 20% in red font (targets), with the two colors isoluminant by design, following the Hillyard principle of holding low-level stimulus properties constant between conditions so that only the cognitive state of interest varies (Mathôt & Vilotijević, 2023). Participants were instructed to fixate the screen and press a response button only upon detecting a red string; the experimenter repeated this instruction verbally between trials, when necessary, without interrupting the recording window. Total task duration was approximately six minutes per session.

Throughout this paper, each preprocessing and analysis decision is evaluated against the within-subject Target-versus-Distractor contrast, a well-established oddball effect that serves as a known benchmark: an appropriate choice should preserve it. The same procedures apply equally to between-group comparisons, the more common question in this population, which the present data do not support because the two cohorts were not harmonized (Section 5.1). This question has, however, been addressed with the same approach within each cohort, in relation to biomarker levels and dementia risk (Martínez-Flores et al., 2026a , 2026b , 2026c). 3. Preprocessing

We use the term preprocessing to refer to every step that transforms the raw, sample-bysample pupil-size recording given by the eye tracking SDK software into a format suitable for statistical analysis. This included identifying and excluding invalid samples and trials, reconstructing missing samples, correcting for pre-stimulus fluctuations in pupil size, and smoothing. As in Mathôt and Vilotijević (2023), the order of these steps is not arbitrary, and we present them here broadly in the order in which they were applied.

General logic and terminology of pupil-size preprocessing are covered in detail elsewhere (Kret & Sjak-Shie, 2019; Mathôt & Vilotijević, 2023). For each step we focus specifically on whether, and why, the recording context of the present study, older adults with MCI, recorded at 33 Hz, required a departure from existing recommendations, and we report the empirical evidence behind each such departure. Where no departure was necessary, we say so explicitly and adopt the existing recommendation as is.

3.1 Data validity screening

3.1.1 Physiologically plausible pupil range

Cognitive-pupillometry pipelines typically define a physiologically plausible range for raw pupil diameter, since pupil size varies between roughly 2 and 8 mm in diameter under normal conditions (Mathôt & Vilotijević, 2023). This convention, however, was not developed with older or clinical populations in mind, nor validated across the range of consumer-grade devices increasingly used in clinical eye-tracking contexts; absolute pupil-diameter values are not necessarily directly comparable across devices, since device-specific recording and computation methods can shift measurement precision (Romeo et al., 2024). Population-specific physiology compounds this further: as outlined in the Introduction, senile miosis (Guillon et al., 2016; Winn et al., 1994) and medications common in this population, such as acetylcholinesterase inhibitors (Nuzzi et al., 2020) and opioids (Larson, 2008), can bring genuine pupil sizes close to or below the conventional 2 mm bound.

For this reason, we visually inspected the distribution of raw pupil diameters in our own data and found a substantial concentration of samples in the lower tail: 18.9% of all valid samples fell between 1.5 and 2 mm, against only 2.3% below 1.5 mm and 0.0% above 8 mm (Figure 2A-B). Based on this observation, we set the lower bound at 1.5 mm for this population. We emphasize that this specific value is dataset-specific, and not a general lower bound to be adopted elsewhere.

To further test whether this concentration reflects genuine physiology rather than a tracking artifact, we ran two additional checks: one evaluating interocular concordance directly, the other evaluating whether the decision itself changes the effect this paper reports. The role of pharmacological treatment specifically is addressed in Section 5.1: in our primary analyses of these cohorts, cholinergic/anticholinergic medication status showed no relevant effect on the pupil response.

If the 1.5-2 mm band reflected corneal-reflection tracking noise rather than true measurement, interocular agreement should be worse in this band than in an equally narrow window elsewhere in the plausible range, since genuine miosis is consensual across the two eyes while tracking noise need not be. We compared interocular agreement (Bland-Altman) for samples in the 1.5-2.0 mm range (n = 97,653) and samples in the 2.0-2.5 mm range (n = 117,410; Figure 2C). Bias and limits of agreement were closely comparable between the two ranges (1.5-2.0 mm: bias = 0.040 mm, 95% LOA = [-0.672, 0.753] mm; 2.0-2.5 mm: bias = 0.075 mm, 95% LOA = [-0.666, 0.815] mm), with the 1.5-2.0 mm range showing marginally tighter, not wider, agreement. In other words, there is no excess interocular disagreement in the 1.5-2 mm range.

More directly, we tested whether this decision changes the reported effect itself: we re-ran the complete exclusion cascade (minimum coverage, baseline z-score, boundary edge, max gap; see the next sections) using a minimum pupil diameter of 2.0 mm, the conventional default, in place of the production value, and compared the resulting Target time-course mean in the same 1000-1500 ms window used elsewhere in this pipeline (Figure 2D).

Our chosen lower bound of 1.5 mm yielded a Target window-mean of 0.065 mm (n = 67 participants, BCa 95% CI = [0.044, 0.085]), and conventional lower bound of 2.0 mm yielded 0.056 mm (n = 59, BCa 95% CI = [0.036, 0.078]); a paired permutation test on the 59 participants with data under both floors found no significant difference (mean diff = 0.008 mm, p = .113).

Beyond leaving the reported effect unchanged, the lower bound of 1.5 mm retained eight additional participants who would otherwise have been dropped for insufficient valid data. Together, these two checks indicate that the 1.5-2 mm band is not distinguishable from the surrounding range on interocular agreement, and that adopting it in place of the conventional 2 mm default does not alter the effect this paper reports, while retaining more usable data. 3.1.2 Minimum per-trial data coverage

Beyond the plausible range of individual samples, a trial must retain enough valid data overall, in at least one eye, before it can be used at all. This problem is more consequential in this population than in the samples on which most pupillometry pipelines were developed: elevated spontaneous blink rate has been documented in MCI relative to healthy older adults (D'Antonio et al., 2021; Ladas et al., 2014), so any threshold governing tolerable missing data removes a larger share of trials here than it would in a young, healthy sample. Neither Mathôt and Vilotijević (2023) nor Kret and Sjak-Shie (2019) prescribe a specific proportion of missing data per trial for this purpose: the former rely on blink reconstruction together with a baseline-level z-score criterion (Section 3.3), with no separate missing-datapercentage rule, and the latter focus on identifying and removing invalid samples rather than setting a trial-level percentage cutoff. Downstream studies following their general frameworks have adopted differing values, ranging from 20% to 50% missing data tolerated per trial, without apparent consensus (Fan et al., 2023; Pitem & Mama, 2025; van Rij et al., 2019), reflecting the absence of a field standard rather than a converged recommendation.

Given this absence of a standard, we treated the choice as an empirical question: rather than adopting any single published value, we tested four candidates spanning the full range in use (50%, 40%, 30%, and 20% missing data tolerated), recomputing the full exclusion cascade under each, since lowering the coverage requirement also changes the population entering the per-participant baseline z-score calculation, not only the final trial count. The Target mean level in the 1000-1500 ms window was nearly identical across all four candidates (Figure 3B). Because each candidate reduces to a single value per participant, and pupil-size measures are known to be sensitive to outliers (Steinhauer et al., 2022), we compared the four candidates pairwise using sign-flip permutation tests rather than a parametric alternative. Bias-corrected and accelerated (BCa) bootstrap 95% confidence intervals for the four candidates overlapped substantially (Figure 3B), and none of the six pairwise permutation comparisons was significant, confirming no systematic bias from the choice of threshold within this range. Extending the range to 10-80% (Supplementary Figure S1) showed that only the most stringent value, 10%, which retained less than half of all trials (49.1%), yielded a Target mean level distinguishable from the others (0.073 mm; all pairwise p ≤ .035), whereas 60-80% remained indistinguishable from the literatureanchored range (all p ≥ .28). Given the absence of any detectable difference, a stricter threshold might seem preferable on the grounds that it is more conservative; however, we retained the most permissive value (50%) instead, for a reason grounded in the population rather than in the absence of a measured effect alone. Elevated blink rate in MCI (D'Antonio et al., 2021; Ladas et al., 2014) is a general physiological trait of the participant, largely independent of trial content, rather than a signal-dependent artifact; the stability of the resulting time-course across this entire range is consistent with, though not direct proof of, missingness in this population that is unrelated to the measured response, at least within the range tested. Under these conditions, retaining trials with more missing data does not bias the estimate; it only affects its precision, since data missing completely at random reduce statistical power without distorting the underlying parameter (Little et al., 2012).

3.1.3 Interocular coverage balance

Even when a trial retains enough valid data, the two eyes may not be tracked equally well.

Age-related dry eye (see Introduction), together with eyelash occlusion and gaze-dependent measurement error in corneal-reflection eye trackers (Brisson et al., 2013), can leave one eye substantially better tracked than the other within a given trial. Averaging both eyes indiscriminately under these conditions introduces noise from the poorer-quality eye rather than genuine binocular signal.

To quantify this imbalance, we computed, for each trial, the coverage of each eye: the percentage of samples in the post-stimulus window that were flagged as valid by the eye tracker, fell within the screen, and lay within the plausible pupil range defined in Section 3.1.1. We then expressed the two as a ratio, the coverage of the worse-tracked eye divided by that of the bettertracked eye. A ratio of 1 means both eyes were tracked equally well; a ratio near 0 means one eye contributed almost no valid data. When the ratio fell below a given threshold, only the better eye's samples were used; otherwise both eyes were averaged, with samples valid in only one eye taken from that eye alone.

Using the better-covered eye when the other has excessive missing data is common practice, but no generally adopted criterion for when to do so exists. Rather than fixing a value a priori, we plotted, for each candidate threshold, the percentage of trials (among those meeting the minimum coverage criterion, Section 3.1.2) that would be switched to single-eye data, and looked for a region where small changes in the threshold produced negligible changes in this percentage (Figure 3C). This stable region spanned ratios of approximately 0.25-0.40, within which about 2.0% of trials were switched, a small percentage, consistent in spirit with Mathôt and Vilotijević's (2023) ~5% guideline for artifact checks. We selected 0.30 within this region: both eyes were averaged only if the worse eye covered at least 30% of what the better eye covered.

3.1.4 Maximum tolerable gap length

A closely related decision concerns the maximum single gap within a trial that can reasonably be reconstructed through interpolation rather than left as unreliable. Reconstruction algorithms in this literature typically impose an upper limit on gap length, on the grounds that interpolation is only meaningful over periods short enough for pupil size to remain predictable. A gap duration of 500 ms is the threshold conventionally used in the literature to define the maximum plausible duration of a single blink (Steinhauer et al., 2022); Mathôt and Vilotijević's (2023) blinkreconstruct() algorithm, for example, does not attempt to interpolate gaps longer than 500 ms, whereas Kret and Sjak-Shie (2019) did not interpolate over gaps longer than 250 ms in their worked example, so the literature offers two reference points rather than one. Although there is no evidence that blinks last longer in this population, it remains good practice to empirically examine blink-gap duration in the data to characterize the range of gaps likely to be encountered across trials.

To check whether this value was appropriate for our data, we applied a marginal-dropflattening algorithm to the distribution of maximum within-trial gap length, identifying the point at which the marginal rate of trial exclusion, as a function of candidate gap threshold, stabilizes, that is, where the slope of the exclusion curve approaches zero (the elbow, or knee, point; Figure 3D). This point corresponded to 440 ms in our data, a difference of 0.81 percentage points in trials excluded relative to Mathôt and Vilotijević's (2023) 500 ms default. As with the coverage threshold above, we retained the more permissive value: the negligible difference between the two thresholds, together with the interpolation validation in Section 3.2, where linear reconstruction remained accurate for gaps up to 500 ms, the longest tested, indicates that 500 ms remains an appropriate ceiling for this dataset's gap-length distribution. 3.1.5 Stimulus balance of exclusions

Because the oddball paradigm used here assigns unequal saliency to Target and Distractor stimuli, an asymmetry that has no counterpart in Mathôt and Vilotijević's (2023) original design, the exclusion and reconstruction criteria described above could in principle be applied unevenly across conditions, for instance if one condition were associated with systematically more blinking or poorer tracking than the other. Mathôt and Vilotijević (2023) explicitly recommend checking for this: blink rate, or a proxy for it, should not differ systematically between conditions, and if it does, this difference should at minimum be transparently reported so that readers can judge whether it threatens the interpretation of the results. We used the proportion of valid samples per eye and the interocular coverage ratio as such a proxy, and compared Target and Distractor trials using linear mixed-effects models with random intercepts and slopes by participant. Coverage was higher on Target trials for both the left eye (β = 4.54, SE = 0.68, p < .001) and the right eye (β = 4.35, SE = 0.66, p < .001), and the interocular coverage ratio was also higher on Target trials (β = 0.024, SE = 0.009, p = .011; this model's random-effects structure was singular, so this estimate should be interpreted with caution). In this paradigm, this asymmetry is expected rather than problematic: Target trials are the infrequent, task-relevant oddball events by design, and are accordingly associated with fewer blinks and less gaze instability than the frequent Distractor trials, consistent with oculomotor inhibition around salient stimuli (Valsecchi & Turatto, 2009).

We report this check here, following Mathôt and Vilotijević's (2023) recommendation, rather than treating the asymmetry itself as evidence of a problem.

3.2 Interpolation method: linear vs. cubic-spline

Once a trial's maximum tolerable gap has been established (Section 3.1.4), any gap that survives must be reconstructed before the signal can be baseline-corrected and averaged. The pupillometry preprocessing literature generally recommends one of two interpolation methods for this purpose: linear interpolation, which draws a straight line between the two valid samples bounding a gap, or cubic-spline interpolation, which fits a smooth curve using additional anchor points on either side. Mathôt and Vilotijević's (2023) blinkreconstruct() algorithm, for example, is not neutral between the two: it attempts cubic-spline interpolation first, using two additional anchor points on either side of the gap, and falls back to a straight line only when those additional points are unavailable. Their stated rationale is that a smooth curve "more closely resembles natural pupil-size changes" than a straight line. This preference, however, rests on the plausibility of a smooth reconstruction rather than on validation against ground truth, and was developed on highfrequency recordings from young, healthy samples, where gaps are typically brief and anchor points are drawn from a comparatively clean signal. Elevated spontaneous blink rate in MCI (D' Antonio et al., 2021; Ladas et al., 2014) means gaps are both more frequent and, on average, longer in this population, giving the choice of reconstruction method more opportunity to matter, and making the anchor points cubic-spline interpolation depends on more likely to themselves sit in a noisier stretch of signal.

To test whether Mathôt and Vilotijević's (2023) recommendation was appropriate for our data, we compared the two methods in two complementary ways, implementing the same local, four-point cubic-spline procedure their algorithm itself uses (two valid samples immediately before the gap, two immediately after), rather than a spline fit across the whole trial, so that the comparison reflects the actual reconstruction method being evaluated. First, we compared the two methods directly against each other. For each trial, we identified the longest stretch of missing data and took the sample at its temporal midpoint, the point furthest from any valid data and therefore where the two reconstructions are free to diverge the most. We then compared the value reconstructed at that sample by linear and by cubic-spline interpolation, yielding one pair of values per trial. This comparison was run on the baseline-corrected but unsmoothed signal, to isolate the interpolation method's own contribution from the separately justified smoothing step (Section 3.4).

Across all accepted trials, the two methods correlated strongly (r = .92) but showed a Bland-Altman bias of 0.053 mm and 95% limits of agreement of [-0.39, 0.49] mm at the point of maximal divergence (Figure 4A-B), wide relative to the effect under study, although this represents a worst case at the single-sample level rather than its impact on the averaged response. Restricting the comparison to trials with a longer gap (>100 ms), where reconstruction is least constrained by nearby valid samples, widened this disagreement further (bias = 0.098 mm, 95% LOA = [-0.49, 0.69] mm); at this gap length, 76.6% of trials showed a disagreement between methods exceeding 0.05 mm, meaning divergence between the two interpolation methods was common whenever a gap was not brief.

A direct disagreement between two methods, however, only shows that they differ, not which one is more accurate. To answer that question, we validated both methods against known ground truth using synthetic gap injection: gaps of realistic length, sampled from this dataset's own distribution of real gap lengths, were artificially introduced into trials with otherwise complete raw signal, then reconstructed via both methods and compared against the original, unblanked signal at the gap's midpoint (resampled onto the 33 Hz analysis grid). Across 3,000 synthetic gaps, linear interpolation was closer to the true value in 70.4% of cases overall (Wilcoxon test on absolute error, p < .001), and its root-mean-square error (RMSE) against ground truth remained essentially flat regardless of gap length (approximately 0.05-0.06 mm throughout). Cubic-spline reconstruction, by contrast, degraded consistently as gap length increased, from an RMSE of 0.057 mm for gaps under 100 ms to 0.171 mm for gaps between 300 and 500 ms, roughly a three-fold increase, and linear interpolation was closer to the true value in every gap-length stratum tested (65.4% to 77.7% of draws; Figure 4C). This pattern is consistent with the overshoot cubic-spline interpolation is known to produce when its anchor points are drawn from noisy or irregularly sampled data, a documented limitation of cubic-spline reconstruction in other biological signals (Morelli et al., 2019).

Bias alone understates this difference: cubic-spline reconstruction's errors are large but partly symmetric, canceling on average, which is why RMSE, not bias, is the appropriate criterion for judging reconstruction quality here. On this ground-truth basis, linear interpolation is not merely a more conservative default; for this dataset's gap-length distribution, it is the more accurate method, and we retained it in production, departing from Mathôt and Vilotijević's (2023) own default preference for cubic-spline interpolation, and in line with consensus pupillography standards, which recommend linear interpolation between valid samples for short-duration artifacts (Kelbsch et al., 2019). This disadvantage of cubic-spline reconstruction demonstrated here grows specifically with gap length, the exact condition under which populations with elevated blink rates, such as the one studied here, most often find themselves, so a method validated on one dataset's gap-length distribution may not generalize to another. Whether the same advantage holds for high-frequency recordings in healthy samples remains to be tested; the synthetic gap-injection procedure used here requires only trials with near-complete signal and can be applied to any dataset. 3.3 Baseline window and reliability

The baseline period was defined as the last 7 samples (212 ms at 33 Hz) of the preceding mask screen, immediately before stimulus onset. Mathôt and Vilotijević (2023) recommend keeping the baseline period short, both because a short window is less likely to be contaminated by blinks or artifacts, and because, when a baseline period coincides with stimulus onset, its duration must stay below 200 ms to avoid capturing the earliest phase of the pupil light response.

Because our baseline window falls entirely within the preceding mask and therefore fully precedes stimulus onset, it is not subject to this second constraint; its short duration follows the same general logic of minimizing artifact contamination. Because mask and stimulus durations were fixed, anticipatory dilation may have contributed to the baseline; since participants could anticipate when, but not whether, a Target would appear, any such effect would apply to both conditions alike. No evidence in the literature suggests that a different baseline duration is better suited to older adults with MCI, so we retained this minimal, literature-anchored window without empirical re-derivation.

Using this baseline period requires two further decisions: how to summarize the baseline window into a single value per trial, and how to identify and exclude trials with an anomalous baseline. Both decisions conventionally rely on the mean and standard deviation: the mean as the summary value, and deviation from the participant's mean in units of standard deviation as the exclusion rule, with Mathôt and Vilotijević (2023) recommending the exclusion of trials with |z| > 2. Both statistics, however, are well known to be sensitive to the presence of outliers (Leys et al., 2013), and this sensitivity is plausibly more consequential in older adults with cognitive impairment, where fatigue, attentional lapses, or difficulty sustaining fixation can produce a smaller number of unusually large deviations that pull the mean and inflate the standard deviation.

Rather than assuming this is or is not a problem in a given dataset, we recommend that researchers treat it as an empirical question: compute both the mean-based and a robust, median-based alternative, and check whether the two converge.

We used the mean-based z-score as our main criterion, both because it is the criterion recommended by Mathôt and Vilotijević (2023) and because it is more directly interpretable. This criterion excluded 4.57% of trials (Figure 5A), with the excluded trials distributed fairly evenly across participants rather than concentrated in a few (Figure 5B). To check whether this choice was masking an outlier problem, we compared it directly against a robust alternative: the median and the scaled median absolute deviation (MAD × 1.4826), with exclusion at 2.5 MAD from the median (Leys et al., 2013). For each trial, we computed its baseline z-score within participant under both definitions and compared the two across all trials (n = 7,000). The two scores correlated at r = .957, with a Bland-Altman bias of -0.023 and 95% limits of agreement of [-0.75, 0.70].

More directly relevant to the exclusion decision, the two criteria agreed on 97.7% of trials (Cohen's κ = .69): 192 trials (2.7%) were excluded by both, 128 (1.8%) by the mean-based criterion only, and 34 (0.5%) by the MAD-based criterion only. The mean-based criterion was therefore the more conservative of the two in this dataset, and the robust alternative flagged very few trials that it retained. We retained the mean-based z-score as the production criterion accordingly, since it is the one recommended by Mathôt and Vilotijević (2023) and its behavior here does not indicate that a more complex, robust alternative is necessary. As a further, complementary check, we computed each trial's baseline both as the mean and as the median of the samples in the baseline window, and compared the two values trial by trial across all trials (n = 7,000). Pooling across participants is appropriate here, since both values summarize the same samples of the same trial.

The two agreed closely on average (r = .9985; Bland-Altman bias = -0.0015 mm), although individual trials could differ by up to ±0.07 mm (95% limits of agreement [-0.072, 0.069] mm; Figure 5C), with 7.0% of trials differing by more than 0.05 mm. Because these trial-level differences showed no systematic direction, they canceled out on averaging: the resulting Target time-courses were virtually indistinguishable (Target peak amplitude 0.0697 vs. 0.0696 mm, both at 1394 ms; Figure 5D). Together, this indicates that outliers in the baseline window are not distorting our results in this dataset, and that the simpler, mean-based approach is adequate here.

3.4 Smoothing

Once the signal has been reconstructed, a final smoothing step is standard practice in psychophysiological pupillometry (Steinhauer et al., 2022) to reduce residual high-frequency noise before averaging. The appropriate smoothing method depends on the sampling rate of the recording and on which component of the signal is of interest: filtering that is appropriate for isolating a fast, high-frequency signal is not necessarily appropriate for a slow one, and vice versa.

Pupil dilation itself is a slow physiological response, with cognitively driven fluctuations typically confined below approximately 4 Hz (Mathôt & Vilotijević, 2023); for this reason, low-pass filters, including Gaussian kernels such as the one used here, are the type of filter generally applied in cognitive pupillometry rather than a method designed to preserve faster signal components.

There is no consensus, however, on which specific low-pass method to use. Mathôt and Vilotijević (2023) do not apply a smoothing step in their own pipeline at all, relying on reconstruction alone. Kret and Sjak-Shie (2019), by contrast, recommend a Butterworth low-pass filter at 4 Hz, matching the frequency range noted above. We used a Gaussian kernel (σ = 2 samples) instead, a choice made for consistency with prior work from our group rather than because it is the convention in either reference pipeline; we disclose this openly rather than presenting it as a literature-derived default. This is a decision with as little external anchor as the criteria discussed in the preceding sections, and, as with those criteria, we treated its adequacy as an empirical question rather than an assumption. We compared our Gaussian filter against four alternatives spanning the range in common use, all low-pass in character given the slow signal noted above: no smoothing at all (NS), a 5sample moving average (MV), a Savitzky-Golay filter (SG; 7-sample window, third-order polynomial), and Kret and Sjak-Shie's (2019) recommended 4 Hz Butterworth low-pass filter (BL-P); our Gaussian filter is labeled GS* in Figure 6. Following the same participant-level logic used for the coverage threshold (Section 3.1.2), each of four metrics was reduced to one value per participant (n = 67): the Target mean level and the Target-Distractor contrast in the 1000-1500 ms window, the latency of the Target peak, and within-participant residual noise. Each alternative was compared against our filter with a paired sign-flip permutation test, and summarized with BCa bootstrap 95% confidence intervals across participants.

Peak timing did not differ from our filter in any comparison (1214-1256 ms across methods; all p > .48; Figure 6C). Target mean level (0.0647-0.0654 mm) and Target-Distractor contrast (0.108-0.109 mm) were statistically distinguishable in every comparison (all p < .001; Figure 6A-B), but the differences were minute: at most 0.0008 mm, about 1% of the response, and a small fraction of each method's confidence interval (approximately ±0.02 mm). These differences are detectable only because the comparison is paired: a given filter shifts every participant's curve in the same direction by nearly the same amount. Their direction is also expected: the Gaussian kernel, the strongest of the filters tested, slightly flattens the peak of the response, which falls within this window, whereas the peak-preserving Savitzky-Golay filter was indistinguishable in magnitude from no smoothing. We therefore regard these differences as reliable but practically negligible.

Within-participant residual noise was where the methods genuinely differed: our Gaussian filter showed the lowest noise of the five (0.116 mm, against 0.119-0.132 mm), significantly lower than every alternative, including Kret and Sjak-Shie's (2019) recommended Butterworth default (all p < .001; Figure 6D). Together, these results indicate that our Gaussian filter preserves the timing of the response, changes its amplitude and contrast by about 1% at most, and consistently reduces residual noise. 3.5 Gaze Position

Mathôt and Vilotijević (2023) recommend checking whether any measured covariate differs systematically between experimental conditions, since gaze position specifically can introduce a confound distinct from the blink-rate proxy addressed in Section 3.1.5: pupil foreshortening error (PFE), the systematic underestimation of pupil size as the eye rotates away from the camera, which follows an approximately cosine-of-angle relationship with gaze eccentricity (Hayes & Petrov, 2016). This concern reflects a broader, well-documented class of measurement artifacts in pupil-corneal-reflection (P-CR) video-based eye tracking, where pupil size and gaze position estimates are not fully independent (Hooge et al., 2019; Solé Puig et al., 2021). Such artifacts are known to shrink with viewing distance and become negligible beyond approximately 50 cm (Jaschinski, 2016), the distance used in the present study (Section 2.2). If Target and Distractor trials differed systematically in gaze position, any resulting difference in measured pupil size could reflect this optical artifact rather than a genuine cognitive response.

We tracked horizontal, vertical, and total gaze eccentricity (degrees of visual angle from screen center, averaged across the two eyes) across the trial time-course for each condition (Figure 7A-C), and separately summarized each trial's average eccentricity to compare conditions using linear mixed-effects models with random intercepts and slopes by participant. Target trials showed lower average eccentricity than Distractor trials on the vertical (β = -0.078°, SE = 0.022, p < .001) and total (β = -0.108°, SE = 0.031, p < .001) measures, with a similar but non-significant trend on the horizontal measure (β = -0.070°, SE = 0.038, p = .064): fixation was measurably tighter during Target trials than during Distractor trials. Tighter fixation around rare, task-relevant oddball stimuli relative to frequent standards is a well-documented oculomotor phenomenon, oculomotor inhibition (Valsecchi & Turatto, 2009).

The relevant question for the pupil analysis is a different one: could this small, expected difference in gaze position itself be responsible, through pupil foreshortening error, for part or all of the Target-Distractor pupil dilation difference? The magnitude of the gaze difference argues against this: a commonly cited safe zone for negligible pupil foreshortening error spans approximately ±8° horizontal and ±6° vertical, within which pupil size alteration remains below 5% (Hayes & Petrov, 2016). Both conditions' average eccentricities (2.24-2.66°) and, more importantly, the difference between them (0.06-0.11°) sit well within this zone. We report this confound check here, following Mathôt and Vilotijević's (2023) recommendation, rather than treating the difference as grounds for excluding trials or correcting the pupil signal. 3.6 Grand-average response and a priori analysis windows

Every decision described in the preceding five sections culminates in a single check: whether the resulting grand-average pupil response actually recovers the oddball effect. We computed the grand-average, baseline-corrected pupil response separately for Target and Distractor trials, using the same two-level hierarchical averaging (trial to participant and participants) applied throughout this pipeline (Figure 8). The resulting curves show the expected pattern: sustained dilation on Target trials and a gradual constriction on Distractor trials, the two diverging from approximately 500 ms after stimulus onset and remaining separated for the remainder of the trial window. This pattern closely resembles the response previously reported with the same paradigm in patients with MCI and Alzheimer's disease (Jiménez et al., 2021).

We recommend that researchers generate and inspect a figure of this kind as a standard step before proceeding to inferential analysis, a practice long established in other psychophysiological measures, particularly event-related potentials in EEG, where the grand-average waveform is the conventional first check that a manipulation produced the expected pattern before any statistical test is run (Luck, 2014). Mathôt and Vilotijević (2023) make the same recommendation specifically for pupillometry: even when the planned analysis relies on a predetermined time window rather than the full time-course, they note that a time-course figure remains informative because it often reveals information that summary line or bar plots lose. This inspection, however, should not be used to select the window in which the plotted conditions will then be compared, since choosing a window from a figure that already displays the contrast of interest biases the subsequent test toward significance (Luck & Gaspelin, 2017). Analysis windows should instead be fixed independently of that contrast, from prior literature or from a waveform collapsed across the conditions or groups being compared.

Following this logic, we specified six trial-level features to be extracted from the baselinecorrected pupil signal, each intended to capture a different aspect of the response visible in the grand-average waveform: an initial slope (linear regression of pupil size on time, in mm/s, over the first 500 ms), a global slope (the same regression over the full trial duration), a late slope (the same regression restricted to 1000 ms onward, capturing the sustained portion of the response), the area under the curve (trapezoidal integration of pupil size over time, in mm•ms), the time to peak (the time, in ms, at which the absolute baseline-corrected pupil size reaches its maximum), and peak amplitude (that maximum absolute value, in mm).

These features and their windows were specified a priori, following prior studies using this paradigm (Martínez-Flores et al., 2026a , 2026b , 2026c), in which they were defined on the grandaverage waveform collapsed across stimulus types, and therefore independently of the Target-Distractor contrast tested here. Specifying multiple candidate features a priori in this way supports a range of analytical strategies a study may wish to apply to trial-level pupil data, rather than committing to a single summary measure selected after inspecting which portion of the signal happens to be significant. Target (red) and Distractor (blue) trials, shown as the mean across participants with CI bands.

When no a priori window can be motivated by prior literature, cluster-based permutation testing (Maris & Oostenveld, 2007; Mathôt & Vilotijević, 2023) offers a widely used alternative for identifying which segments of a time-varying signal differ between conditions, without requiring the comparison to be specified in advance. In the present within-subject comparison, this technique would add little beyond what Figure 8 already shows directly: because Target and Distractor diverge from approximately 500 ms and remain separated thereafter, a cluster-based test would identify a single cluster spanning most of the trial window, offering no finer temporal resolution than visual inspection of the grand average itself. The value of this technique is more likely to be realized in a use case common in this population, comparing a full response curve between separate groups, for instance MCI patients against cognitively healthy older adults, or across different biomarker-defined or clinical stages of impairment, where the shape and timing of any between-group divergence is not known in advance and a predetermined window would risk missing where in the trial the groups actually differ.

4. Statistical Analysis

Cognitive pupillometry data can be analyzed at different levels of detail, and the appropriate method depends on the question being asked rather than on any single preferred technique. If the question is how an effect unfolds over time (when it emerges, when it peaks, whether it has distinct phases), the full time-course must be modeled, and a generalized additive mixed model (4.1) does so without assuming its shape. If the question is how large the effect is on average, a linear mixed-effects model with a single coefficient (4.2) answers it directly and interpretably, and is closely equivalent to analyzing each trial's mean or area under the curve.

Between these two lies the analysis of trial-level features (4.3): by decomposing each trial into a small set of summary values specified a priori, such as the initial slope, time to peak, or peak amplitude, specific aspects of the response's temporal shape can be tested with the same simple models, without the complexity of a GAMM. These approaches are complementary rather than redundant. The GAMM describes the whole shape of the response but yields no single effect size for downstream analyses such as correlations with biomarkers or comparisons between groups, whereas the single-coefficient model and trial-level features yield such effect sizes but capture only the aspects of the response specified in advance. We therefore recommend, when feasible, reporting the full time-course alongside one or more summary measures chosen to match the study's hypothesis (Table 1). These recommendations are not specific to MCI and apply to cognitive pupillometry in general. They weigh more heavily, however, in samples like the present one, where unequal trial counts between conditions, data quality that varies widely between participants, and strong autocorrelation at a low sampling rate make the assumptions behind model-based inference harder to take for granted. In the present Target-versus-Distractor comparison, we therefore proceed from the most to the least detailed description of the effect. A generalized additive mixed model (4.1) models the non-linear time-course of each condition and the autocorrelation between samples directly, and shows how the difference between conditions unfolds: it becomes reliable at approximately 485 ms and grows to a maximum of 0.118 mm at approximately 1455 ms. A linear mixed-effects model with a single coefficient (4.2) then summarizes this difference as its average across the trial; although it uses every sample, it is in practice a summary-value analysis, closely related to the trial-level features specified in Section 3.6 and analyzed in Section 4.3, such as the area under the curve or the global slope. Mathôt and Vilotijević's (2023) cross-validation technique addresses the same underlying question by a different route: it uses part of the data to locate the sample at which the difference between conditions, or groups, is largest, and tests the difference at that sample in the held-out data. It is therefore equally applicable to within-subject and between-group comparisons, and in many cases answers the same question as a GAMM; we use the GAMM here because it additionally describes the shape of the difference across the whole trial.

4.1 Modeling the non-linear shape of the pupil signal: generalized additive mixed models (GAMM) and AR(1) correction

Most pupillometry studies that test an effect against the full time-course of the pupil response do so either by extracting a small number of features per trial (peak amplitude, latency, mean pupil size within a window) or by fitting mixed-effects models with polynomial time terms to the trajectory (van Rij et al., 2019). The latter, growth curve analysis with linear, quadratic, and cubic time terms and samples nested within trials and participants (Mirman, 2014; e.g., Kret et al., 2014 e.g., Kret et al., , 2015)), already captures much of the curvature of the response and is a sound choice when the expected shape is simple enough to be described by a few polynomial terms. Its limitation is that the polynomial order must be fixed in advance, and low-order polynomials can describe an asymmetric dilation curve poorly at its onset and tail. The resampling checks described in Sections 4.2 and 4.3 apply equally to such polynomial models. The pupil dilation response to a salient stimulus is a smooth, asymmetric curve, and assuming a fixed functional form without checking the actual shape of the signal risks both under-fitting genuine non-linear structure and mis-specifying the autocorrelation of the model residuals, which itself inflates Type I error (van Rij et al., 2019).

Generalized additive mixed models (GAMMs) address both problems from within the same model, rather than treating them as separate corrections layered on afterward. A smooth term for time, allowed to take a different shape for each condition, lets the curve differ between Target and Distractor trials rather than only its overall level; an additional smooth term for time nested within participant absorbs each individual's own non-linear trajectory, the additive-model equivalent of the "keep it maximal" logic for random effects (Barr et al., 2013) applied to curve shape rather than to a single slope. Critically, GAMMs also allow a first-order autoregressive (AR(1)) correlation structure to be estimated and fit directly into the model, letting the model itself, rather than a separate correction applied to the test statistic, account for the dependency between neighboring samples that motivates Mathôt and Vilotijević's (2023) multiple-comparisons techniques in the first place.

We modeled baseline-corrected pupil size with stimulus (Target vs. Distractor) as the predictor of interest. Rather than assuming any particular shape over time, the model estimated a smooth curve over time for each condition, so that Target and Distractor could differ not only in their overall level but also in the shape of their time-course. A second smooth curve was estimated for each participant, allowing each participant's response to take its own shape, in the same spirit as a by-participant random slope in a linear mixed model (Baayen et al., 2017).

Because consecutive pupil samples are strongly correlated, the model must also account for this autocorrelation; otherwise it treats every sample as independent information and overstates the certainty of its estimates (van Rij et al., 2019). We did this in two steps. We first fit the model without any correction and measured how strongly each residual correlated with the next one within the same trial, never across trial boundaries. This correlation was nearly perfect (ρ = .986).

We then refit the model with a first-order autoregressive, or AR(1), term fixed at that value, which tells the model that each sample largely repeats the information of the one before it. The correction barely changed how well the model fit the data (deviance explained 16.69% vs. 16.37%), but the estimated curves became more flexible (effective degrees of freedom, a measure of how 'wiggly' a curve is, where 1 corresponds to a straight line, increased from about 9.5 to about 15 for each condition). We report the AR(1)-corrected model (Table 2).

The parametric Target coefficient, representing the average shift in pupil size for Target trials relative to Distractor trials (the reference level), pooled across the smooth terms, was significant (β = 0.065 mm, SE = 0.003, t = 19.07, p < .001), and all three smooth terms were significant (all p < .001). To characterize when within the trial window this divergence emerges, we additionally computed the population-level difference curve (Target minus Distractor), excluding the by-participant smooth, with a 95% confidence band derived from the model's full coefficient covariance matrix, which properly propagates parameter uncertainty rather than naively combining two separate per-condition standard errors. The confidence interval excluded zero at 51 of the 67 unique timepoints sampled across the trial window, from approximately 485 ms onward to the end of the window; the difference peaked at 0.118 mm (95% CI [0.110, 0.125]) at approximately 1455 ms, consistent with a divergence that emerges early and is sustained for most of the window rather than being confined to a brief interval. This approach is most valuable whenever the question concerns how an effect unfolds over time, or whether the relationship with a continuous predictor is linear: it models the shape of the signal rather than assuming it, and accounts for temporal autocorrelation within the model itself. When the question is instead the average size of an effect across the trial, a linear mixed-effects model offers a substantially simpler and more directly interpretable estimate (4.2), and the two approaches agree on that average, as shown next.

4.2 Full time-series linear mixed model

The generalized additive model described above shows how an effect unfolds over time, but it comes at a real cost in complexity and interpretability: a single, directly interpretable effect size is harder to extract from a set of smooth terms than from a single regression coefficient (Wood, 2017). In our previous study of the cross-sectional cohort described in Section 2.1 (Martínez-Flores et al., 2026b), the effect of interest was the interaction between stimulus type and CSF biomarkers of neurodegeneration, and a GAMM was first used to verify that the relationship with each biomarker was linear: all smooth terms had effective degrees of freedom close to 1 (1.01-1.07), supporting a linear term in the primary analysis. Here, the linear model serves a simpler role: it estimates the average Target-Distractor difference across the trial with a single coefficient.

We modeled baseline-corrected pupil size as the dependent variable, with stimulus (Target vs. Distractor) as the predictor of interest, using every sample of every accepted trial as a separate observation. This choice has one consequence that shapes the whole analysis: consecutive samples within a trial are strongly autocorrelated (Section 4.1), so the model's own standard errors and pvalues, which assume independent observations, cannot be trusted on their own; moreover, pupilsize measures are frequently non-normal even at sample sizes considerably larger than this one (Steinhauer et al., 2022). We therefore based inference on two resampling procedures that make no distributional assumption and respect the structure of the data, described below, and report the model's Wald statistics only for comparison.

The random-effects structure included a by-participant random intercept and random slopes for stimulus and for time. The random slope for stimulus follows Barr et al. (2013): because the Target versus Distractor effect can differ between participants, omitting it would treat that effect as identical across participants and understate its uncertainty. Time was included both as a fixed covariate and as a by-participant random slope, capturing the average and each participant's own trend across the trial window; neither, by itself, corrects for the autocorrelation between neighboring samples. Because stimulus is tested once, in a single model, no multiple-comparisons correction is needed. This estimate is effectively the average Target-Distractor difference across the trial, so it is closely equivalent to first averaging each trial into a single value and analyzing those values; fitting it at the sample level is chiefly a matter of convenience, for instance when the stimulus effect is to be tested in interaction with trial-or participant-level covariates, as in the study that motivated this analysis (Martínez-Flores et al., 2026b). The model was fit by restricted maximum likelihood (REML; Bates et al., 2015), with Wald degrees of freedom obtained via the Satterthwaite approximation (Kuznetsova et al., 2017).

The two resampling procedures answer distinct questions. A block-permutation test addresses whether the effect exists at all: the stimulus label of each trial is shuffled within participant, which leaves each trial's samples, and therefore their autocorrelation, intact, and the model is refit on each relabeled dataset. Because the autocorrelation is preserved in every permuted dataset, it affects the null distribution exactly as it affects the observed statistic, so the resulting pvalue remains valid. Because the observed labeling is itself one of the possible permutations, a permutation p-value is bounded below by 1/(B + 1), where B is the number of permutations, and can never be exactly zero (Phipson & Smyth, 2010); results beyond this bound are therefore reported as p < .001. A BCa cluster bootstrap (Efron, 1987) addresses the precision of the Target coefficient itself, by resampling participants with replacement and refitting the model on each resample. Both procedures resample whole trials or participants rather than individual samples, respecting the hierarchical structure of the data (Field & Welsh, 2007). Each used 5,000 iterations.

To verify that the block-permutation test keeps its nominal false-positive rate when samples are this strongly autocorrelated, we additionally ran a calibration check by simulation: we generated 100 datasets with no stimulus effect by construction (15 participants, 20 trials each with 20%

Target trials, 40 samples per trial at 30 ms intervals, participant-specific random intercepts, and within-trial AR(1) noise with the autocorrelation estimated from our data, ρ = .986), fit the same model to each, and applied the same block-permutation procedure (199 permutations per dataset).

A well-calibrated test should return p < .05 in approximately 5% of these datasets, with p-values uniformly distributed.

The Target coefficient (401,263 samples from 5,989 trials of 67 participants) was significant, confirming the same Target versus Distractor divergence recovered by the GAMM in Section 4.1. The two estimates are close (0.066 mm here against 0.065 mm from the GAMM's parametric term), as expected, since both estimate the average Target-Distractor difference across the trial window. This agreement confirms that the simpler model recovers the average effect correctly, while the GAMM additionally shows how that difference evolves over time. Both resampling-based checks agree with this result (Table 3): none of 5,000 permuted datasets produced a |t| as large as the one observed (|t| = 9.55; p < .001), and the BCa bootstrap interval excludes zero and tracks the Wald interval closely. 4.3 Trial-level features and correction for multiple comparisons

Each feature was analyzed with its own linear mixed-effects model, feature ~ stimulus + (1 + stimulus | participant), retaining the by-participant random slope for stimulus for the reason given in Section 4.2 (Barr et al., 2013); time no longer appears in the model, since each feature already summarizes the whole trial. Reducing each trial to such summary values and analyzing them with mixed-effects models is a well-established approach in clinical pupillometry (e.g., Boxhoorn et al., 2020). 4.4 Leave-one-subject-out sensitivity check

In populations that are inherently difficult to measure, such as older adults with MCI, data quality varies widely between participants, and some retain relatively few trials after qualitycontrol exclusions; in the oddball paradigm used here, the 20% Target frequency further reduces the number of Target trials per participant. A mixed-effects model already accounts for unequal tested against it, one change at a time relative to the production pipeline: neither the choice of minimum pupil diameter, baseline criterion, smoothing filter, nor coverage threshold changed the qualitative outcome, even where the specific numerical estimate shifted, and the interpolation method retained was the more accurate of the two against ground truth. This robustness could not have been established without the decision-by-decision testing reported here: without it, the choice of any single default would have been an untested assumption rather than a confirmed non-issue, and a smaller or subtler effect could easily have depended on exactly the choice that here made no difference. This pattern is what a decision-by-decision adaptation is meant to produce: not a single correct pipeline, but confidence that the reported effect is a property of the population and paradigm rather than an artifact of any single preprocessing choice among several defensible ones.

This decision-by-decision test produced no uniform verdict on the guidelines it started from, and that absence of a uniform verdict is itself informative. Some defaults held up unchanged once tested: the 500 ms maximum-gap threshold was corroborated within under one percentage point by a data-driven elbow, and Mathôt's mean-based baseline z-score criterion (Mathôt & Vilotijević, 2023) agreed with a more robust MAD-based alternative (Leys et al., 2013) on 97.7% of exclusion decisions, and was the more conservative of the two. Others did not transfer: cubicspline interpolation, Mathôt and Vilotijević's (2023) stated preference, performed worse than simple linear interpolation against ground truth when implemented as the same four-point local fit their algorithm uses. Still others had no applicable default at all and were derived empirically from this dataset alone: the minimum pupil-size cutoff, informed by this sample's pupil-size distribution and checked against interocular agreement and its effect on the reported response, rather than the generic 2 mm heuristic; the interocular coverage ratio threshold, anchored to an empirical selection curve; and the minimum per-trial coverage, retained after permutation tests across the full range of literature-anchored cutoffs found no detectable bias. This pattern, spread across defaults that transfer, defaults that do not, and genuine gaps, suggests that existing cognitive-pupillometry guidelines generalize reasonably across sampling rate and population for some preprocessing stages but not others, consistent with the field's broader lack of a single standardized preprocessing framework (Schwartz et al., 2026). The contribution of this paper is this decision-by-decision map of which assumptions transfer unchanged, which do not transfer, and which have no existing anchor at all, not a single new pipeline to be adopted wholesale.

The statistical analysis adds a complementary point. The techniques Mathôt and Vilotijević (2023) propose for the multiple-comparisons problem fit a separate model at every sample and therefore make no assumption about the shape of the pupil time-course; summarizing an effect with a single coefficient, by contrast, answers a narrower question, the average size of the effect across the trial, and is silent on how it unfolds. In our data, the GAMM showed that the Target-Distractor difference became reliable at approximately 485 ms and peaked at approximately 1455 ms, while the single-coefficient model and the trial-level features recovered its average size and specific components; where these approaches overlapped, they agreed, which supports using the simpler ones when the question concerns the size of an effect rather than its timing (Table 1). The leave-one-subject-out check in Section 4.4 served a similarly specific purpose: in a population where data quality varies widely between individuals, it confirmed that no single atypical participant drove the pooled estimate, something a mixed-effects model does not by itself guard against (Nieuwenhuis et al., 2012). Finally, the block-permutation, max-t, and BCa checks in Sections 4.2 and 4.3 agreed closely with their Wald counterparts, suggesting that, at this sample size, model-based inference was not misleading despite the autocorrelation and non-normality of the signal.

Both patterns just described, partial transfer of preprocessing defaults and the added necessity of statistical verification in this population, converge on the translational motivation stated in the Introduction: moving cognitive pupillometry out of specialized laboratories and into more accessible clinical and lower-cost recording contexts (Cuve et al., 2022; Romeo et al., 2024; Wolf et al., 2023). The guidelines this paper builds on were developed and validated on eye trackers such as EyeLink or Tobii Pro systems, sampling at rates an order of magnitude higher than the 33 Hz used here. Every threshold that required empirical derivation in this paper rather than direct reuse, the minimum pupil size, interocular coverage balance, and minimum per-trial coverage, was one where the coarser temporal resolution and noisier signal of a lower-cost, lowersampling-rate tracker plausibly changes where a reasonable cutoff falls. This paper's central practical message for that translational goal is accordingly not that any specific threshold reported here should be copied into a lower-cost pipeline, but that preprocessing itself is a sensitive stage that must be adapted and re-tested for the recording conditions at hand, rather than copied wholesale from pipelines built for laboratory-grade, high-sampling-rate eye trackers.

5.1 Strengths and Limitations

The pipeline's decisions were tested on two independently collected cohorts, so each threshold and modeling choice was evaluated under more heterogeneous recording conditions than a single cohort would provide. The resulting sample, 67 participants and close to 600,000 raw samples, was large enough to support the data-driven checks this paper relies on, which would be considerably noisier, or infeasible, in a smaller sample. Because every check is implemented in the accompanying code, researchers can re-run them on their own data rather than adopt the thresholds reported here.

This work also has limitations. No healthy older-adult comparison group was available, so we cannot rule out that some empirically derived thresholds would differ in healthy older adults, and all data came from a single session per participant, so their stability across sessions remains untested. Because screening instrument, biomarker modality, and cohort are confounded in this sample (Section 2.1), neither between-group comparisons nor pooled covariates for medication status and cognitive severity were possible, although neither covariate showed a relevant effect within each cohort (Martínez-Flores et al., 2026a , 2026b , 2026c). Finally, a single paradigm, population, and eye tracker were used, so the specific thresholds reported here should be re-derived rather than transferred directly to other contexts.

6. Conclusion

This paper adapted a cognitive-pupillometry pipeline, decision by decision, to older adults with MCI recorded at 33 Hz, a context that departs substantially from the one existing guidelines were developed on. Its contribution is not a new method or a clinical marker, but a transparent account of which defaults hold in this harder context, which require empirical re-derivation, and which have no literature anchor at all.
