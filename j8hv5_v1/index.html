Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard), also Internal Review Board - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term); not IEC followed by a number, which is a technical standard - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee/Commission, in the singular or plural - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee, Bioethics Committee/Commission, Ethikkommission - Ethical Review Authority/Unit/Office/Agency - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Human Research Protection Office/Program, Office for Human Subjects Protection - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee(s) on/for (Medical and Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt - Ethics review that was not needed: Ethics review was not required, did not require further ethical review, exempt from formal human research ethics review, no new institutional ethics review

A template line the authors have not filled in (“[Add ethics approval … here.]”) is not a statement.

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

When a statement is expected

The module sorts each paper by where its data came from (the internal .data_source(); the signs and how the rule was tested are described in R/data-source.R): - own: the authors collected data from people or animals, for this paper, in an earlier study of their own, or (a protocol) will collect them; - existing_records: records about individual people that the authors obtained for this study, such as hospital records or a cohort; - existing_unclear: existing data about people from a source the module could not identify; - open_resource: an open resource any researcher can use, such as UK Biobank or NHANES; - none: no data about people or animals (a review, simulation, commentary, theory, tutorial, coding of published papers, or figures about groups); - uncertain: the signs were missing or pointed both ways; - no_text: the paper has no text.

A statement is expected for own, existing_records and existing_unclear. A paper without a statement in these classes, or in uncertain, is flagged. The module reads the text, and the results of power, stat_p_exact, stat_check, repo_check, data_check and prereg_check when they ran before it (run it after them); without stat_p_exact, it finds the p-values in the text.

On 199 PsyArXiv and medRxiv preprints labelled after the rule was fixed, the module decided right whether a statement is needed for 86.9% (the earlier live-data search: 83.4%). Its main errors: other people’s studies described in the words of a new study, records and specimens described only as counts, and open resources the paper does not call open.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 685 tests with 34 instances of inconsistent reporting, Statcheck correctly detected 34 of them, and incorrectly identified 26. Therefore 0% of true instances were missed, and 43% of detections were false positives. See https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/ for more details of the validation.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on 226 Psychological Science papers with 5,019 hand-coded p-values, 438 of them imprecise (scienceverse/validation, branch stat_p_exact). Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). Of the misses, 13 are starred table notes (“*p < .05”) that the module leaves out on purpose and 11 are p-values it did not find; of the wrong reports, 20 are p-values the ground truth does not contain (such as “ps < .05”) and 6 use ≤ or ≥. How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on three hand-coded sets of papers (scienceverse/validation: 249 Psychological Science, 250 Economics and 250 MDPI papers). Of 1,783 non-significant p-values in the ground truth, version 0.4.4.3 found 1,719 (3.6% missed), and 94.8% of its 1,814 detections were in the ground truth. Some differences come from the ground truth: it lacks p-values with a correction name (“p Bonf = .061”), and counts partial eta squared written with its subscript after it (“η 2 p = .05”), “p = .05”, and some table notes and figure legends (“† p < .10, *p < .05”), which this module leaves out, as non-significant p-values. How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Abstract

Multiple influential constructs in social and moral psychology-including symbolic racism, modern racism, racial resentment, modern sexism, social dominance orientation, system justification, belief in a just world, implicit bias, authoritarianism, and moral foundations theory-share a structural feature. In each case, the construct's standard psychological interpretation depends on treating a contested premise as settled: that disparate outcomes constitute evidence of discrimination. When that premise is treated as contested rather than given, the standard interpretation becomes observationally equivalent to substantive disagreement about how institutions function. This paper demonstrates that equivalence across the constructs and offers a historically grounded account of how the premise became embedded in the field's conceptual vocabulary. Moral Foundations Theory provides the crucial test case: even research explicitly designed to avoid pathologizing conservatives encodes the same framework-not through its assumptions about the justice of the system, but through the definitions of harm and fairness built into its taxonomic architecture.

I) Introduction

Over the past half century, social psychology has produced an extraordinary body of research on political ideology, values, and moral judgment. This research has generated some of the field's most influential and enduring constructs: authoritarianism (Altemeyer 1981), social dominance orientation (Pratto et al. 1994), system justification (Jost and Banaji 1994), belief in a just world (M. Lerner 1980; M. J. Lerner and Simmons 1966), implicit bias (Anthony G. Greenwald et al. 1998; A. G. Greenwald and Banaji 1995), moral foundations (Haidt and Joseph 2004; Haidt 2012; Graham et al. 2009), and the family of constructs measuring contemporary racial and gender attitudes -symbolic racism (Kinder and Sears 1981), modern racism (McConahay 1986), racial resentment (Kinder and Sanders 1997), and modern sexism (Swim et al. 1995).

Each of these research programs has produced robust, replicable findings and extensive empirical literatures. Each has been validated through standard psychometric procedures. And each has become part of the conceptual vocabulary that educated people use to understand political disagreement.

These programs differ in their theoretical commitments, their methods, and the specific claims they make. But, in each case, the interpretive force they've been assigned rests in significant part on the treatment of a contested premise as given: that disparate outcomes constitute evidence of discrimination.

The directness of this connection varies across the constructs. It is most transparent in measures of contemporary racial and gender attitudes-symbolic racism, modern racism, racial resentment, modern sexism-where the premise is not a background assumption shaping the construct definition but is itself what the instrument measures. In other cases-system justification, social dominance orientation, implicit bias-the connection is nearly definitional: the construct can only be interpreted as measuring a psychological deficit or disposition if existing institutional arrangements are presumed unjust, if hierarchical sorting is presumed to reflect dominance rather than function, or if equal automatic associations across demographic groups is presumed to be the unbiased baseline. In these cases, removing the premise doesn't merely weaken the standard interpretation-it collapses it. In still other cases-authoritarianism, belief in a just world-the connection operates through construct definitions that classify substantive positions about institutional function as psychological dispositions, encoding assumptions about which positions require psychological explanation and which do not. The connection is real but less immediately visible, because the construct definitions have additional content beyond the premise itself. And in the case that receives the most extended treatment-Moral Foundations Theory-the connection operates at the deepest architectural level: not through the presupposition that the system is unjust, but through the definitions of foundational moral concepts-harm, fairness, authority-that determine how responses are categorized and interpreted. MFT warrants particular attention precisely because it was designed to avoid the pathologizing tendency of the other constructs yet encodes the same framework through a different mechanism.

As I'll demonstrate, only with the core assumption in place do the constructs work as advertised. Skepticism about the extent of ongoing discrimination is racism (symbolic racism, modern racism, racial resentment, modern sexism). Deference to those institutions is deference to injustice (authoritarianism), endorsing hierarchies that produce them indicates dominance (social dominance orientation), defending them is motivated rationalization (system justification), believing the system is just despite them is cognitive bias (just world belief), and automatic associations tracking those disparities are prejudice requiring remedy (implicit bias). And moral foundations theory is typically interpreted as showing innate differences across political divides. Across the constructs, as soon as we stop treating the underlying assumption as settled, an alternative interpretation emerges.

To be sure, the causal claim upon which these research programs rest might, in fact, be correct. Disparities might indicate institutional discrimination in some or even most cases. My argument rests only on treating the premise as what it is-a causal claim about complex social processes, subject to inherent uncertainty. A reasonable objection might be that the premise is sufficiently well established that treating it as given is justified. Such an argument might point to, for instance, audit studies and redlining-both of which demonstrate ongoing and institutional discrimination. But those examples, as important as they are, do not negate the argument made here.

Audit studies demonstrate that discrimination exists in specific domains-hiring, lending, housing-but such studies cannot quantify what portion of observed disparities that discrimination explains, nor can they distinguish statistical from taste-based discrimination. Redlining is often invoked as an example of institutional racism (as evidence for the impact of racist effects from facially neutral policies), but it actually undermines the premise rather than supporting it. Redlining involved explicit racial intent-deliberate government policy designed to exclude black Americans from mortgage lending and homeownership. It is an example of intentional discrimination, not of facially neutral processes producing disparate outcomes. The premise this paper identifies-that disparate outcomes themselves constitute evidence of discrimination, regardless of intent-requires precisely the cases that redlining is not: cases where no discriminatory intent can be identified and yet the disparities are treated as sufficient proof. Beyond audit studies and cases of demonstrable intent, the primary evidence offered for the premise is disparities themselves-which cannot serve as both the evidence for discrimination and the conclusion that discrimination has occurred.

The consequences of treating the dominant interpretations as given have extended far beyond the immediate reach of academic journals. These bodies of research have shaped how educated Americans-particularly on the political left-understand political disagreement itself (Haidt 2012; Banaji and Greenwald 2016). What presented itself as scientific discovery about human psychology has helped legitimize the interpretation of conservative positions-about authority, hierarchy, tradition, meritocracy, individual responsibility, social order-as symptoms of psychological limitation rather than as defensible moral and empirical positions about how the world works.

To be sure, the observation that politically driven asymmetry-via liberal bias in particularexists within the field of social psychology is hardly new (Redding 2001); conservative critics have noted it for decades. Survey data consistently show that social psychologists are overwhelmingly liberal (Duarte et al. 2015; Inbar and Lammers 2012), raising obvious questions about whether the field's conceptual frameworks reflect its members' political commitments. But the standard critique-"the field is politically biased"-has struggled to gain traction within the discipline, and for understandable reasons. The constructs under fire are well-validated (Duckitt 2001; Jost et al. 2009). The findings replicate (Duckitt and Sibley 2010; Jost 2017). The measures predict what they are supposed to predict.

Not only that, but there's a readily available counterpoint to such attacks: that the critics are themselves ideologically motivated. They are often politically conservative, with self-evident reasons to dislike research portraying conservatism as rigid, biased, or cognitively distorted. But there is a deeper problem with the political bias critique, beyond the question of who is making it. The critique cannot explain why the asymmetry takes the specific form it does. A field that was simply liberal would be expected to produce instruments favoring liberal policy positions. What the field actually produced is something more specific: instruments encoding a particular understanding of what harm, fairness, discrimination, and bias mean-definitions that shifted in identifiable ways, at identifiable points in institutional history, for identifiable reasons. The political bias critique treats the asymmetry as a reflection of who the researchers are. The present argument treats it as a reflection of what the words mean in the institutional context in which the researchers work.

The argument proceeds as follows. Section II traces the institutional transformation that reshaped how the professional and academic class understands harm, fairness, discrimination, and biasand describes how that reshaped understanding entered the field's construct definitions. Section III demonstrates the dependence of each construct's standard interpretation on the contested premise, organized from the most transparent cases to the most architecturally subtle. Section IV provides an extended analysis of Moral Foundations Theory as the crucial test case-the construct that should be immune to the encoding but is not. Section V addresses likely misreadings. And Section VI considers what follows if the argument is correct-for the field's research questions, for what its existing interpretations can legitimately claim, and for the relationship between measurement and ideological disagreement.

II) Transformation

The premise that entered the field's measurement instruments has a specific institutional history. The intellectual groundwork -the idea that racism could operate systemically, that facially neutral practices could perpetuate inequality, that disparate outcomes could constitute evidence of discrimination -predated 1971 (e.g., Carmichael and Hamilton 1967; Clark and Clark 1950). But in 1971, the Supreme Court's unanimous decision in Griggs v. Duke Power Co. gave that set of ideas something intellectual argument alone had not: legal authority (Griggs 1971). While this section provides a compressed account of that transformation, a fuller treatment can be found elsewhere (Redstone 2025).

I am not arguing that psychology researchers were necessarily aware of this underlying shift. In fact, the opposite it far more likely. And yet, the present argument doesn't depend on that knowledge. Consider an analogy: A person doesn't need to know who built the interstate highway system to live in a suburb that exists because of it. A researcher did not need to know the history to work within an intellectual environment in which the claim was treated as established fact rather than contested.

A) The Legal Shift: Disparate Outcomes as Evidence of Discrimination

In March 1971, the Supreme Court ruled unanimously in Griggs v. Duke Power Co. that employment practices producing racially disparate outcomes could violate Title VII of the Civil Rights Act of 1964, even in the absence of discriminatory intent. Chief Justice Burger wrote that "good intent or absence of discriminatory intent does not redeem employment procedures" that produce unequal racial results. The Court's decision included a business necessity exceptionemployers could defend practices with disparate impact if the practices were essential to job performance. The exception excused liability; it did not change the classification. By its equation under Title VII, the practice remained a form of discrimination, even when the employer could legally continue it.

Given the facts of the case itself-Duke Power Co. had an explicit history of racial exclusion and adopted facially neutral requirements that produced similarly exclusionary outcomes the same day Title VII of the Civil Rights Act took effect-the Court's decision would have likely appeared self-evidently correct to most observers. Up to that point, anti-discrimination law mainly existed under the Equal Protection Clause (EPA) (Fourteenth Amendment 1868). But cases brought under the EPA generally required plaintiffs to demonstrate discriminatory intent, which could be done either through "smoking gun" evidence of bias or by ruling out all other non-discriminatory explanations (Washington v. Davis 1976; Redstone 2025; Yick Wo v. Hopkins 1886). Because hard evidence of intentional bias is rare and plausible non-discriminatory explanations exist in the vast majority of situations, an unknown number of discrimination cases were almost certainly lost or never brought to court. By making disparate outcomes a violation of Title VII, the Court shifted the operative question from whether policies treated people the same to whether they affected people the same.

B) From Legal Doctrine to Institutional Framework

The breadth of the adoption of this shift matters for the argument of this paper, because the more thoroughly a premise is absorbed into the institutional environment, the less anyone within that environment needs to be aware of its origins-or even that it is a premise at all-in order to be shaped by it. For starters, the equation codified in the Griggs v. Duke Power Co. decision didn't just change the field of employment law, although it did that too (U.S. Equal Employment Opportunity Commission 2024; Equal Employment Opportunity Commission, First Annual Report 1967; Garrow 2014).

Another indication of its ultimate reach is the widespread adoption of diversity, equity, and inclusion (DEI) initiatives. DEI frameworks operationalize the principle that underrepresentation constitutes evidence of discrimination and that institutional legitimacy requires addressing disparate outcomes rather than merely ensuring fair process. This is visible in the shift in corporate diversity management from "do our processes treat people fairly?" to "do our outcomes look proportional?" (Ng et al. 2025; Abrica and Oliver Andrew 2025; Alicea 2026). By 2025, the World Economic Forum reported that 83 percent of surveyed employers had implemented DEI measures (World Econ. F. 2025).

Similar growth is reflected in the emergence of organizations like the American Institute for Managing Diversity in 1984 (Am. Inst. for Managing Diversity 2002) as well as the increase in diversity officers in higher education. In the latter case, the National Association of Diversity Officers in Higher Education went from 30 members at the time of its founding in 2003 to over 2,200 by 2025. (NADAHOE, n.d.-b, n.d.-a). Professional associations (ASA, n.d. ; Task Force on Re-envisioning the Multicultural Guidelines for the 21st Century 2017), accreditation bodies (NASPAA, n.d.), funding agencies (Lind 2022), and academic journals (Burgess 2025) also incorporated diversity standards into their evaluative criteria.

On the academic front, scholarship that had previously theorized racism as a systemic phenomenon-operating through institutional mechanisms independent of individual attitudes or conscious bias also grew. It now found its claims indirectly validated by the legal authority that stood behind an impact-based definition of discrimination (see, for instance, Feagin and Bennefield 2014; or see Redstone 2025 for a summary). 1,2 For social and moral psychology, this widespread absorption of the framework meant that researchers building instruments to measure authoritarianism, bias, fairness, or harm wouldn't have had to be aware of any particular contested claim. They were operating in a professional and academic context where the shift was seen as deeper understanding of how discrimination operates, more sophisticated recognition of harm, greater precision about what fairness requires.

C) The Recoding of Moral Vocabulary

By determining that disparate outcomes can violate Title VII of the Civil Rights Act of 1964, Griggs explicitly established that those unequal outcomes-regardless of intent-could constitute evidence of discrimination. That naturally led to a corresponding moral shift, albeit one that was implicit. Here's the logic: Disparate outcomes violate Title VII->the purpose of Title VII is to prevent discrimination in the workplace->therefore, disparate outcomes constitute discrimination. And because discrimination is broadly understood as an expression of racism, disparate outcomes became evidence of racism.

Once unequal outcomes were considered evidence of discrimination and racism, the meaning of related moral concepts also changed. "Discrimination" shifted from intentional differential treatment to any process producing unequal results. "Harm" expanded from direct suffering inflicted on identifiable individuals to systemic disadvantage reflected in statistical disparitiesand further, to subjective experiences of discomfort or exclusion regardless of the speaker's intent. "Fairness" shifted from procedural equality (identical treatment regardless of group membership) to outcome equity (proportional representation across groups). "Racism" expanded from conscious hostility or deliberate mistreatment to any pattern, practice, or attitude that sustained racial disparities-a definition capacious enough to encompass the denial of its own premises.

Additional conceptual changes followed. Because intent no longer mattered when it came to harm affecting groups now seen as structurally disadvantaged, the actions and utterances that counted as bigotry further expanded to include any that a member of a disadvantaged group perceived or could reasonably perceive as such. And because institutional arrangements were now understood as the source of unequal outcomes, any defense of those arrangements-appeals to authority, hierarchy, tradition, meritocracy, or institutional stability-became suspect. The transformation didn't just redefine discrimination; it recast the new understanding as morally and empirically correct, thereby delegitimizing an entire way of thinking, including about how institutions function and what makes them legitimate-a way of thinking most closely associated with conservative political positions-the defense of institutional authority, meritocratic sorting, traditional norms, and incremental reform over structural transformation.

The recoding was invisible precisely because, among left-leaning scholars in particular, it would have been absorbed as insight rather than ideology. Nevertheless, the consequence of treating the underlying claim as definitive and final was the transformation of an entire family of substantive political positions from positions that could be argued for or against on their merits to symptoms of a failure to understand how discrimination operates.

III) Institutional Recoding Enters Measurement

The process described here suggests that this shift entered psychology's measures the same way it entered the rest of academia-through the absorption of redefined concepts into professional vocabulary. The fact that social psychologists are overwhelmingly liberal (Inbar and Lammers 2012; Duarte et al. 2015) undoubtedly made the absorption smoother. In a field with more political diversity, the consistent appearance of conservative positions on the pathological side of the instruments might have generated internal resistance earlier. But political homogeneity is a facilitating condition, not a cause. It explains why the confound went unchallenged-not how it got there. With that context established, the specific constructs can be examined. I'll address them in order of the visibility of the underlying assumption-from constructs where the premise is most transparently on the surface to the one where it operates at the deepest architectural level. The first three groups are treated below; Moral Foundations Theory, which constitutes the fourth, receives its own section.

In the first group are scales that directly measure agreement with the premise and code disagreement as prejudice. The symbolic racism scale (Kinder and Sears 1981; Henry and Sears 2002) measures the conjunction of anti-black affect with opposition to policies designed to address racial inequality-affirmative action, busing, welfare programs associated with black beneficiaries. Respondents who attribute racial inequality to factors other than discriminationwho believe that individual effort matters, that progress has been substantial, or that demands for special treatment are unwarranted-score higher. Similarly, the modern racism scale (McConahay 1986) includes items such as "Discrimination against Blacks is no longer a problem in the United States" and "Blacks are getting too demanding in their push for equal rights." Skepticism about the extent of ongoing discrimination is coded as prejudice. Racial resentment (Kinder and Sanders 1997) operates similarly: items about whether black people should work their way up "without special favors" and whether the government should make "special efforts" to help black people measure the degree to which respondents endorse the institutionalized assumption on racial policy. Modern sexism (Swim et al. 1995) applies the same structure to gender: items measuring whether respondents believe gender discrimination remains a serious problem. Skepticism is sexism. In each of these cases, the instrument does not encode the premise as a background assumption that shapes how responses are interpreted. It measures the premise directly and treats rejection of it as the psychological phenomenon under study.

The field has already entered a debate over the point made here. Tetlock (1994) argued that symbolic racism scales confounded racial prejudice with principled conservatism-that the same opposition to racial policies could be reached through genuinely racist motivation, through ideological commitment to limited government and equal treatment, through skepticism about the efficacy of race-targeted programs, or through some combination, and that the scales could not distinguish between these pathways. Sniderman and Tetlock (1986) and Sniderman and Piazza (1993) made parallel arguments about racial policy attitudes more broadly. Sears (1994) responded that principled conservatism was itself a rationalization of racial resentment-that the ostensibly principled positions were motivated by racial hostility operating beneath conscious awareness. Tetlock identified the right problem-the instruments encode a contested premise and measure disagreement with it as prejudice. What he lacked was an account of how that encoding got there-why an entire family of constructs, developed independently across different subfields and decades, shared the same structural feature. This paper provides that account.

In the second group are constructs where the settled premise is required for the interpretation. For instance, the System Justification scale includes items like: "In general, the American political system operates as it should" and "American society needs to be radically restructured" (Jost and Banaji 1994). The scale interprets system defense as anxiety management-a way to avoid confronting injustice. But if institutional arrangements might serve important coordinating functions despite producing unequal results, then defending the system could reflect a theory about the costs of destabilization rather than psychological defensiveness.

Also in this group is the Social Dominance Orientation scale. It presents items like: "We should do what we can to equalize conditions for different groups" and "Group equality should be our ideal" (Pratto et al. 1994). Here, the scale interprets disagreement as dominance motivation. But if inequality might simply reflect differences in freely chosen priorities, accumulated cultural capital, or performance on merit-based criteria, then opposing engineered equalization could reflect concern about dismantling functional sorting mechanisms. The construct gains a substantial proportion of its psychological interpretation from the premise that inequality itself is a problem.

The Implicit Association Test (IAT) also belongs in this category, though it operates somewhat differently. The IAT measures reaction-time differences when pairing demographic categories with positive versus negative attributes (Anthony G. Greenwald et al. 1998). Without the disparities equal discrimination assumption in place, the IAT reveals something about automatic cognitive associations-but given its weak predictive relationship to discriminatory behavior (Oswald et al. 2013), it's unclear why those associations should be interpreted as requiring correction. The premise described here gives the IAT its diagnostic and moral significance. The logic runs as follows: the premise tells us that racism persists and is pervasive-inequality itself is the evidence. At the same time, explicit prejudice has measurably declined (Krysan and Moberg 2021; Schuman et al. 1998). If discrimination is so tenacious but conscious bias has decreased, part of the explanation must be that it operates through unconscious mechanisms. The IAT provides that missing mechanism-it shows how facially neutral decision-making can produce discriminatory outcomes without conscious intent. Faster negative associations with disadvantaged groups become evidence of the unconscious bias that explains persistent disparities. The premise gives the IAT a problem to solve, and the IAT gives the premise a causal mechanism it had lacked. But the entire structure-the interpretation of automatic associations as evidence of unconscious discrimination requiring correction-gains its force from treating the underlying assumption as settled.

The third group consists of constructs where the premise shapes which positions get classified as psychological phenomenon. The Belief in a Just World scale includes items like: "People usually get what they deserve in this world" and "By and large, people deserve what they get" (M. Lerner 1980). The scale interprets agreement as motivated bias-a defensive mechanism to preserve psychological comfort. But if disparities might reflect factors beyond discrimination-if the relationship between effort and outcomes, while imperfect, is real and substantial-then believing in a roughly just world could be either a defensible empirical position about how institutions work or a principled philosophical commitment about desert and fairness, not necessarily a distorted denial of systemic injustice.

The final construct in this third group is authoritarianism. Authoritarianism research presents what might appear to be a challenge to the present argument. After all, Adorno, Frenkel-Brunswik, Levinson, and Sanford published The Authoritarian Personality (1950) as a study of the psychological conditions that had given rise to fascism-decades before the timeline for the present analysis begins. Not only that, but that same work was written by German Jewish emigrés with intellectual roots in the Frankfurt School, not in American debates about racial inequality. And several subsequent contributors to literature were also based outside the United States-Altemeyer at the University of Manitoba (1981), Duckitt at the University of the Witwatersrand and later the University of Auckland (2001, 1994).

Neither component, however, negates the argument presented here. The first thing to notice is the marked difference between Adorno's original construct and what the field uses today. Adorno's F-scale was defined against a specific historical referent: susceptibility to fascist ideology in the aftermath of its most catastrophic expression. Whatever its methodological problems (Rokeach 1960), it was studying something specific. Altemeyer's right-wing authoritarianism (RWA) scale, developed in the 1980s and refined over subsequent decades, measures something broader. It captures deference to established authority, adherence to conventional norms, and aggression toward norm-violators (1981, 1996). While Adorno's construct was defined against fascism, Altemeyer's is defined against a general baseline in which support for institutional authority and conventional norms is treated as a dispositional tendency-a baseline that structurally overlaps with substantive conservative positions about institutional order in ways Adorno's original formulation did not.

Even the most sophisticated elaborations of the construct do not escape the underlying dependence on the premise. Duckitt's (2001) influential dual-process model distinguishes authoritarianism (linked to social conservatism and threat sensitivity) from social dominance orientation (linked to economic conservatism and competitive worldview). This refines the taxonomy of conservative psychology-but it does not question whether the positions being measured are psychology at all. Both factors remain defined as psychological dispositions. The model asks what kind of psychological tendency conservatism reflects-threat-driven conformity or dominance-driven competition-not whether the positions being measured might reflect substantive disagreement about how institutions function.

As for the international origins of the researchers, it is entirely plausible that the non-U.S.-based scholars were shaped by the same institutional transformation. After all, the field's journals, conferences, and citation networks are largely centered in American and Anglophone institutions that had absorbed it. That said, I can't refute the possibility that the development of the non-U.S. based work was the result of factors independent of what I've described here.

What I can say is that regardless of how the construct definitions were arrived at, their psychological interpretations rest on some background assumption about the authorities and institutions in question. Interpreting deference to authority as a syndrome rather than a substantive position requires assuming that the authorities are illegitimate. Interpreting conventionalism as rigidity rather than as reasoned commitment requires assuming that the norms being endorsed are unjust. Interpreting aggression toward norm-violators as punitive intolerance rather than as concern for social order requires assuming that the norms being violated deserve to be violated. In each case, the interpretation depends on a prior judgment that the institutional arrangements the respondent is defending are wrong-and the most readily available ground for that judgment is the premise that institutions producing disparate outcomes are discriminatory. If that is not the assumption underwriting these interpretations, the burden falls on the field to identify what is-because without some such assumption, the psychological interpretation has no foundation. Deference becomes a position, conventionalism becomes a commitment, and norm enforcement becomes a policy preference. The syndrome disappears.

The fourth and final group is comprised of Moral Foundations Theory. Before turning to that analysis in the next section, there's one more point worth surfacing explicitly. By every standard criterion of psychometric science, the measures described above passed-and they should, because they are well-constructed instruments that reliably measure what their constructs define. But if all these instruments encode the same set underlying assumption, then convergent validity is expected regardless of whether any of them measure the psychological universals they claim. The agreement comes from treating a shared assumption as fact, and not necessarily because they all track the same underlying psychological reality.

IV) Case Study: Moral Foundations Theory

Moral Foundations Theory was developed not to study prejudice or discrimination but to understand the structure of moral judgment across cultures. It was explicitly designed to take conservative moral concerns seriously-to credit conservatives with additional moral foundations rather than diagnosing them with psychological deficits. If any construct in political psychology should be immune to the encoding described in this paper, it is MFT. And yet, as this section demonstrates, the same mechanism operates-not through the pathologizing of conservative positions, but through the definitions of foundational moral concepts that determine how responses are categorized. MFT encodes the institutional framework not in what it says about conservatives, but in what it assumes about what harm and fairness mean. In other words, the premise operates through the taxonomic structure itself.

A) The Central Finding and Its Interpretation

MFT originally identified five moral foundations: Care/harm, Fairness/cheating, Loyalty/betrayal, Authority/subversion, and Sanctity/degradation. Using the Moral Foundations Questionnaire and related measures, Graham et al. (2009 Graham et al. ( , 2013 Graham et al. ( , 2011) report a consistent pattern across large samples, multiple methods, and diverse cultural contexts. Graham, Nosek, and Haidt (2012) found it in a representative U.S. sample. Graham et al. (2011) found it across eleven world regions. McAdams et al. (2008) found corresponding themes in life narrative interviews with politically engaged adults. The pattern holds across self-report relevance ratings, agreement with moral judgment statements, willingness to violate foundation-related taboos for money, and content analysis of church sermons (Graham et al. 2009).

Liberals, on the MFT account, build their morality primarily on two foundations-Care and Fairness-which protect individual welfare and rights. Conservatives build their morality on all five. The liberal moral domain is narrower; the conservative moral domain is broader. Haidt and Graham (2007) proposed that the MFT framework explains many features of the American culture war: the specific issues that become battlefields, the intractability of the debates, and the inability of the two sides to understand each other. Because liberals and conservatives are building their moral arguments on different foundations, arguments about specific issues-gay marriage, abortion, flag desecration, welfare policy-fail to persuade across the divide. Each side finds the other's reasoning not merely wrong but unintelligible, because the foundations on which it rests are not shared.

Before turning to the item-level analysis, a specific modification to MFT warrants brief attention here: the addition of a sixth foundation, Liberty/Oppression. Because the sixth foundation was proposed largely in response to concerns that the original five did not adequately capture libertarian and conservative notions of fairness (Iyer et al. 2012), it might appear to address the critique presented here. There are at least two reasons it does not. First, the addition of the liberty/oppression foundation still accepts the premise that the Harm foundation already captures the harm concern correctly, and that conservative priorities constitute additional, separate moral content.

Second, for those operating within the recoded moral vocabulary, oppression too has a broader definition. Institutions producing disparate outcomes are oppressive-they are discriminatory systems restricting opportunity. If liberals and conservatives fundamentally disagree about what constitutes oppression in the first place-whether it's institutions producing unequal outcomes or government coercion restricting individual choice-then the Liberty/Oppression foundation cannot distinguish between these competing definitions. It measures endorsement of liberty without resolving which version of oppression respondents are using. Put simply, the Liberty/Oppression foundation does not escape the definitional recoding; it gets absorbed into it.

i) Harm: What counts As Suffering

The Harm/Care foundation includes statements that appear to be straightforwardly about preventing suffering: "Whether or not someone suffered emotionally," "Whether or not someone cared for someone weak or vulnerable," "Whether or not someone was cruel," "Compassion for those who are suffering is the most crucial virtue." But the recoding invites definitions that diverge along political lines.

When a respondent rates the relevance of "whether or not someone suffered emotionally," are they thinking about microaggressions, systemic exclusion, and structural disadvantage? Or about direct interpersonal cruelty, personal loss, and identifiable trauma? Similarly, "caring for someone weak or vulnerable" could mean advocating for policies that address structural inequities or helping specific individuals in immediate need. "Cruelty" could mean perpetuating discriminatory systems through inaction or deliberate interpersonal mistreatment.

The item assumes words like "suffering," "vulnerable," and "cruelty" have single, shared meanings when the redefinition has made them contested. And yet, suffering attributed to racist institutional systems carries moral weight beyond the suffering itself-it indicates pervasive injustice requiring structural remedy. Further, if systemic harm operates through every institution producing disparate outcomes, it's omnipresent; if harm means interpersonal cruelty, it's occasional. A liberal thinking of systemic suffering and a conservative thinking of interpersonal suffering may care equally about preventing suffering while differing fundamentally about how frequently the morally relevant harm occurs.

ii) Fairness: What Constitutes Justice The fairness foundation includes items whose interpretation also depends entirely on which theory of fairness the respondent holds. Consider examples like, "Whether or not some people were treated differently than others," "Whether or not someone acted unfairly," "Whether or not someone was denied his or her rights," "When the government makes laws, the number one principle should be ensuring that everyone is treated fairly," and "Justice is the most important requirement for a society." Each uses a concept-"treated differently," "unfairly," "rights," "treated fairly," "justice"-that has been redefined in the later framework. Within the redefined concepts, acting unfairly includes implementing facially neutral policies that produce disparate outcomes. Rights include proportional representation and equal results, not just procedural protections. Being "treated fairly" means experiencing equitable outcomes, not identical treatment. Justice requires addressing group-level inequalities in results. Liberals may rate these items as more relevant than conservatives not because fairness matters more to them, but because they see institutional unfairness as ubiquitous and indicative of ongoing racism, while conservatives do not.

iii) Authority, Loyalty, and Sanctity: What Makes Institutions Legitimate

The remaining three foundations measure endorsement of institutions, groups, and norms. Consider the authority foundation which measures the subjective relevance of statements like "Respect for authority is something all children need to learn," "If I were a soldier and disagreed with my commanding officer's orders, I would obey anyway because that is my duty," and "Whether or not an action caused chaos or disorder." These measure attitudes toward authority structures-educational systems, corporations, law enforcement, military hierarchies, governmental institutions. But the interpretation diverges entirely based on whether those institutions are seen as legitimate despite producing unequal outcomes or illegitimate because of those outcomes.

The loyalty foundation operates through similar logic. Statements include, "People should be loyal to their family members, even when they have done something wrong," "It is more important to be a team player than to express oneself," and "Whether or not someone did something to betray his or her group." Within the recoded framework, in-group loyalty can perpetuate exclusion, protect harmful traditions, and privilege belonging over justice. Any perceived benefits of collective welfare or mutual support will be morally swamped by the need to advance justice.

The sanctity foundation extends the pattern with statements like, "I would call some acts wrong on the grounds that they are unnatural," "Chastity is an important and valuable virtue," and "People should not do things that are disgusting, even if no one is harmed." Under the new framework, traditional norms become oppressive restrictions on individual freedom. We can expect any perceived benefit through their provision of stability to be swamped by the moral urgency of fighting oppression. The effects are pervasive: if traditional norms enforce oppression wherever they operate, sanctity concerns encounter injustice constantly. For someone who has absorbed the redefined concepts, authority, loyalty, and sanctity become concerns to be minimized rather than prioritized. Here again, the liberal-conservative difference in relevance ratings follows naturally from these definitional and pervasiveness differences, not necessarily from different moral foundations.

Consider the survey item that reads, "Men and women each have different roles to play in society." This item appears on the Authority subscale. It can be interpreted as either endorsing discriminatory gender norms or recognizing that men and women might have some different strengths, preferences, or priorities on average. For respondents who have absorbed the redefined concepts, gender disparities constitute evidence of discrimination. That means any endorsement of different roles sustains that discrimination. By contrast, a respondent who believes that some average differences between men and women are real and not entirely socially constructed would endorse the item for reasons the instrument cannot distinguish from endorsement of discriminatory norms.

B) Interpretive Ambiguity and an Alternative Explanation

The pattern MFT reports is real and replicable. Liberals consistently prioritize Care and Fairness; conservatives distribute their concern more evenly across all five foundations. The interpretation of this pattern has been that liberals and conservatives have genuinely different moral architectures-different kinds of concern, built on different psychological substrates. But an alternative explanation is that the two groups define these concepts systematically differentlycapturing two sides of a documented historical shift rather than positing innate psychological differences. If this is correct, the MFT pattern reflects genuine disagreement-but disagreement about definitions, not about what matters morally.

And yet, the data cannot distinguish between these two interpretations. A conservative scoring low on the relevance of "whether someone suffered emotionally" while scoring high on the relevance of "respect for authority" looks identical whether they (a) care less about suffering and more about obedience, or (b) define suffering as interpersonal cruelty rather than systemic disadvantage and therefore encounter it less frequently as a morally relevant consideration. While the instrument records both as the same pattern, the psychological meaning differs entirely. Before proceeding to the next section, it is worth distinguishing the present argument from existing critiques of MFT-because the most likely reason for a reader to dismiss it prematurely is to mistake it for one of them.

C) How the Present Argument Differs from Existing MFT Critiques

Critiques of MFT tend to fall into two types: those proposing alternative taxonomic arrangements (Janoff-Bulman and Carnes 2013; Rai and Fiske 2011) and those arguing that all morality reduces to a single concern with harm (Gray et al. 2012). The present argument is neither. Unlike the taxonomic critiques, it does not rearrange the foundations-it questions whether the move that separates harm from the binding foundations reflects psychological reality or institutional history. And unlike Gray's monism, which claims that everyone already processes all moral judgments through a harm template whether they know it or not, the present argument claims something more specific: that MFT's definitions of harm and fairness instead encode a substantive disagreement about what those concepts mean.

It is worth addressing one specific empirical objection directly. MFT researchers report that the liberal-conservative pattern replicates cross-culturally (Graham et al. 2011), which might appear to favor the innate foundations interpretation over the institutional framework explanation. But this follows only if cross-cultural consistency is taken as evidence of innateness rather than diffusion. The two interpretations make identical predictions when the framework has spread internationally. As discussed in the previous sections, the redefined American institutional vocabulary traveled through universities, international organizations, multinational corporations, and professional associations to become the shared conceptual infrastructure of credentialed professional life across much of the world (Redstone 2025). Finding the liberal-conservative MFT pattern in populations not exposed to this institutional redefinition would support the MFT interpretation, but, to date, this has not been the design of the cross-cultural replication studies.

V) What This Argument Is Not

The argument advanced in this paper is susceptible to multiple misreadings, each of which would allow the field to dismiss the critique without engaging it. This section addresses the most likely misreadings directly.

First, nothing in this paper is meant as an argument that the world is just, that authority is always legitimate, that hierarchies invariably serve the common good, or that disparate outcomes never reflect discrimination. The argument is not that the conservative causal theory of harm prevention is right and the progressive causal theory is wrong. It is that both are theoriessubstantive positions about how social institutions function, advanced by people who believe them to be true, supported by arguments and evidence that reasonable people can evaluate-and that the field has treated one as a perception and the other as a fact.

Second, I am not suggesting that the field's findings are entirely artifactual. The IAT is measuring something real-people genuinely do form automatic cognitive associations, and those associations vary across individuals. Authoritarianism may also be measuring something real-some people likely are more rigid, more threat-sensitive, more intolerant of ambiguity than others, and these tendencies genuinely correlate with political conservatism. The claim is not that these phenomena are illusory. It is that the instruments cannot determine how much of what they measure reflects these genuine psychological tendencies and how much reflects substantive disagreement with the premise built into the construct definitions. The field has treated the full measured effect as psychology. Some unknown portion of it is disagreement.

The present argument should also not to be misunderstood to be describing a problem that can be fixed by simply writing better survey items. The problem resides in the construct definitions, the theoretical frameworks, and the interpretive conventions that determine what the items are supposed to measure. Writing new items within the existing construct definitions will produce new items that encode the same institutional vocabulary.

Diversifying samples faces the same limitation. Including more conservative respondents in validation studies will not correct the confound, because the validation procedures assess whether the instrument predicts what the theory says it should predict-and the theory already encodes the institutional framework. A more diverse sample will produce the same findings if measured with the same instruments, and the findings will pass the same validation checks. The sample is not the problem. The conceptual architecture is.

Even adversarial collaboration-widely and rightly advocated as a corrective to confirmation bias-faces constraints here. Adversarial collaboration works when the adversaries disagree about predictions but share a conceptual vocabulary in which the predictions can be stated. The present argument identifies a case in which the conceptual vocabulary itself is what is contested.

None of this means the problem is unsolvable. It means that the solution requires something more fundamental than methodological refinement within the existing paradigm. It requires the field to historicize its own conceptual vocabulary-to recognize that the definitions of harm, fairness, discrimination, and related terms that it treats as scientific starting points are products of a specific institutional transformation, and to develop practices for examining how those definitions shape the instruments through which ideological differences are measured.

VI) Implications

If the argument of this paper is correct, the field of social and moral psychology faces a problem that is uncomfortable but not hopeless.

A) Reconceptualizing Moral Disagreement

The most consequential implication concerns how the field conceptualizes the object of its study. Political and moral psychology has overwhelmingly treated ideological differences as differences in psychology-in personality traits, cognitive styles, emotional sensitivities, and moral intuitions. The present argument suggests that a substantial portion of what the field has classified as psychological difference is better understood as stemming from substantive disagreement about whether systems generate disparate outcomes.

Such a reframing would transform the research questions the field asks. Instead of treating deference to authority as a dispositional tendency requiring psychological explanation (a question that presupposes authority-valuing is a psychological phenomenon), the field could ask "what causal model of institutional harm prevention leads some people to prioritize institutional authority, and what evidence would they find relevant to updating that model?" Instead of assuming the MFQ's definition of harm is neutral and studying why conservatives score lower on it (a question that presupposes the definition is both correct and shared), the field could ask "what are the different causal theories about where harm comes from and how it is prevented, and how do these theories map onto the political spectrum?" Such a framework could also open research questions that are currently invisible. For instance: What factors predict the belief that disparate outcomes reflect discrimination versus other factors and vice versa? Does the answer differ based on direct versus statistical evidence, or personal versus aggregate experience? When do people attribute suffering to individual circumstances versus systemic injustice, and what role do institutional performance and personal mobility play in that attribution?

B) What the Interpretations Actually Require

If the argument of this paper is correct-if the standard psychological interpretations depend on treating a contested causal claim as settled-then those interpretations require something they do not currently have: independent evidence for the claim they have been treating as given. Each interpretation would need to be justified on grounds that do not assume what is in dispute. To interpret deference to authority as a personality syndrome, the field would need to establish that the institutions in question are unjust on grounds independent of the contested premise. To interpret hierarchy endorsement as dominance motivation, it would need to demonstrate that the hierarchies are discriminatory without assuming disparate outcomes prove discrimination. To interpret institutional defense as motivated rationalization, it would need to establish system injustice without circularity. To interpret just-world belief as cognitive bias, it would need to show that the world is comprehensively unjust on criteria independent of disparate outcomes. To interpret automatic associations as unconscious prejudice requiring correction, it would need to demonstrate that those associations predict discriminatory behavior -which the evidence suggests they do not (Oswald et al. 2013).

C) Rethinking What Measurement Can and Cannot Do

Ideological beliefs and moral values are the terrain on which the deepest disagreements in a pluralistic society play out. The question of what counts as "harm," what counts as "fairness," and what counts as a "bias" are not technical questions with consensus answers. They are precisely the questions that ideological disagreement is about. When the field builds contested concepts into construct definitions and then uses the resulting instruments to measure ideological differences, it does not stand outside the disagreement as a neutral observer. It takes a position within it and then reports findings that confirm the position it took-a circularity invisible to researchers who experience the recoded definitions as morally neutral scientific precision. The invisibility makes the problem intractable. The recoded framework doesn't announce itself as a framework; it presents itself as reality.

This does not mean the field cannot study political ideology. It means the field needs a different relationship to its own constructs when studying ideology than when studying intelligence or personality. It needs to recognize that respondents who reject the framework embedded in the instruments are not necessarily displaying a psychological deficit-they may be disagreeing with the instrument's premises. And it needs to develop methods for distinguishing these two possibilities, rather than assuming that the instrument is always right and the respondent who scores unexpectedly is always revealing something about their psychology.

The field of social and moral psychology has produced a remarkable body of work over the past half century. That work includes genuine discoveries about human cognition, motivation, and social behavior. But it has also produced a systematic distortion-a structural confound that has inflated the apparent psychological differences between ideological groups and obscured the degree to which those differences reflect substantive disagreement about how institutions work. Recognizing this distortion is not an indictment of the field. It is an invitation to do better-to build instruments and frameworks that can study moral and political disagreement without unwittingly encoding one side's institutional vocabulary as the language of science.

VII) Conclusion

This paper began with an observation: some of the most influential research programs in social and political psychology share a structural feature. Symbolic racism, modern racism, racial resentment, and modern sexism code skepticism about the extent of ongoing discrimination as prejudice. System Justification Theory treats defense of existing institutions as motivated cognition. Social Dominance Orientation treats endorsement of functional hierarchy as a dominance motive. Implicit bias research treats deviation from an egalitarian baseline as unconscious prejudice. Belief in a Just World research treats the conviction that the system broadly works as a cognitive bias. Authoritarianism research treats deference to institutional authority as a personality syndrome. And Moral Foundations Theory treats concern about institutional order, group solidarity, and normative boundaries as moral content categorically distinct from concern about harm. In every case, the standard psychological interpretation depends on treating a contested premise -that disparate outcomes constitute evidence of discrimination -as settled. Once that premise is treated as contested, each construct's interpretation becomes observationally equivalent to substantive disagreement about how institutions function.

The argument developed here shows that the standard psychological interpretations of these constructs are not uniquely determined by the data they produce but depend on treating a contested premise as settled. It then offers a historically grounded account of how that premise became embedded in the conceptual vocabulary of the field. The force of the argument lies not in claiming that this is the only possible explanation, but in demonstrating that prevailing interpretations rely on assumptions that remain open to reasonable disagreement.

The account of how the premise became embedded in the field's construct definitions is necessarily more speculative than the demonstration that the constructs' standard interpretations depend on it. The causal case is strongest for constructs developed within American institutions after the transformation was complete-system justification, social dominance orientation, implicit bias, and the symbolic racism family-and weakest for authoritarianism, whose intellectual lineage predates and is independent of the transformation described here. But even where the historical pathway is uncertain, the analytical point holds: the standard psychological interpretations depend on a premise that remains contested, and the field has not reckoned with what that dependence means for the conclusions it has drawn.

The mischaracterization described here has consequences beyond the academy. It has provided the left in particular with a scientific vocabulary for understanding conservatives as morally alien at best, racism apologists at worst. But if the argument of this paper is correct, the portrait is wrong. Liberals and conservatives may well share similar moral concerns. They may not. They may disagree about whether suffering is best addressed by dismantling hierarchies or maintaining them, by equalizing outcomes or preserving the institutional structures that produce unequal but broadly beneficial ones, by expanding the domain of recognized harm or by attending to the harms that follow from too-rapid institutional change. These are the kind of disagreements a democracy must weather, not pathologize.

Correction requires the recognition that the field's most trusted instruments may encode the very thing they were designed to study. Instruments built from contested definitions cannot neutrally measure disagreement about those definitions. None of this is easy. But the first step is simple enough. It is to notice the pattern-to see that across the major research programs of social and political psychology, spanning decades of independent work by different researchers using different methods, the same structural feature appears: one side's institutional theory is treated as a perception of reality, the other side's is treated as a psychological symptom. Once that pattern is visible, the question is no longer whether something has gone wrong, but what to do about it.
