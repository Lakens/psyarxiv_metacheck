This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. Abstract

Theories suggest that capturing people's motives (the 'why') for regulating allows for a better understanding of how people choose to regulate their emotions. Yet, little empirical research systematically examines the link between motives and regulation strategies. Addressing this gap, we conducted an experience-sampling study to: 1) capture the prevalence of a wide range of regulatory motives (hedonic, instrumental) in everyday life and (2) test whether motives were associated with the selection of six regulation strategies, controlling for affect intensity. Data were collected in Australia in 2024, from an adult community sample (NID=303; Nobs=3496; Mage = 26.11; 80% women, mostly identifying as East Asian and South-East Asian). Hedonic motives were most frequent, but instrumental motives were also common. Regulatory motives showed distinct strategy selection profiles: prohedonic motives were linked with greater distraction and cognitive reappraisal, whereas contrahedonic motives were rare, and only predicted greater rumination. Different instrumental motives were differentially associated with the use of specific strategies. Only cognitive strategies (reappraisal and rumination) were linked to multiple motives, being significantly associated with prohedonic, contrahedonic (rumination), personal performance, social, epistemic, and eudaimonic motives. By investigating the momentary association between a broad range of motives and regulation strategies, this work suggests that emotion regulation strategies are associated not only with what people feel, but also with why they regulate.

Future empirical work should explicitly consider individuals' regulatory motives, rather than assuming regulatory effectiveness based on hedonic motives.

Associations Between Motives and Emotion Regulation Strategy Use in Everyday Life.

Self-regulation involves coordinating what people want to achieve or experience (why they regulate) with the means they use to reach their desired state (how they regulate; Kruglanski et al., 2002). Emotion regulation can be understood as a specific form of regulation in which emotional states are the target of regulation (Tamir, 2021). Accordingly, the broader self-regulation literature provides a useful theoretical framework for understanding how motives and regulatory means may be coordinated in emotion regulation. Self-regulation perspectives suggest that the strategies people use to regulate their emotions should be systematically associated with the motives they are pursuing, suggesting it is important to examine whether different motives are associated with different patterns of strategy use.

Consistent with these perspectives, theories of emotion regulation propose that people regulate their emotions using a wide range of strategies in service of different motives (Gross, 2015; Tamir, 2016).

However, research examining emotion regulation motives (i.e., the "why") and emotion regulation strategies (i.e., the "how") has often considered them separately. Building on existing research evidence, this study comprehensively examines these links in a large community sample, testing the associations between seven motives and six emotion regulation strategies in everyday life.

Motivated Emotion Regulation

When people notice a gap between their current feelings and how they want to feel, they may actively try to reduce that gap -that is, initiate emotion regulation (Gross, 2015; Tamir, 2016).

Emotion regulation can occur through both non-conscious, automatic mechanisms, and conscious, deliberate processes (Braunstein et al., 2017; Gyurak et al., 2011). We focus on the latter, examining instances in which individuals deliberately attempt to influence their feelings, regulating their emotions to obtain desired emotional end-states -emotion goals (e.g., I want to feel less anxious). These goals serve higher-order end-states -emotion regulation motives (e.g., I want to feel less anxious so that my presentation goes well; Tamir, 2016).

To classify these motives, Tamir (2016) proposed a taxonomy of two broad emotion regulation motives inspired by the desired end-states that emotions are likely to influence. The first category is hedonic motives, which are satisfied by the experience of desired emotional states (e.g., regulating your emotions to feel happiness for its own sake). The second category is instrumental motives, which capitalize on what emotions offer beyond pleasure and pain (e.g., I want to feel happy because it will help me finish my essay). Whilst immediate pain-avoidance and pleasure-gain is not the core focus of instrumental motives (e.g., vocational performance), satisfying these motives may promote future pleasure or pain (e.g., having finished my essay, I might feel good about it).

Theory suggests that motives guide the emotion regulation process and should therefore be linked to the strategies people use to regulate their emotions. A growing literature has begun to document the frequency of different motives in daily life (e.g., Kalokerinos et al., 2017; Springstein & English, 2025), but only a handful of studies have directly tested links between motives and strategies.

Understanding these links is important for clarifying how people tailor their emotion regulation to achieve different goals. For example, during a challenging work meeting, a person motivated to appear professional may choose to hide their emotions, whereas someone motivated to feel better may use cognitive reappraisal or distraction.

Preliminary findings support the idea that how people regulate depends, in part, on why they regulate (Kruglanski et al., 2002). More specifically, empirical research has suggested that different emotion regulation strategies work best to achieve different goals, from reducing or increasing emotional intensity (Millgram et al., 2019; Sheppes et al., 2014), to meeting social expectations (Greenaway & Kalokerinos, 2017), to dealing with uncontrollable situations (Haines et al., 2016).

To our knowledge, only one paper examines these links more directly, investigating whether endorsement of four types of motives (prohedonic, contrahedonic, pro-social and impression management) were associated with emotion regulation strategies (Eldesouky & English, 2018).

However, this paper investigated habitual endorsement of goals (Studies 1 and 2) and endorsement of goals across the day (Study 3, 9-day daily diary), rather than linking motives and strategies within particular moments. Further, these studies investigated only three (prohedonic, contrahedonic, and social) of the seven classes of motives we outline below. In our investigation, we extend this analysis in two ways. First, we capture the full range of motives, both hedonic and instrumental, to better understand their relative prevalence in everyday life, and examine their links with six emotion regulation strategies. Second, we test these links using experience-sampling methodology. In a large sample, we minimised retrospective bias by sampling people multiple times per day, thereby yielding richer momentary and person-level data. By focusing on assessment periods in which participants reported actively regulating their emotions, we were able to examine associations between motives and strategy selection within a one-hour assessment period, in everyday life. These two additions mean that our work offers the most comprehensive investigation of motive-strategy links undertaken to date.

Emotion Regulation Strategies

Emotion regulation strategies can be distinguished according to both the systems they target and the psychological functions they fulfil (Koole, 2009), and are associated with different emotional and psychological consequences (Aldao et al., 2010; Webb et al., 2012). Strategies may target different components of the emotional process, including attention, cognitive representations, physiological or behavioral responses, or the situation in which an emotion occurs. They may also fulfil different psychological functions, such as changing the intensity or duration of an emotional experience, shifting attention away from an emotion, facilitating goal pursuit, or influencing interpersonal interactions (Koole, 2009). Consequently, strategies that differ in their targets and functions may also be associated with different emotional and psychological consequences. Meta-analytic evidence suggests that the consequences associated with particular strategies vary across individuals and contexts (Aldao et al., 2010), while experimental work indicates that strategy effectiveness can depend on characteristics of the situation, the regulatory goal, and the outcome being considered (Webb et al., 2012).

Of the wide repertoire of ways to influence emotions, we examine six strategies that are commonly studied in daily life (Boemo et al., 2022; Brans et al., 2013; Kalokerinos et al., 2017a).

Despite all representing ways of regulating emotion, these strategies differ substantially in the processes through which regulation occurs. First, strategies differ in the processes through which regulation is enacted (Parkinson & Totterdell, 1999). For example, cognitive reappraisal (changing how one thinks about the situation in a way that alters emotion; Gross & John, 2003) and rumination (dwelling repetitively on emotion and emotion-eliciting events; Nolen-Hoeksema et al., 2008) involve cognitive processing of emotion or emotion-eliciting events. In contrast, situation selection (strategically deciding to engage with-or avoid-particular situations to manage emotions; Livingstone & Isaacowitz, 2015) and expressive suppression (hiding the outward expression of inner emotional experience; Gross & John, 2003) involve changing behavior, by approaching or avoiding situations, or modifying the outward expression of emotion. Distraction, in turn, operates primarily through the deployment of attention, as people direct their attention away from their feelings (Sheppes & Meiran, 2007), often focusing on other thoughts or activities (Nolen-Hoeksema et al., 2008), while social sharing involves communicating emotional experiences to others (Rimé, 2009).

Further, these strategies differ in the extent to which they involve engagement with versus disengagement from emotional material (Parkinson & Totterdell, 1999). For instance, distraction can involve redirecting attention away from an emotion and its eliciting circumstances, whereas rumination involves sustained engagement with them, and reappraisal requires engagement to change their interpretation. Thus, even strategies that involve cognitive processes can differ substantially in how people engage with their emotional experiences.

Another distinction concerns when strategies intervene in the emotion-generation process (Gross, 2015). Situation selection occurs relatively early, by shaping which emotion-eliciting situations people encounter, whereas expressive suppression operates relatively later, by modifying an emotional response once it has been generated. Strategies can therefore target different stages and components of an unfolding emotional episode. Emotion regulation strategies also vary in their interpersonal character: whereas many can be implemented individually, social sharing inherently involves another person and places regulation within an interpersonal context (Niven et al., 2009).

Given that emotion regulation strategies differ in their underlying mechanisms and features, these differences may be relevant to the motives they serve. Accordingly, in this study, we selected six strategies that represent different types of regulation, allowing us to examine how their use is associated with a wide range of everyday emotion regulation motives.

Mapping Strategies to Motives

In this section, we outline how specific features of different strategies may be relevant to achieving different emotion regulation motives. For some motive-strategy links, we were able to make a-priori hypotheses guided by existing work, whereas some of our analyses were necessarily exploratory (See Table 1). While our data do not allow us to discriminate between positive and negative events, we note that our hypotheses were informed by literature focused on emotion regulation of negative events. This is because in daily life emotion regulation is used much more in response to negative events and emotions (Brans et al., 2013), and emotion regulation strategy use has been much more comprehensively studied in this context (Webb et al., 2012; although positive and neutral events elicit important regulatory processes, e.g. Quoidbach et al., 2015).

Prohedonic and contrahedonic motives. Prohedonic motives involve increasing immediate pleasure or decreasing immediate pain to promote hedonic balance. In contrast, processes decreasing pleasure and increasing pain align with contrahedonic motives. People generally find positive emotions pleasant and negative emotions unpleasant (Fredrickson, 1998; Larsen, 2000). This suggests that when wanting to feel pleasure, people should tend to use strategies that effectively down-regulate negative emotions, and up-regulate positive emotions. Some strategies better afford the increase or decrease of negative or positive emotion than others: Experimental work has demonstrated that rumination is associated with increased negative emotion, while reappraisal, distraction, and situation modification are associated with decreased negative emotion (Baker & Berenbaum, 2007; Webb et al., 2012).

Perhaps unsurprisingly, the research linking motives and strategies is deepest for hedonic motives. Four lines of research have directly addressed this question. First, across several studies, people who were motivated to decrease their negative emotion selected distraction, and people who were motivated to increase their negative emotion selected rumination (Millgram et al., 2019; 2023). Second, in daily life, wanting to change mood was associated with distraction and reappraisal, particularly during low points (English et al., 2017). Third, endorsing hedonic motives, either habitually or daily, was associated with situation selection and distraction (Eldesouky & English, 2019), reappraisal (Brandao et al., 2023; Eldesouky & English, 2019; Shao et al., 2024), and social sharing (Brandao et al,. 2023). Last, the relationship between prohedonic motives and expressive suppression is inconsistent, and may depend on contextual moderators such as gender and age. For instance, Brandao and colleagues (2023) found a negative association among adults, whereas among children, prohedonic motives were associated with increased suppression in boys but not girls (Shao et al., 2024).

Taken together, these findings suggest that people may be aware of how emotion regulation strategies influence their emotional experiences, given their tendency to use strategies that reduce negative emotions. As hypothesized in Table 1, to the degree that people are aware of these benefits, when they have prohedonic motives, they should be more likely to select strategies that are more effective at decreasing negative emotion. Vice versa, when they have contrahedonic motives, people should be more likely to select strategies that are more effective at increasing negative emotion.

Performance motives. Optimal performance usually requires active engagement in a task, and thus, strategies that facilitate active engagement with an emotional stimulus are likely to best afford performance motives (Beal et al., 2005; Leroy et al., 2012). Both cognitive and behavioral engagement strategies could prove useful, given that performance outcomes can be cognitive (e.g., acing a math test) or behavioral (e.g., winning a race). On the behavioral side, we hypothesise a link between performance motives and situation selection. By selecting situations that minimise potential stressors or distractions and maximise factors that promote focus and confidence, individuals can create conditions that align their emotional state with their performance objectives. For example, an athlete might opt to arrive early at a competition venue, to reduce pre-performance jitters, which might otherwise negatively impact their performance.

Moving to the cognitive side, we suggest a link between performance motives and reappraisal.

Reappraisal is an adaptable strategy that can be modified using different tactics to a variety of useful ends (McRae et al., 2012). For example, if a student changes the way they are thinking about a tough math problem, they might reduce their anxiety while also finding new angles for solving the problem. Supporting this view, empirical studies have found that reappraisal is more frequently used when individuals-both adults and adolescents-endorsed performance-oriented goals (Brandao et al., 2023; English et al., 2017; Shao et al., 2024). These findings offer converging support for the idea that reappraisal enables emotion regulation in ways that maintain task focus and promote goal pursuit.

Other strategies have also been shown to relate to performance motives. Expressive suppression may be used to prevent emotions from interfering with task execution-indeed, performance motives have been associated with greater use of suppression among adolescents (although this association was only significant in boys, not girls; Shao et al., 2024). However, despite its potential short-term utility, suppression often backfires when it comes to sustained performance success (Benita et al., 2021; 2023; Low et al., 2017). Similarly, English and colleagues (2017) found that individuals were more likely to use distraction when endorsing performance-oriented instrumental goals (i.e. completing work). Like suppression, distraction may help people maintain focus on a task and productivity by temporarily disengaging from emotional experiences. Further, some studies have found that social sharing can be beneficial for performance in various contexts (e.g., Klep et al., 2010; Niven et al., 2012; 2013), and this relationship may extend to performance in everyday situations.

Functional theories of emotion posit that emotions evolved to facilitate successful goal pursuit (Keltner et al., 2006). Performance motives involve regulating emotion to facilitate the successful execution of one's activities, and are the instrumental motive most frequently endorsed in daily life (Kalokerinos et al., 2017b). There is both theory and emerging empirical evidence speaking to motivestrategy links, but this is mostly from work focused on vocational performance (i.e., academic and occupational performance; Milyavskaya & Koestner, 2011; Milyavskaya & Werner, 2018). Yet, people pursue performance goals in a range of other personal contexts, such as hobbies, health, and household management. Given the variability of personal contexts, it is possible that the regulatory strategies used in vocational contexts may differ from those that would best fit with personal performance contexts. For example, in vocational contexts, professional composure may take priority and be pursued through suppression, whereas when pursuing performance goals in hobbies (e.g. playing sport; Lane et al., 2011), it might be most effective to employ strategies that actively modulate arousal and expression, for instance by channeling negative emotions rather than dampening them. Accordingly, distinguishing between vocational and personal performance motives is important for understanding whether and how people use different strategies across different domains.

Social motives.

Emotions have social functions at the level of the individual, dyad, group, and culture (Keltner & Haidt, 1999). Social motives involve capitalizing on these functions, using emotions to facilitate relational goals. There are three strategies targeting emotion expression that are likely to afford social motives. First, people often regulate their emotion using interpersonal strategies (Niven et al., 2009; Williams et al., 2018), such as social sharing (Rimé, 2009), likely with social end-states in mind. Second, people might facilitate social interaction by hiding the outward expression of emotion (Greenaway & Kalokerinos, 2018). Indeed, people tend to use suppression strategically for conflict avoidance and impression management (Brandao et al., 2023; Eldesouky & English, 2018; English et al., 2017 , Wilms et al., 2020), especially when holding a lower position in the social hierarchy (Catterson et al., 2017). Although generally viewed as a maladaptive strategy, when deployed in a socially strategic way, expressive suppression nets social benefits (Kalokerinos et al., 2014; Kalokerinos et al., 2017c). Third, pro-social goals have been linked with greater use of reappraisal among adults and adolescents (Eldesouky & English, 2018; Shao et al., 2024). Accordingly, we hypothesise that social sharing, expressive suppression, and cognitive reappraisal will be positively associated with social motives.

Epistemic motives.

Emotions serve as a valuable source of information about the world around us, and our inner states (Barrett & Gross, 2001). Epistemic motives may drive people to regulate their emotions as a means of acquiring this information, thereby facilitating learning and understanding.

While there is currently no direct evidence linking epistemic motives with specific emotion regulation strategies, we can draw hypotheses from related processes. Emotional awareness, clarity, and differentiation-all key components of emotion knowledge-all require engagement with emotional experiences (Boden et al., 2013; Kashdan et al., 2015; Lane, 2000). This suggests that epistemic motives will be best afforded by strategies allowing engagement with emotions, rather than those providing diversion from emotions, by redirecting attention elsewhere (Parkinson & Totterdell, 1999).

Three strategies involve emotion engagement, via different paths. First, reappraisal enables the reframing of emotional events in a different light (Gross & John, 2003). This reframing process could enable epistemic outcomes, as viewing an event from different perspectives allows one to gain additional information. Second, rumination is a putatively maladaptive strategy, but it also has a dimension that involves more purposeful inward contemplation, and is associated with deeper self-knowledge (Trapnell & Campbell, 1999; Treynor et al., 2003). Thus, we hypothesize that epistemic motives will be positively associated with both cognitive reappraisal and rumination. Third, social sharing is another strategy that allows individuals to engage with their emotions for the purpose of satisfying epistemic motives. By discussing emotional experiences with others, people can gain new perspectives, validate their feelings, and find ways of framing emotional events through consultation with their social network (Duprez et al., 2014; Finkenauer & Rimé, 1998; Rimé et al., 1998; Rimé, 2020). Accordingly, we hypothesize that through emotional understanding and meaning-making, the engagement strategies of reappraisal, rumination and social sharing will positively link with epistemic motives. Vice versa, because distraction inhibits emotion processing by favouring disengagement, we hypothesize that it will be negatively associated with epistemic motives.

Eudaimonic motives.

Eudaimonic motives involve regulating emotions to promote selfrealization, contributing to a sense of meaning in life (Ryan & Deci, 2001). As with epistemic motives, we suggest that these motives would be best promoted by cognitive engagement strategies (Trapnell & Campbell, 1999)-with reappraisal and rumination being the strategies most likely to facilitate eudaimonic motives. Ruminating fosters deeper engagement with emotion, allowing oneself time to fully process and understand an emotional event in a way that could promote meaning-making. Second, theory has highlighted the role of reappraisal in making meaning from adversity, fostering eudaimonic well-being (Garland et al., 2015; Park, 2010). Thus, reappraisal could facilitate the reframing of events in ways that promote growth and resilience.

The Role of Valence and Intensity of Emotions

The valence of emotions influences how individuals manage their emotional experiences in daily life. For instance, people tend to regulate negative emotions more frequently than positive emotions (Gross et al., 2006; Heiy & Cheavens, 2014), and intense emotions more than less intense ones (Nezlek & Kuppens, 2008). Disengagement strategies are particularly favoured to regulate high-intensity negative emotions and engagement strategies for low-intensity negative emotions (Sheppes et al., 2014).

Thus, the choice of regulation strategy may not only depend on individual motives, but also on the nature of the experienced emotion. To account for these potential influences, we include both positive and negative emotions as covariates in our study, thereby providing more precise estimates of how motives relate to strategies.

The Current Research

In this study, we tested the potential associations between seven emotion regulation motives and six emotion regulation strategies in everyday life, to identify whether people deliberately select different strategies, according to their motives. To test our hypotheses, we used experience sampling data from a large community sample that completed ten surveys per day for a week. When participants reported that they had tried to regulate their emotions in the last hour, they were asked about why they did so, and which strategies they used. This approach meant that we sampled strategies and motives across many measurement occasions, delivering the ideal setting for describing these links comprehensively at the momentary level. In conducting this study, we tested some pre-registered hypotheses (Table 1), guided by existing empirical and theoretical insights, and conducted some analyses as exploratory. Our hypotheses focus on the association between motives and strategy use, while controlling for positive and negative emotional experiences.

We make two main contributions to the field. First, we capture the relative frequency of each category of motives in everyday life. Understanding the prevalence of these motives is crucial to build insights into the primary drivers of emotion regulation in real-world contexts, which can inform both theoretical models and practical interventions. Second, we investigate the relationships between a wide range of motives-including those that have been less explored, such as personal and vocational performance, epistemic, and eudaimonic motives-and the selection of six emotion regulation strategies. This comprehensive approach allows us to paint a more nuanced picture of the complex interplay between diverse motives and emotion regulation strategies as they unfold in real-life contexts. The sample size of the dataset was planned based on an a-priori power analysis conducted using Lafit et al.'s (2021) multilevel power simulation tool. Specifically, the study was designed to have 80% power (with α = .05) to detect a cross-level interaction effect estimated in preliminary analyses of an existing experience sampling study (Pauw et al., 2022; Study 1). Simulations using Lafit et al.'s (2021) tool indicated that a sample size of n=280 participants (with ~70 measurement occasions each) would provide sufficient power. Anticipating a 15% participant attrition, the target sample size was increased to 330. For more details, including pre-processing steps and data exclusion criteria, see the dataset preregistration (https://osf.io/zntdg).

The study recruited 333 participants, but data from seven participants were excluded based on pre-registered criteria (n=1 failed the attention checks; n=1 did not complete any ESM surveys; and n=5 were outside of the AEST timezone during the ESM phase of the study).

Accordingly, we had data from 326 participants recruited in Australia, aged 18 to 66 (M = 26.11, SD = 8.52, 80% identifying as women, 17% as men, 3% as non-binary or other). Most participants identified as East Asian (31%), South-East Asian (25%), White/Caucasian (22%), and South Asian (11%). From that sample, we removed 23 participants (7%) who never reported any occasion of regulation throughout the study (and consequently, had no data on motives for regulation). 2 Accordingly, our analysis included an effective sample size of 303 participants and 3496 observations of instances of emotion regulation (out of 17,049 total surveys completed). We report sample demographics for the effective sample and the excluded sample in Supplementary Materials (Table S1).

Recruitment

Participants were recruited from an Australian University first-year psychology cohort (N = 2), and from the wider Australian community through online and printed advertisements (N=331). After completing a screening survey to assess eligibility, participants who met eligibility criteria (i.e., aged 18 and over; owned a smartphone; located within the AEST time zone, and not currently taking part in another ESM study) could sign up for the baseline part of the study.

Procedure

The study had two parts. Part 1 consisted of a baseline survey. Part 2 started the day after Part 1, and consisted of seven days of ESM surveys. Codebooks for both parts are available at https://osf.io/vhsau.

Part 1: Baseline Survey

Participants first attended an in-person baseline session. They were instructed to download SEMA3 (O'Brien et al., 2024) on their smartphones, which delivered the ESM surveys. After reading the Plain Language Statement and providing informed consent, participants completed self-report measures assessing demographics, and other measures not relevant to the current study. Three attention checks embedded in the baseline survey were used to screen out inattentive participants. Participants were then introduced to the ESM protocol and completed a practice survey using the SEMA3 app to ensure comprehension, with researchers available to support participants with any questions.

Part 2: ESM Surveys

The ESM part began the day after baseline and continued for seven consecutive days.

Participants received ten prompts per day, delivered through SEMA3 on a stratified pseudo-random schedule across waking hours. Prompts were spaced at approximately 90-minute intervals within fixed windows, and participants selected one of three pre-defined schedules to best match their routine. Each prompt triggered a 27-item survey (requiring 2-3 minutes for completion) assessing momentary emotions, moods, goals, and emotion regulation decisions and strategies. Branching was balanced, so that each survey contained an equal number of items, irrespective of participants' responses. Surveys remained open for completion for up to 30 minutes. Researchers emailed participants on days 3 and 7 with content based on participants' compliance (i.e., encouragement and offering assistance to participants with less that 70% compliance; praising those above 70%).

Compliance was 73.67% and the total number of surveys completed was 17,049.

Reimbursement

Community participants were reimbursed a fixed amount (AU$ 20 voucher) for completing the baseline survey, and on a pro-rata basis for ESM survey completion (up to AU$ 50 voucher). University participants received 1% towards a course credit for the baseline session, and up to 2.5% for ESM surveys.

Measures

Demographics

At baseline, participants reported their age, gender, and ethnicity.

Momentary emotion regulation motives

In the ESM surveys, participants were first asked whether they had tried to influence their feelings in the last hour. If participants reported not having tried to influence their feelings, they completed an equal number of subsequent questions as the "yes" branch, but focused on non-regulation.

If participants responded yes, the survey would branch so that they could respond to a single-item question capturing their motives (Table 2). Each motive was presented in plain language (e.g. for prohedonic motives: "to feel better"), with items adapted from published work (Kalokerinos et al., 2017b; Uchida et al., 2024; for personal performance motives: Benita et al., 2021; 2023; 2024).

Responses were dichotomous, whereby selected motives were coded as 1 (and non-selected motives as 0). Participants could select multiple motives at each survey.

Emotion regulation strategies

Participants reported their emotion regulation strategy use, by responding to six single-item questions capturing expressive suppression, distraction, social sharing, situation selection, cognitive reappraisal, and rumination (Table 2). Participants reported their use of six emotion regulation strategies on a slider scale ranging from 0 (not at all) to 100 (very much).

Negative and Positive Affect

Participants rated the intensity of seven negative emotions (sad, tired, lonely, guilty, angry, stressed, and anxious) and five positive emotions (happy, energized, loving, relaxed, grateful), on a slider scale ranging from 0 (not at all) to 100 (very). The selected emotions span a range of valence and arousal, distributing broadly across the circumplex structure of affect (Yik et al., 1999). Items were phrased as "How [emotion] do you feel right now?" Between-person reliabilities were good to excellent (w= 0.92, w = 0.88) while within-person reliabilities were acceptable (w= 0.77, w = 0.68) for both negative and positive affect scales, respectively.

Statistical Analysis

The statistical analysis plan was pre-registered and can be accessed via the Open Science Framework (https://osf.io/k5dgf/overview?view_only=cb60aef953284f8cb837d554a06ca843). All analyses were conducted in R (ver. [4.2.3]), using the lme4 package (ver. [1.1.31]; Bates et al., 2015), and calculating p values using the lmerTest package (Kuznetsova et al., 2013).

Analytic Strategy

To address our first research question, we estimated the frequency and co-occurrence of each emotion regulation motive both at the survey-and the person-level. We calculated Spearman's rho correlations among motives using the psych package (Revelle, 2022).

To investigate the second research question, we ran six multilevel models, each predicting one emotion regulation strategy (expressive suppression, distraction, social sharing, situation selection, cognitive reappraisal, and rumination) from all momentary motives (hedonic, contrahedonic, vocational performance, social, personal performance, epistemic and eudaimonic) entered simultaneously as Level-1 predictors. To aid interpretation, predictors (dichotomous) were not centered (Nezlek, 2011). We preregistered models that included random intercept and random slope for participant, but due to convergence issues, we reduced complexity by only running models with random intercepts. In Supplementary Materials we report all final model specifications (Table S5) and results from these models (Table S6).

In addition, and central to our hypotheses, we ran pre-registered analyses by including the fixed effects of momentary positive and negative affect (person-centered) in the models as covariates (with random slopes), to explore whether motives predicted strategy use independently of the intensity of positive and negative affect felt at that occasion. We report results from this set of models below.

Finally, to examine whether individual differences in the endorsement for each motive also predicted the use of each strategy, we conducted exploratory multilevel models adding people's proportion of endorsement for each motive (level-2 predictors). We report results from this set of models in Supplementary Materials (Table S7). Discussion

Theory posits that emotion regulation is shaped by what people want to achieve, so that the how (i.e., the strategies people use) would depend on the why (i.e., people's motives). We drew on the idea that different emotion regulation strategies may serve different functions, and hypothesised that this functional diversity may be relevant to which strategies are considered most useful in particular contexts, and for particular motives. Accordingly, our study tested whether people's strategy use varies as a function of context-specific needs -linking strategy use to motives such as feeling better, performing better, or self-growth. Leveraging the strengths of experience sampling methods, we collected data from a large and culturally diverse sample, capturing people's momentary affective experiences across multiple naturalistic contexts-at work, at school, spending time with friends and family, or running errands. In doing so, our study builds a fine-grained picture of six strategies and their link to seven motives at the momentary level.

Prevalence of Hedonic and Instrumental Motives in Everyday Life

The first aim of this study was to capture the natural prevalence of motives for emotion regulation as people go about their lives. In line with the existing literature, in over half of the overall regulation initiation occurrences, people reported aiming regulation at feeling better, and only very rarely regulating with the opposite goal in mind (Kalokerinos et al., 2017b; Tamir, 2009). The prevalence of hedonic motives underscores the well documented trend that people are generally motivated to approach pleasure and avoid pain (Larsen, 2000; Tamir, 2016). The infrequency of contrahedonic regulation raises questions about the circumstances under which people might consider feeling worse as necessary and the potential adaptive outcomes of such choices (Tamir & Ford, 2012; Riediger et al., 2009; Parrott, 1993).

Also consistent with previous work, instrumental motives occurred relatively often and with low overlap with one another (Kalokerinos et al., 2017b), offering empirical support for the utility of Tamir's taxonomy of motives (2016). Indeed, vocational motives emerged as the second most common driver for regulation, indicating that managing performance in vocational settings is an important motivation for regulation. Our findings also potentially reflect the fact that people spend a considerable amount of their day in vocational contexts such as school and work (Beal, 2014). Personal performance motives were also frequent, demonstrating the perceived importance of emotion regulation to non-work-related goals (e.g., housekeeping, well-being activities, sport and hobbies, etc.). Future research would benefit from further exploring personal performance motives as distinct from vocational motives when testing the role of motives in emotion regulation processes.

Social motives were the third most frequent motive reported, supporting the interpersonal dimension of emotion regulation (Tran et al., 2025; Zaki & Williams, 2013) and the role of emotions in maintaining social relationships and adhering to cultural display rules (Mesquita et al., 2017; Matsumoto et al., 2008). The prevalence of eudaimonic and epistemic motives in daily regulation, albeit at a lower rate than other instrumental motives, is intriguing-and largely unexplored in the existing literature.

Broadly, our findings align with perspectives that view emotion regulation not just as a means of feeling good, but as a pathway to eudaimonic wellbeing through gaining knowledge, personal growth, and selfrealisation (Ryan & Deci, 2001; Tamir, 2016; Gross, 2015).

Strategy Use Varies with Regulatory Motives in Everyday Contexts

The second aim of this study was to test whether people engage different regulatory strategies depending on their motives. Our study investigated seven distinct emotion regulation motives alongside six emotion regulation strategies. To account for the influence of valence and intensity of emotions on the link between motives and strategy use, we included positive and negative emotions as covariates in our models. Because emotion regulation strategies may differ in function and affordances, and be selectively employed in contexts where they are perceived to be most effective at achieving a given goal, we hypothesized that regulatory motives would be associated with distinct patterns of emotion regulation strategy use. Although half of the hypothesized relationships for this research question were not supported, overall our findings suggest a complex interplay between emotion regulation motives and patterns of strategy selection in everyday life.

Rumination is associated with most motives

Rumination was positively associated with both prohedonic and contrahedonic motives, presenting an intriguing paradox. It is possible that this pattern partly reflects the item wording: our single rumination item did not distinguish reflective pondering (often linked to constructive insights leading to positive affect outcomes) from brooding (typically associated with sustained negative affect and poorer short-term affective outcomes), and so may have conflated distinct facets of rumination (Treynor et al., 2003; Nolen-Hoeksema et al., 2008). First, people may recognize rumination post-hoc, as a habitual process that automatically happens when trying to regulate emotions, rather than a strategy initiated with intentionality (Watkins & Nolen-Hoeksema, 2014). Alternatively, the presence of rumination might simply signal general regulatory effort, indicating that an individual is actively attempting to manage their emotions, regardless of the effectiveness of the strategy (Gross, 2015; Gutentag et al., 2026). Prohedonic motives are associated with distraction and reappraisal

When seeking pleasure and attempting to avoid pain (i.e., prohedonic motives), people reported greater use of distraction and reappraisal. Our findings align with previous research, indicating that individuals often employ distraction as an effective strategy to alleviate negative emotions and maintain or enhance positive affect (Sheppes et al., 2011; Webb et al., 2012). More broadly, the link between distraction and reappraisal strategies with prohedonic motives may speak to people's general understanding of what might work best to feel better: using those strategies that are effective in decreasing negative emotions, and increasing positive ones (Gross, 2015; Webb et al., 2012).

Cognitive engagement strategies are associated with eudaimonic motives

Distraction was negatively associated with eudaimonic motives. Instead, and in line with our predictions, personal growth motives were associated with greater use of rumination and cognitive reappraisal, both cognitive engagement strategies. We also found a significant positive relationship between eudaimonic motives and social sharing, further supporting the idea that people may engage with their emotions to facilitate personal growth. Taken together, these findings suggest that when regulating emotions for personal growth, individuals might be less likely to employ disengagement strategies like distraction, considering instead engagement strategies as most effective to pursue these types of goals (Sheppes et al., 2014; Tamir & Gross, 2011). This regulatory profile may reflect the emphasis of eudaimonic motives on understanding and integrating emotional experiences to foster learning and meaning-making (Rimé, 2009; Tamir, 2009; Treynor et al., 2003). Indeed, engagement strategies such as reappraisal, reflective rumination, and social sharing have been proposed to promote insight, even if they sometimes entail short-term increases in negative affect (Nolen-Hoeksema et al., 2008).

People use reappraisal when holding hedonic and instrumental motives

Reappraisal was associated with a diverse array of instrumental motives (performance, social, epistemic, and eudaimonic), potentially supporting its versatility and effectiveness as an emotion regulation strategy. Indeed, reappraisal has been consistently shown to be adaptive in managing emotions, with better psychological health and well-being outcomes (Aldao et al., 2010; Gross & John, 2003). The association between reappraisal and personal performance motives aligns with research demonstrating reappraisal helps with managing emotions in the context of personally important goals (Wallace et al., 2009), and in navigating interpersonal situations (English et al., 2017). Further, reappraisal involves reinterpreting situations to alter their emotional impact (Gross, 1998), a process that may be seen as conducive to learning and self-development, key aspects of epistemic and eudaimonic pursuits (Kashdan & Rottenberg, 2010). Therefore, as hypothesized, the association of reappraisal with epistemic and eudaimonic motives may be consistent with the relevance of reappraisal to goals involving understanding and personal growth.

Personal and professional performance motives link to different regulation strategies

Our study is the first to empirically investigate whether patterns of emotion regulation strategy use vary depending on whether regulation occurs alongside professional performance or personal performance motives. The results revealed differences in strategy use across these motives.

Contrary to our hypothesis, vocational performance motives predicted lower social sharing.

Perhaps individuals in this sample saw vocational performance as a solo pursuit, and were less inclined to regulate by sharing their emotions in relevant contexts (e.g., due to concerns about maintaining a professional image or to avoid showing vulnerability in a hierarchical context; Catterson et al., 2017; Eldesouky & English, 2023; Grandey, 2000; Pilch et al., 2018). Also counter to our hypotheses, vocational motives did not predict any of the other regulatory strategies. Perhaps in professional settings, people rely on strategies or tactics that our momentary self-report measures failed to capture. Moreover, restrained workplace settings may lead people to mask or perform affective processes as part of the job, hence not recognizing and describing these actions as emotion regulation per se (Grandey, 2000).

Personal performance motives predicted higher use of reappraisal and rumination. These findings suggest that people may invest more cognitive resources in regulating emotions tied to personal goals, possibly due to a higher internal locus of control for those goals, comparatively to vocational ones (Deci & Ryan, 2000; Eldesouky & English, 2018). Reappraisal may reflect attempts to reinterpret situations in ways that support personally relevant goals (Gross & John, 2003), whereas rumination may reflect sustained attention to emotions surrounding personal achievements or setbacks, which may be constructive or maladaptive depending on its focus and duration (Watkins, 2008). Together, these findings suggest that the strategies associated with performance-related motives may vary according to the context in which those motives arise. This interpretation is consistent with research showing that goal pursuit varies across domains, including between work/academic and other goals (Milyavskaya & Koestner, 2011; Milyavskaya & Werner, 2018). Future research should examine further whether differences in emotion regulation across performance contexts reflect distinct motivational processes or different situational manifestations of a broader performance motive. Our findings support the theoretical argument that regulation is fundamentally motive-driven and suggest that instrumental motives themselves are context-sensitive, so that different settings may alter which instrumental aims (e.g. performance, social, epistemic) are prioritized, and which strategies are considered appropriate (Tamir, 2016).

Finally, the relationship between an emotional experience and a performance goal may be more nuanced than we originally hypothesized: an emotion may be perceived as relevant to, facilitative of, or interfering with task performance. These different functional relationships may, in turn, be associated with differences in which emotion regulation strategies individuals select in the service of performance motives. Indeed, research on instrumental emotion regulation suggests that people may seek to increase or decrease particular emotions depending on their perceived utility for achieving a goal, including performance goals (Tamir, 2009; Tamir et al., 2008; Lane et al., 2011). Future research would do well in examining whether the relationship between performance motives and strategy selection depends on the perceived functional relationship between the emotional experience and the task.

Social motives link to a broad spectrum of emotion regulation strategies

Our study links social motives to a broad spectrum of emotion regulation strategies (suppression, social sharing, reappraisal, and rumination). This diverse strategy selection serving social motives aligns with models that see emotion regulation as deeply embedded in social interactions (Zaki & Williams, 2013). Specifically, the link between social motives and suppression was consistent with the possibility that people may, in relevant contexts, hide their feelings to adhere to social norms or preserve relationships (Kalokerinos et al., 2017c). However, this association should be interpreted cautiously, because this within-person effect was no longer statistically significant in analyses that also included the between-person effects (see: Supplementary Materials, Table S7). Conversely, the association between social motives and social sharing aligns with theory positing that people may rely on this strategy when focusing on social goals or outcomes (Rimé, 2009). The use of reappraisal in social contexts may indicate attempts to reframe situations in ways that facilitate social harmony or enhance interpersonal relationships (English et al., 2017). Finally, our exploratory analysis revealed a significant link between social motives and rumination. Indeed, research on co-rumination suggests that ruminating with others can foster a sense of social connection, albeit at the cost of feeling worse in the moment (Rose, 2021).

Overall, these findings highlight the complex interplay between strategies and social motives, suggesting that social motives may be associated with regulation patterns that do not necessarily prioritise immediate hedonic states.

Importantly, these associations may partly reflect the breadth of social motives captured with our single item. Indeed, research on interpersonal emotion regulation has highlighted considerable diversity in the motives underlying regulation in social contexts (Tran et al., 2025). Social motives encompass potentially distinct aims (e.g., promoting relationships, avoiding conflict, and managing one's impressions of others), which may, in turn, differentially shape strategy selection. Indeed, Eldesouky and English (2019) found distinct links between specific social motives and emotion regulation strategies, with prosocial motives linked to reappraisal and impression-management motives linked to suppression. Thus, the associations observed here may reflect the convergence of different strategy profiles across distinct social motives.

Limitations and Future Directions

Despite this study's strengths, some limitations warrant further consideration.

Constraints on Generality.

Our sample was predominantly female, and composed largely of Asian participants living in Australia, which limits the generalizability of our findings across cultural contexts. Prior work suggests that people from Eastern and Western backgrounds may regulate emotions differently, and with different social and personal consequences (e.g. Butler et al., 2007; Ford & Mauss, 2015; Liddell & Williams, 2019), so both motives and frequencies with which they arise are likely to vary across cultures. Consequently, the regulatory profiles we observed may not reflect universal processes. Future research could recruit more culturally diverse and gender-balanced samples, to better determine how gender and culture shape motive-strategy relationships.

Two further limitations stem from our correlational design, and the temporal resolution of our design. First, although we conceptualised emotion regulation in relation to specific episodes, the study captured processes occurring within a defined time window (the hour preceding each survey), rather than discrete regulatory episodes. It is therefore plausible that a single assessment period encompassed multiple regulatory episodes. Consequently, when multiple motives or strategies were reported within the same survey, we were unable to precisely determine which motive was associated with which strategy. Second, we cannot rule out reverse or reciprocal effects, whereby strategies shape subsequent motives, rather than motives being associated with subsequent strategy use. For example, rumination could lead to higher negative affect and thereby prompts a hedonic aim to "feel better", rather than a pre-existing hedonic motive prompting rumination. Theoretically, this distinction is critical: if strategies commonly generate subsequent motives, models of emotion regulation should accommodate bidirectional feedback of goals and tactics (Tamir, 2016). Future studies could address this issue by combining time-lagged ESM with experimental manipulations or ecological momentary interventions, to test causal pathways and refine dynamic theories of motive-strategy relations.

Finally, we did not assess whether motives predicted people's emotion regulation goals and their affective outcomes (Tamir, 2016). By not measuring regulation goals and immediate outcomes, we cannot link motives (the "why") and chosen strategies (the "how") to the short-term affective outcomes, so we cannot tell whether the strategy profiles we observed actually helped people achieve the motives they reported. More broadly, assessing the situational demands surrounding these motives and strategies would help determine whether particular strategies are selected and effective because they fit the demands of the situation. For example, the same motive may be pursued through different strategies depending on the social, interpersonal, or task-related demands of a situation. This would provide a useful link to research on emotion regulation flexibility and strategy-situation fit, where the adaptiveness of a strategy depends partly on the context in which it is used (Haines et al., 2016). In this way, future research could examine how motives and situational demands jointly shape strategy selection and regulatory success, providing a more comprehensive understanding of the relationship between motives, goals, strategy selection, and regulatory effectiveness (Springstein & English, 2024).

Conclusion

Our findings offer novel insights into the associations between people's regulatory motives, and the regulatory strategies they use in everyday life. Feeling better was the most common motive for people to initiate regulation, and feeling worse was rare. Moving beyond feeling good, we found that holding instrumental motives was quite common, and each motive category linked to distinct strategy selection profiles. Strategies that are effective in feeling better (distraction and reappraisal) were linked to prohedonic motives. Strategies that involve cognitive engagement (reappraisal and rumination) were associated with both hedonic and instrumental motives. Personal and vocational performance motives showed different regulatory profiles, suggesting the importance of considering context: associations between motives and strategy use may vary depending on the domain in which the regulatory goal arises. Our work contributes to the theoretical understanding of emotion regulation by showing that the motives people report for regulating their emotions are associated with the strategies they report using in everyday life. As we continue to unravel the intricacies of people's everyday emotional life, considering the motivational context of everyday emotion regulation may be important for understanding how regulatory strategies relate to emotional well-being and adaptive functioning across life domains.
