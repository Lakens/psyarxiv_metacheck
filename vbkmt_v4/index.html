How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard), also Internal Review Board - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term); not IEC followed by a number, which is a technical standard - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee/Commission, in the singular or plural - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee, Bioethics Committee/Commission, Ethikkommission - Ethical Review Authority/Unit/Office/Agency - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Human Research Protection Office/Program, Office for Human Subjects Protection - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee(s) on/for (Medical and Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt - Ethics review that was not needed: Ethics review was not required, did not require further ethical review, exempt from formal human research ethics review, no new institutional ethics review

A template line the authors have not filled in (“[Add ethics approval … here.]”) is not a statement.

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

When a statement is expected

The module sorts each paper by where its data came from (the internal .data_source(); the signs and how the rule was tested are described in R/data-source.R): - own: the authors collected data from people or animals, for this paper, in an earlier study of their own, or (a protocol) will collect them; - existing_records: records about individual people that the authors obtained for this study, such as hospital records or a cohort; - existing_unclear: existing data about people from a source the module could not identify; - open_resource: an open resource any researcher can use, such as UK Biobank or NHANES; - none: no data about people or animals (a review, simulation, commentary, theory, tutorial, coding of published papers, or figures about groups); - uncertain: the signs were missing or pointed both ways; - no_text: the paper has no text.

A statement is expected for own, existing_records and existing_unclear. A paper without a statement in these classes, or in uncertain, is flagged. The module reads the text, and the results of power, stat_p_exact, stat_check, repo_check, data_check and prereg_check when they ran before it (run it after them); without stat_p_exact, it finds the p-values in the text.

On 199 PsyArXiv and medRxiv preprints labelled after the rule was fixed, the module decided right whether a statement is needed for 86.9% (the earlier live-data search: 83.4%). Its main errors: other people’s studies described in the words of a new study, records and specimens described only as counts, and open resources the paper does not call open.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download information from other preregistration sites, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Therefore 0% of true instances were missed, and 43% of detections were false positives. See https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/ for more details of the validation.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on three hand-coded sets of papers (scienceverse/validation: 249 Psychological Science, 250 Economics and 250 MDPI papers). Of 1,783 non-significant p-values in the ground truth, version 0.4.4.3 found 1,719 (3.6% missed), and 94.8% of its 1,814 detections were in the ground truth. How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. A TRO uses them for each trov:TransparentResearchPerformance. * run_results$exit_status: the exit status of the process that ran the script (0 = ended normally); NA when the process did not report one (a time limit, or Docker failing to start the container). * artifacts: one row per file, with stage (deposit: as downloaded from the repository, hashed from the downloaded file; before_run: the run folder just before the first script ran, after metacheck’s own changes, such as rewritten paths and unpacked archives; after_run: only the files the scripts made (status new), changed (changed) or removed (removed), as an unchanged file is the one listed before the run), path (relative to the run folder, as valid UTF-8, a byte of another encoding written as <xx>), size (bytes), sha256, mime_type (by extension, NA when unknown), source (the file’s address in the repository, for deposit). The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). Files metacheck itself copied or made between passes (see modifications, output_copied, file_placed) show in after_run too. A TRO uses the rows as trov:ResearchArtifacts and each stage as a trov:ArtifactArrangement. * modifications$sha256_before, modifications$sha256_after: the hash of the changed script as downloaded and as it ran; NA when the script is not at the same path in both (a script made from an R Markdown file). * environment: the system that ran the code (trov:TransparentResearchSystem in a TRO): metacheck_version, started_at, ended_at, host (R version, platform, operating system), sandbox, network_isolated (TRUE only when every Docker run had --network none; the process sandbox does not stop the network), internet, timeout_per_script, total_timeout, python_local (the Python run outside Docker and its version), and docker: runs (for each kind of run, r_run, r_install, python_run, python_install, the image and the docker run arguments, such as --network none, --read-only, --cap-drop ALL, no-new-privileges, the process limit and the memory and processor limits) and images (for each image, its ID and digests from docker image inspect, its creation time and the R and Python versions its environment declares).

run_results$checked says what the run of each script shows about the code, in five groups, so that a script that does not reproduce is kept apart from one the check could not test: ran (it ran to the end); code_error (it did not reproduce: it stopped with an error of the code or the deposit, could not be read, or needs a file the deposit does not hold); dependency (a package or program was not available: a package that could not be installed or was not loaded, a program outside R and Python, a system library, or R crashing in a package’s compiled code); time (it did not finish within the time limit for one script, or was not started because the time for all scripts had run out); and not_checkable (it could not be checked automatically: it needs a person, the internet, a key or another setting of the authors’ computer, one platform such as Google Colab, Windows or OpenSAFELY, more memory than the run allows, a file that was not downloaded, a screen, or a program that runs it, or it is a file of a package of the code that Python runs only as a module of that package). It is NA for files run inside another script, files that are not an analysis, files not read, and a Docker failure. summary_table counts each group (repro_ran_n, repro_code_error_n, repro_dependency_n, repro_time_n, repro_not_checkable_n), and the report’s first sentence gives them. The traffic light reflects the static readiness signals by default; a script in code_error that stopped with an error makes it red, as a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure, and a script in one of the other three groups keeps it from being green. Calls that need a screen, which the run does not have (dev.print(), dev.copy2pdf(), dev.copy2eps(), savePlot(), windows(), win.graph(), x11(), quartz(), View()), are replaced before the run by calls that need none, which modifications records as screen_call_replaced. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Full text

Abstract

Humans increasingly collaborate with AI, yet most research focuses on AI's net impact without modeling human-AI performance as a collaborative process. This limits our ability to understand who benefits from AI, and why. We develop a framework to explore the joint contributions of humans and AI. We separately estimate each person's individual and collaborative ability, while accounting for task difficulty. Using data from 667 people answering 396 questions alone and with one of two large language models (11,008 total answers), we find substantial synergy that cannot be explained by people simply adopting the AI's answers. We show that collaborative ability is a latent, measurable trait, distinct from individual problem-solving ability: people who perform best alone are not necessarily those who perform best with AI. We find that people who are better at inferring what the AI knows collaborate more effectively with it-but are no better at solving problems when working alone-and that moment-to-moment shifts in perspective-taking predict the quality of the AI's responses.

Introduction

Collaboration between humans and artificial intelligence-especially large language models (LLMs)is rapidly expanding in cognitive work. Firms are redesigning jobs around AI, educators are deciding whether and how to teach students to work with it, and policymakers are debating whether it will narrow or widen the skill gap. Yet what is known about human-AI collaboration is largely confined to its net effect on performance from comparing people working alone to those working with AI (Noy & Zhang, 2023; Bansal et al., 2021; Vaccaro et al., 2024; Brynjolfsson et al., 2025; Dell'Acqua et al., 2026b,a). While these human-AI productivity studies often find gains from working with AI, comparisons of mean effects leave many questions unanswered. For example, it is difficult to explain whether gains reflect genuine synergy or substitution (with people simply adopting answers the AI already had) (Steiner, 1972). Distributionally, focusing on mean performance often obscures which individuals benefit from AI and on which tasks because task difficulty, individual ability, and collaborative skill are confounded in many work settings. Most importantly, comparing mean performance with and without AI cannot explain why synergy arises. An imperfect understanding of synergy may, in turn, limit the ability to design AI models that complement and extend-rather than imitate-human capabilities (Srivastava et al., 2023; Haupt & Brynjolfsson, 2025; Mancoridis et al., 2025; Acemoglu & Restrepo, 2018; Riedl, 2024).

Here, we develop and apply a framework that moves the study of human-AI collaboration from measuring uplift to quantifying and explaining synergy. We treat human-AI interaction as a form of teamwork, building on the insight that intelligence-whether human or artificial-is inherently interactive, contextual, and collaborative (Minsky, 1986; Hutchins, 1995; Lévy, 1997; Riedl et al., 2021). We define human-AI synergy as the performance uplift attributable to the joint human-AI process itself: gains that are not reducible to either the human's or the AI's contribution alone. This is conceptually analogous to the synergistic information atom in partial information decomposition (Williams & Beer, 2010; Riedl & Rosas, 2026), but operationalized behaviorally (see Methods for details and empirical decomposition). Synergy so defined includes gains from scaffolding and co-construction, but also from calibrated reliance-i.e., the joint decision of whose answer to adopt on a given task, in the sense of a transactive memory system (Wegner, 1987; Lewis, 2003).

If synergy arises from the joint process, some people should be better at it than others. To explain why some people collaborate better with AI than others, we draw on research on human teams, where the most robust individual-level predictor of collaborative performance is Theory of Mind (ToM)-the capacity to represent and reason about what another agent knows and intends (Premack & Woodruff, 1978; Riedl et al., 2021; Weidmann & Deming, 2021). ToM allows collaborators to anticipate each other's actions, disambiguate and repair communication, and coordinate contributions during joint tasks (Frith & Frith, 2006; Clark, 1996; Sebanz et al., 2006; Tomasello, 2010). Work in human-computer interaction likewise shows that effective collaboration with AI depends on forming accurate mental models of what the system knows and can do (Bansal et al., 2019b; Paleja et al., 2021; Bansal et al., 2019a; Gero et al., 2020; Alipour et al., 2021; Fügener et al., 2022; Westby & Riedl, 2023). Because LLMs generate novel unpredictable output in response to user inputs, working with them plausibly benefits from similar mechanisms that support human teamwork (e.g., anticipating teammate contributions, correcting errors of understanding) making ToM a natural candidate mechanism (see Appendix C for extended justification).

We use Bayesian Item Response Theory to model human-AI performance as a joint process. The model accounts for task difficulty, user ability, and the AI's standalone capability. We leverage partial pooling (shrinkage) to stabilize estimates and propagate uncertainty across all estimates, avoiding the pitfalls of point-estimate approximations. Crucially, our approach estimates each person's individual ability (θ) and collaborative ability (κ) as separate latent traits (see Methods). This yields modelbased counterfactuals that turn a single average effect into a distribution of gains across people and questions. It lets us separate synergy from substitution, and test whether synergy is a uniform boost or specific to particular user-task pairings. A model-free partial information decomposition provides an independent check. We apply the framework to data from 667 people who completed 396 multiple-choice questions in math, physics, and moral reasoning, first alone and then with one of two LLMs of different capability, GPT-4o and Llama-3.1-8B(11,008 total answers) (Chang et al., 2025). To answer not only who benefits from AI but why, we score each user's prompts for signatures of ToM (see Methods).

We have three main findings. First, human-AI collaboration produces synergy. On average, individuals improve by 16-24 percentage points when working with AI. We observe synergy even with a weak AI model that scores below typical human in the solo condition. We also observe synergy on items that the AI cannot solve by itself. This suggests that people are not simply adopting AI answers. A model-free information decomposition independently confirms a positive synergistic component. Second, we find that the ability to collaborate with AI is a latent trait separable from individual problem-solving ability. In our setting, the collaborative trait and individual problem-solving ability are roughly equally important in determining human-AI joint performance. Because the two are only moderately correlated, performance rankings reshuffle when people work with AI: the best solo performers are likely, but not certain, to remain on top. The collaboration boost is largest on the hardest problems and for the lowest-ability users, compressing the skill gap by roughly 59%. Because the gain is additive, AI does not act as a multiplier for experts. Third, Theory of Mind (ToM) predicts collaborative ability but not solo ability. Moreover, both stable individual differences and moment-to-moment variation in ToM predict the quality of AI responses, whereas individual ability does not. Read together with the additivity of synergy, this points to social cognition as a candidate mechanism and to an asymmetry in the collaboration: the human adapts to the AI, but current models do not yet adapt to the human. These findings turn human-AI synergy from an aggregate treatment effect into a measurable, decomposable, and explicable dimension of collective intelligence (Riedl et al., 2021).

Results

Quantifying Synergy. We begin with a model-free analysis (Figure 1A). Working alone, humans answer 55.5% of questions correctly on average (based on 2,072 solo observations). GPT-4o performs better than the human average (71% accuracy) whereas Llama-3.1-8B does worse (39%). When working jointly with humans, even the relatively weak Llama-3.1-8B model achieves substantially higher performance, significantly outperforming human-alone performance. GPT-4o-with-human also achieves substantial gains, outperforming GPT-4o-alone. However, the large difference in solo performance between Llama-3.1-8B and GPT-4o shrinks dramatically.

Next, we explore the collaborative capability of each AI model by quantifying its synergy (Figure 1B). We extend the results of Chang et al. (2025) by quantifying the average impact each model has on human-AI performance while accounting for differences in task difficulty, human solo ability, and human collaborative ability. To calculate the percentage point increase, we computed the pairwise difference in probabilities between the AI-assisted and baseline conditions directly across posterior draws. A key advantage of working directly with the posterior draws is that it propagates the joint uncertainty of all parameter estimates, avoiding the pitfalls of point-estimate approximations. We find that Llama-3.1-8B boosts performance by 16 percentage points on average, while GPT-4o boosts performance by 24 percentage points. Confidence intervals overlap, suggesting no clear advantage for either model. 1 To explore the mechanism behind this complementarity, we quantify shifts from answering incorrectly alone to answering correctly with the help of AI via posterior predictive counterfactuals. On average, users switch from wrong answers to right answers on 25% of the tasks when working with Llama-3.1-8B and 29% when working with GPT-4o. Harmful switches from right to wrong are rare (12.3% and 8.6%, respectively) and, per opportunity to be harmed, independent of ability. At the individual level, item-level harm is offset by gains: no user (0 of 667) shows a credibly negative expected boost with either AI model (see Appendix K for method details and additional results, including a breakdown by task difficulty and domain).

Synergy Beyond Direct Substitution.

A separate question is whether the benefits of working with AI reflect genuine complementarity-gains from scaffolding, clarification, and co-construction-or substitution, where the AI provides the correct answer and the user adopts it. We provide several tests. First, controlling for an exogenous measure of each question's AI standalone accuracy, user-level collaborative ability retains 73% of its standard deviation (53% of its variance). We also find that users improve over their solo baseline even on questions the AI could not solve alone (β = 0.80 [95% CI: 0.59, 1.01]). In information theoretic terms, this bounds the unique contribution that AI makes (Riedl & Rosas, 2026; i.e., I(A; Y ) ≈ 0 and thus Un(A) ≈ 0). Hence, gains are unlikely to be substitution. Second, we split problems into "AI-solvable" and "AI-unsolvable" based on the AI solo performance. We find that collaborative ability (see next section for details) estimated separately on both sets is strongly positively correlated (ρ = 0.80 [95% CI: 0.53, 0.96]). This points to a unified collaboration skill-active elicitation and error-checking, and appropriately calibrated reliance on AI output (Caplin et al., 2026)-not passive adoption (Appendix L). Third, a model-free partial information decomposition on the answer-first arm (Riedl & Rosas, 2026), where participants' own and final answers are observed on the same questions, recovers a credibly positive synergistic atom, with its share of total information rising on harder questions (Figure A3). The decomposition is inconsistent with pure substitution, under which final answers would track the AI's and information would load onto the AI-unique rather than the human-unique atom (Appendix N).

Within-User Ability Decomposition.

To test whether working alone and with AI are separately estimable latent abilities we fit the model from Eq. 1 and a nested, simpler model that estimates a single latent ability for each user. We perform Bayesian model comparison with leave-one-out cross-validation to evaluate how well each model predicts unseen data by estimating the expected log predictive density (ELPD) for held-out observations. We find the full model fits the data substantially better (∆ELPD = 50.9, SE = 10.2). Overall model convergence is excellent (all R are 1.00 showing no evidence of chains mixing poorly) and both Bulk ESS and Tail ESS are large (all > 1,500, some > 4,000) indicating very good sampling efficiency and stable posterior estimates.

The model comparison decisively favors the two-ability specification, which is consistent with condition-specific latent abilities-one reflecting users' ability to work alone (θ), and one capturing their ability to work with AI (κ)-supporting the notion that θ ̸ = κ. Note that this is a claim about the population distribution of abilities-estimated from the entire dataset of 667 individuals-and not about any individual's specific ability rank. Several robustness checks support these conclusions: the θ ̸ = κ separation replicates on the subset of users with six solo observations giving more precise ability estimates; solo-ability estimates are reliable and stable across the first and second solo phase; and our results are insensitive to prior specification (Appendix B and M).

Non-Additive Synergy. We perform several tests to answer whether human-AI synergy is additive or non-linear. First, we test for non-additive synergy directly by asking whether the AI boost bends with item difficulty in a user-specific way. That is, we introduce a user × difficulty interaction term (three bins). We target difficulty rather than domain or the raw user × item residual because difficulty is the only such factor that varies in the dataset across a user's items (domain is nested within user, and the cell-level residual is confounded with Bernoulli noise). We find no detectable user-specific difficulty-dependent non-additive synergy (∆ELPD = -1.7, SE = 1.2; adding the interaction does not reveal hidden non-linearity ∆R²M Z = 0.003). A simulation-based power analysis using our full estimation pipeline shows that we would have detected user-by-difficulty non-linear synergy exceeding 16% of the additive-synergy variance (interaction SD 0.63 logits) with ≥80% power (Appendix J). Hence, the absence of such structure bounds any non-linearity to a small fraction of the additive effect rather than leaving it unquantified.

Second, we estimated a 2PL extension of the model that allows item-specific discrimination parameters, permitting the mapping from latent ability to response probability to vary across items-the primary form of user × item interaction identifiable with single-trial Bernoulli data. The standard deviation of log-discrimination was small (0.23 [95% CI: 0.02, 0.46]) and LOO-IC comparison yielded no reliable improvement over the additive model (∆ELPD = 13.7, SE = 13.6), again indicating no reliable non-additive structure.

Third, we decompose performance into a structural signal and stochastic noise (Eq. 2). We recover 45.7% of the total variance as explainable signal (R²M Z = 0.457); crucially, we find that the remaining variance is consistent with aleatoric noise rather than recoverable structure. The residual dispersion statistic (ϕ = 0.658) is consistent with the absence of systematic non-linearities. Furthermore, variance analysis shows that the model fits collaborative data (σ 2 joint = 0.63) nearly as robustly as the solo baseline (σ 2 solo = 0.51). Within this robust structure, we find that the additive boost parameter is a primary driver of performance, accounting for 42.5% of the reliable signal variance.

These diagnostics support a central conclusion: Human-AI synergy is predominantly a structured, additive process, rather than dynamic (at least with current LLM capabilities and within the established detection bounds). This demonstrates AI collaboration ability as a first-order latent trait-distinct from but comparable in magnitude to individual ability. It also supports the proposed framework as a high-fidelity instrument for benchmarking human-AI synergy. Additivity at the latent level is not in tension with the presence of synergistic information in the information-theoretic sense: any aggregation rule under which the pair succeeds when either partner would have (an OR-like channel) carries substantial synergistic information under partial information decomposition, and a model-free decomposition on the answer-first subset confirms a credibly positive synergistic atom (Appendix N). The dispersion and interaction tests establish that the mechanism generating this information is a stable, generalized channel (κ) rather than item-specific dynamics. That is, synergistic information is produced by a structurally additive process (Riedl & Rosas, 2026). Who benefits from AI? Having quantified average synergy we now decompose these effects. First, we estimate the impact of AI on questions of differing difficulty. We find that more difficult tasks (by solo accuracy) benefit more from AI collaboration (Figure 2A). This is consistent with theories of human-machine complementarity suggesting that AI provides greater additive benefit where human performance is weakest (Autor, 2015; Acemoglu & Restrepo, 2018). In information-decomposition terms, this is also the regime in which redundant information is structurally starved: as solo accuracy approaches the floor, the human's answer carries little information about the outcome, and with it the redundancy bound shrinks as well (Riedl & Rosas, 2026). Decomposing Synergy: Equalization and Distinctness. Our Bayesian formulation allows us to further disentangle the relationship between individual and AI collaboration ability by relaxing the independence assumption of the model (Figure 3A). This sheds more light on the two complementary phenomena mentioned above. First, we find an equalizing effect through variance compression. Comparing the latent trait distributions, we find that AI collaboration significantly reduces performance variance (σ 2 solo = 1.39 vs. σ 2 joint = 0.58). This explains the strong negative correlation between solo ability and AI boost (lifting up lower-ability users while simultaneously imposing a ceiling on high-ability users) and confirms that AI compresses the skill gap. AI benefits low-ability users disproportionately and the spread shrinks by 59%.

Second, we find a positive but weak correlation between individual and collaboration ability (ρ p = 0.34 [95% CI: 0.15, 0.54]). Pure automation would imply ρ ≈ 0; pure amplification ρ ≈ 1 with variance expansion. The observed moderate ρ with variance compression indicates additive complementarity through a partially distinct skill. Concretely, this means performance rankings shuffle when users move from solo to collaborative settings (Figure 3A). The top solo performers are likely but not certain to remain on top, because a largely independent ability now co-determines outcomes. While higher-ability users retain an absolute advantage, AI does not act as a multiplier for experts: synergy is additive rather than multiplicative, and the proportional gain for lower-ability users results in a net compression of the skill gap. While individual ability provides a statistically significant advantage, it explains only 11.6% of the variance in collaboration ability and is not the primary driver. The latent boost-solo correlation (ρ p = -0.89 [CI: -0.93, -0.83]) matches the naive estimate (ρ p = -0.91), confirming the effect is structural.

Theory of Mind.

To explain the observed human-AI synergy, we draw on established theories from human-human collaboration, particularly Theory of Mind. ToM refers to the capacity to represent and reason about others' mental states (Premack & Woodruff, 1978) and plays a crucial role in human-human interaction (Nickerson, 1999; Lewis, 2003, see Appendix C for additional background on ToM). As measured here, ToM is the tendency to model the AI's capability and informational state, and tailor prompts to it. It is distinct from prompt effort (turns, characters, edit ratio) and from surface prompt quality (concreteness, readability, spelling), both of which we control for directly. We collect task-level ToM measures from the users' portion of the dialogue, using an LLM as a research assistant (LMRA, Eloundou et al., 2025, see Appendix E) and follow best practices for using LLMs in this way (Li et al., 2025). We performed extensive robustness tests to validate construct validity including (a) collecting data from a variety of leading models, (b) exploring robustness to the specific instruction set in the prompt, (c) order bias in the prompt, and (d) absolute vs. relative judgments. We find excellent inter-coder reliability among the different LLM "raters". We also collected gold standard human ratings for a stratified random sample of 120 dialogues to validate the approach of studying dialogue-level ToM signatures in human-AI collaboration. The ensemble LMRA agreement is excellent (ICC2k = 0.90; a single human rater is only 0.13). One LMRA corresponds to about five human raters. We find substantial agreement between the gold standard and LMRA ratings (ICC2 = 0.42; ICC2k = 0.59 [95% CI: 0.42, 0.72]), outperforming a single human rater and approaching the consistency of the aggregated human consensus (see Appendix E, F, G).

The above analysis raises an interesting question: is ToM a fixed user-level trait associated with an individual's latent ability to collaborate-or can we view ToM as the direct mechanism through which collaboration synergy unfolds (Riedl, 2026)? These are testable questions: a direct relationship implies that a user's ToM would influence the quality of human-AI dialogues and, more specifically, the quality of the AI responses. Moreover, a direct relationship would suggest the possibility that dynamic within-user changes in ToM expression may influence the quality of AI responses.

To explore these questions we examine the relationship between ToM and AI response quality (Table 1; for LMRA-based quality scores see Appendix H). Using variance decomposition (Appendix D) we find that trait-level ToM predicts AI response quality, while solo ability does not (Models 1-2), mirroring findings from human teamwork that synergy depends on ToM rather than individual problem-solving ability (Weidmann & Deming, 2021). Notably, the association strengthens rather than attenuates when we control for prompting effort and proxies for prompt quality, which is the opposite of what a pure prompt-specification account would predict; solo ability remains null throughout. Furthermore, within-user, question-by-question variation in ToM predicts response quality (Model 3), with consistent effects in the hierarchical Bayesian specification (Model 4).

A natural alternative is that ToM signatures reflect prior experience with LLMs rather than a general social-cognitive capacity. Two features of our results contradict this account. First, the within-user, question-to-question variation in ToM predicts AI response quality net of each user's own mean. A stable trait such as AI literacy cannot produce this pattern. Second, because AI responses depend only on the user's prompt, experience can affect response quality only through the prompt's content. Yet the effect of ToM strengthens when we add controls for prompt effort and surface quality.

It is important to note a structural limit on what these tests can establish: ToM is scored from users' own prompts, so a well-specified prompt might yield a better AI response whether or not any mentalizing occurred. That said, our controls absorb the most obvious prompt-related confounds (i.e., we control for proxies of "effort" and "quality"). We also note that our user-level ToM measure is a leave-one-out mean (Appendix D): a user's trait score for question j is computed from their prompts on all other questions, so the trait measure and the scored response share no text. Notwithstanding these mitigations, we emphasize that we cannot cleanly identify the causal effect of ToM and that stable user characteristics correlated with both prompting skill and mentalizing remain a plausible alternative account for our results.

Discussion

Our research defines human-AI synergy as the performance uplift attributable to the joint human-AI process-gains from elicitation, error-checking, and calibrated reliance that reflect the interaction rather than either party's independent capability, and that are therefore not reducible to the AI supplying an answer the user adopts (Riedl & Rosas, 2026). We find such synergy: AI collaboration raises performance by 16-24 percentage points over solo baselines and users improve even on items the AI cannot solve alone. The uplift is predominantly additive and decomposes into a stable collaborative ability (κ) that is only moderately correlated with individual ability (θ) rather than into user-by-item dynamics. Because the boost is additive, it is largest on the hardest items and for the lowest-ability users: it compresses the performance gap between the best and worst users by 59% without eliminating it, and no user shows a credibly negative expected boost under either model.

The claim of human-AI synergy does not hinge on any single test: the three alternative sources of uplift-substitution, shared knowledge, and human-alone ability (Riedl & Rosas, 2026)-are each excluded by multiple lines of evidence (Appendix B, K, and L). A model-free information decomposition on the answer-first arm, which shares no assumptions with the IRT model, independently confirms a positive synergistic component (Appendix N). Because three solo items yield limited precision for any single user's rank, we make no claims about individual users; our substantive conclusions-the separability of θ and κ, additive nature of synergy, variance compression, and the ToM associations-are distribution-level quantities (variance components, correlations, and population slopes) that are identified from the full sample, are insensitive to the reliability of any individual's estimate, and are stable across prior specification (Appendix M), in a subsample with twice as many solo items (Appendix B), and in a three-phase model that separates AI assistance from order effects (Appendix I).

Beyond quantifying synergy, the additivity diagnostics double as a mechanism test. Any user-level disposition whose benefit depends on the AI being correct on a given item-trust in AI being the most prominent (Glikson & Woolley, 2020), but also over-reliance and automation bias-must produce a boost that scales with the AI's item-level advantage, and would therefore manifest both as user × difficulty non-additivity and as collaborative-ability variance concentrated on items the AI can solve; the power-bounded absence of the former (Appendix J) and the comparable, highly correlated collaborative variance on items the AI cannot solve alone (Appendix L) together cap this entire class of reliance-based explanations from behavioral data alone, without requiring self-reported trust data.

Who collaborates well is predicted by Theory of Mind. Users who better model the AI's informational state perform better with AI but gain no advantage alone. Both their trait-level and moment-to-moment ToM predict the quality of AI responses, whereas solo ability does not. The association strengthens rather than attenuates when we include controls for prompting effort and prompt quality. This extends a central result in collective intelligence-that ToM predicts team but not individual performance (Riedl et al., 2021; Weidmann & Deming, 2021)-to teams in which one member is a language model. Response quality is not an inherent property of the model alone but emerges from the interaction between human reasoning and AI capabilities. The pattern is consistent with ToM being more than an incidental correlate of performance. ToM may play a role in this regard, potentially acting as a cognitive-behavioral interface that supports the emergence of this synergy (cf., Riedl, 2026). But, we note that our design is observational and does not definitively establish ToM as a causal channel through which AI benefits emerge.

That said, we build on evidence that ToM is a dynamic construct that can vary within users (Qureshi et al., 2010; Schneider et al., 2012; Lin et al., 2010) and find that within-user differences in ToM are associated with the quality of human-AI dialogue. Future work could explore whether variation in a user's ToM triggers or suppresses ToM-relevant circuits inside LLMs (Prakash et al., 2025; Riedl & Rosas, 2026), which could inspire joint probes of both users and models. The insight that synergy is predominantly additive even though ToM varies dynamically supports the idea that current LLMs fail to fully support users as they modulate their behavior-for example based on the affordances of the task. This suggests training AI models for ToM-like responsiveness to users' cognitive states, rather than higher standalone accuracy, as a design target (Buckner, 2024). A counterfactual decomposition of harmful switches points the same way: with the more accurate model, the majority of residual harm occurs despite correct advice, so as advice quality improves what remains to fix is the interaction itself (Appendix K). Furthermore, our finding of additive synergy and skill compression could shift toward multiplicative, inequality-amplifying dynamics as AI models become more responsive to users' cognitive states, or in existing settings that our benchmark does not capture, such as open-ended tasks without performance ceilings or longer iterative collaborations in which effects compound across turns.

Our research provides a unified approach to the key analytical challenges in quantifying human-AI synergy: it separates individual from collaborative ability, controls for task difficulty, distinguishes additive from non-linear synergy, benchmarks different AI models, and-by incorporating exogenous measures of AI standalone capability-enables researchers to bound the contribution of direct answer substitution versus emergent collaborative gains. By providing a methodological framework this paper lays a foundation for understanding and optimizing human-AI collaboration that moves beyond simple performance comparisons toward theoretical explanations of when, why, and how humans and AI systems can achieve synergy.

The comparison across two AI models illustrate why this axis is distinct from standalone leaderboards: a 32-point gap in AI solo accuracy between Llama-3.1-8B and GPT-4o shrinks to roughly six points once a human is in the loop. Hence, a model that fails most questions alone can be nearly as good a collaborator. This would enable fine-grained comparisons of models' capacity to enhance the collective intelligence of human-AI teams in realistic problem-solving settings. Crucially, these contributions are interdependent: the ToM finding is only observable because the framework separates individual from collaborative ability-a distinction invisible to comparisons across means. Our work transforms human-AI collaboration from an anecdotal observation into a measurable, optimizable dimension of AI capability, enabling the design of systems explicitly aimed at emergent collaborative synergy rather than standalone performance.

Limitations and Future Work. While we validate our framework using well-structured academic tasks (adapted from MMLU), these items represent a small subset of real-world collaborative contexts. Because items are multiple-choice with a performance ceiling, part of the equalizing effect reflects limited headroom for high-ability users. Our data also include only two models with users nested within them; while the random assignment of users to models identifies the aggregate synergy benchmark, the nested design prevents us from exploring individual-level heterogeneity in compatibility.

Future work should examine whether our approach generalizes to other collaborative settings such as creative group work, workflow planning, or software development. We note that while the present study focuses on individual human-AI dyads, our framework naturally generalizes to multi-agent configurations. Additional hierarchical terms could model group-level collaborative ability in human teams or capture differential contributions from multiple AI assistants. Such settings may exhibit important emergent phenomena that are not observable in dyadic interactions. Developing and validating these multi-agent extensions represents an important direction for understanding collective intelligence in hybrid human-AI systems.

Study Design and Data. Our data comes from the ChatBench assessment constructed by Chang et al. (2025). ChatBench adapts MMLU (Hendrycks et al., 2021) into a human-AI benchmark with three components: i) solo AI; ii) solo human; iii) human-AI pairs. ChatBench contains multiplechoice questions on three domains: math, physics, and moral reasoning. The dataset includes 396 questions covering a wide range of difficulties. Questions were randomly assigned to users and shared across solo and joint phases, ensuring direct comparability. On average, each question was answered by around 20 different users-about 5 users in solo work and 15 jointly with AI-providing overlap that supports stable item-level estimates.

Overall, the data contains responses from 667 humans. Participants begin the study by answering three questions by themselves (solo human). Decomposing Linear and Non-Linear Synergy.

A key critique of static benchmarks is their inability to distinguish generalizable improvements from idiosyncratic interactions. To distinguish whether AI acts as a truly adaptive partner (non-linear synergy with specific user-item "chemistry") or merely a generally strong tool (additive gains), we perform several tests to distinguish linear from non-linear synergy. First, we test for non-additive synergy directly by asking whether the AI boost bends with item difficulty in a user-specific way. We complement this with a simulation-based power analysis to bound the strength of non-linearity our analysis is able to detect (Appendix J). Second, we estimated a 2PL extension of the model that allows item-specific discrimination parameters. Finally, we propose a dispersion test in which we decompose human-AI synergy into a systematic and idiosyncratic factor: where the first term represents Additive Synergy (stable user ability + item difficulty shifts), and ϵ ij represents Non-Linear Synergy (transient user-item dynamics).

For example, one user might benefit from AI assistance on a physics problem but gain little on a moral reasoning question, while another user shows the opposite pattern. By fitting the additive baseline (Eq. 1) and analyzing the dispersion of the residuals ϵ ij , our framework offers a novel diagnostic: it quantifies the extent to which collaboration is stable (trait-based) vs. dynamic and non-linear (interaction-based). If dispersion of ϵ ij is ≈ 1.0, synergy is stable and trait-based. If ϵ ij is ≫ 1.0, synergy is context-dependent and non-linear. As an additional test, the random-slopes of our model capture heterogeneous effects across ability and task difficulty, allowing decomposition of human-AI synergy beyond the uniform gains assumption of a simple additive model.

C Background on Theory of Mind in Human-AI Settings

To better explain AI's impact, we draw on established theories from human-human collaboration, particularly Theory of Mind (ToM). ToM refers to the capacity to represent and reason about others' mental states (Premack & Woodruff, 1978). It plays a crucial role in human-human interaction (Nickerson, 1999; Lewis, 2003), allowing individuals to anticipate actions, disambiguate and repair communication, and coordinate contributions during joint tasks (Frith & Frith, 2006; Clark, 1996; Sebanz et al., 2006; Tomasello, 2010). ToM has repeatedly been shown to predict collaborative success in human teams (Weidmann & Deming, 2021; Woolley et al., 2010; Riedl et al., 2021). ToM is the glue that binds autonomous agents into a collective epistemic agent (Clark & Chalmers, 1998; Tollefsen, 2006). Its importance is also recognized in AI and LLM research (Prakash et al., 2025; Liu et al., 2025), for purposes such as inferring missing knowledge (Bortoletto et al., 2024), aligning common ground (Qiu et al., 2024), and cognitive modeling (Westby & Riedl, 2023). We argue that working with LLMs similarly requires adaptive, interaction-sensitive behavior due to the dialogic nature of human-AI interactions and the semi-autonomous, unpredictable nature of LLM output. 6When teaming with generative AI like LLMs, users similarly need to anticipate their behavior and adapt accordingly given their semi-autonomous and unpredictable nature. Such adaptation is necessary as LLMs appear to users as having some level of agency and unpredictability. Four aspects of contemporary generative AI systems stand out. First, they possess meaningful agency by generating novel content without explicit commands, creating unpredictability that requires coordination (Dong et al., 2025). Second, successful collaboration with LLM systems depends on the same dialogic mechanisms as human teamwork: eliciting outputs and providing feedback based on an evolving understanding of the partner's capabilities and goals (Clark & Brennan, 1991). Third, deciding whether to accept, reject, or further interrogate LLM responses depends on estimating its competence and "beliefs". Fourth, LLMs exhibit behavior that strongly invites attribution of intentional states, encouraging users to ascribe beliefs (Gopnik & Wellman, 1992). This tendency toward anthropomorphism is not just a cognitive bias but reflects the genuinely interactive nature of AI responses. 7Robust coordination with such agents benefits from users' ability to form and revise mental models of the AI's knowledge and capabilities. Misaligned expectations about the AI's capabilities, knowledge, or "goals" are likely to hinder task performance. That is, successful AI partnerships require users to engage in dialogue with their AI partner, form accurate mental models of what the AI knows and doesn't know, and provide clear instructions informed by this understanding (cf. Laban et al., 2025, for similar arguments). When the AI makes errors, effective collaborators troubleshoot by hypothesizing what information the system lacked and updating their mental model accordingly. 8 In human-AI collaboration, ToM may enable users to form accurate mental models of the AI's capabilities and limitations, creating effective coordination (despite the AI's lack of mutual intentionality). ToM behavior may also support role differentiation, allowing users to leverage the AI's strengths, a division of cognitive labor resembling a transactive memory system in human groups (Lewis, 2003; Wegner, 1987).

Adopting a team-based perspective on human-AI interactions yields theoretical predictions which can be evaluated with human-AI benchmarks. First, we hypothesize that performance varies across conditions in a way that reflects two distinct, condition-specific latent abilities: individual problemsolving ability when working alone and collaborative ability when working jointly with AI. Evidence from human teams suggests that these two dimensions of human ability are separately identifiable, with each broad ability contributing independently to team success (Weidmann & Deming, 2021). Second, given the importance of ToM in enabling effective coordination and adaptive interaction in human groups, we hypothesize that higher ToM capabilities will positively predict collaborative ability, but not solo ability when working alone. Third, we ask whether ToM operates as a direct channel for synergy in human-AI teams (while noting that our design can establish association rather than causation). Concretely, we test the idea that users with strong ToM provide prompts that lead AI systems to engage in higher-quality dialogue. Last, we build on recent advances in psychology suggesting that ToM is a dynamic construct (Schneider et al., 2012). That is, ToM can be thought of as a stable individual trait whose expression is shaped by contextual affordances and cognitive demands. As such, we should expect ToM expression to vary within individuals (Qureshi et al., 2010; Lin et al., 2010; Meijering et al., 2010) and hypothesize that within-user dynamic increases in ToM expression (i.e., when users exhibit unusually strong ToM) will be positively associated with the quality of AI responses.

We build on the decomposition in Eq. A1 to investigate a potential cognitive mechanism underlying effective collaboration between humans and AI. In principle, any user-level characteristic can be used to explore why some users have stronger collaborative abilities when working with AI. In Eq. A2, we focus on Theory of Mind. As discussed in Appendix C, evidence from the literature on collective intelligence and human teams suggests that ToM is a crucial element of successful teamwork.

To measure ToM, we analyze each user's written prompts for signatures of Theory of Mind-the capacity to reason about others' knowledge, beliefs, and intentions (see Appendix E for a description of how ToM was measured). By integrating behavioral performance with linguistic indicators of ToM, we aim to better understand how users' mentalizing abilities contribute to the emergent human-AI collaboration synergy.

We then exploit the panel data structure with repeated observations to perform a variance decomposition (Mundlak, 1978). Both elements could, in principle, relate to AI response quality.

F Validation using Human Ratings

To validate the approach of studying dialogue-level ToM signatures in human-AI collaboration and the LMRA assessment, we collected gold standard human ratings for a stratified (by ToM level) random sample of 120 dialogues. We recruited 32 subjects from Prolific (general US pool), each of them rated 19 dialogues on average (SD=14) yielding an average of five ratings per dialogue. We paid human raters $1.50 for reading the instructions, $0.25 per rated dialogue, and a $1.50 bonus if they rated more than 10 dialogues (to encourage raters to evaluate several dialogues to increase consistency). The rating instructions were mostly identical to those used for the LLM with minor changes to formatting (e.g., breaking instructions up into several pages) and the addition of information about compensation.

Individual human raters showed low reliability (ICC2 = 0.13), reflecting some inherent noise in single ratings. The aggregated gold standard showed excellent reliability (ICC2k = 0.83 [95% CI: 0.78, 0.87]). The high inter-rater reliability among independent human judges demonstrates that dialogue-level ToM signatures in human-AI collaboration can be assessed consistently, validating the construct and supporting the use of this evaluation approach. The LMRA's ratings demonstrate substantial agreement with the gold standard (ICC2 = 0.42; ICC2k = 0.59 [95% CI: 0.42, 0.72]), outperforming a single human rater and approaching the consistency of the aggregated human consensus. Because single human ratings are themselves noisy (ICC2 = 0.13), agreement with the gold standard is bounded from above by noise in the criterion; the LMRA-gold correlation should therefore be read as a lower bound on validity. All ICC estimates between the LMRA and the human gold standard are significant (p < 0.001). These results support the use of a LMRA as a proxy for human assessment, and demonstrate the promise of LLM-based assessments as scalable approximations of human judgment for dialogue-level ToM. However, reliability of individual human ratings is low and the findings may be task-specific.

G Construct Validity of LMRA ToM Ratings

To further establish the construct validity of LMRA ToM ratings of user dialogues we took several additional steps to implement emerging LLM-as-a-judge best practices (Li et al., 2025). First, we inspected dialogues for signs of adversarial prompt injection (Maloyan & Namiot, 2025) but found no signs of it. This is not surprising given that users were unaware that we would code their dialogues for signs of ToM and that they were incentivized to achieve high question answering performance. Next, to address concerns of style confounds or model family effects, we replicated data collection using five other models: Google's Gemini 2.5 Pro, Anthropic's Claude Opus 4, OpenAI's gpt-oss-120b, Alibaba's Qwen3 32B, and DeepSeek's Deepseek R1. All models were accessed via the OpenRouter API. To assess robustness to prompt bias, we also collected data using an expanded ToM-assessment instruction set and alternative rating schemes (point-based assessment vs. pairwise comparisons).

To explore ordering bias, we collected data using a prompt which reverses the instructions for high vs. low ToM signatures. Across all nine LMRA "raters" we find excellent (Cicchetti, 1994) ensemble inter-coder reliability (ICC(2,k) = 0.90 [95% CI: 0.85, 0.92]). We also show all pairwise ICC2 scores (Figure A1). The smaller Llama-3.1-8b shows substantially lower agreement with the other models, consistent with limited capacity to judge ToM reliably. As a sensitivity analysis, we exclude this model which increases ICC(2,k) to 0.92 [95% CI: 0.91, 0.94]. Taken together, these findings provide convergent evidence that LMRA ToM ratings validly capture the intended construct, are robust across models and prompts, and suggest that any single model's ratings are representative of the ToM construct. Finally, to test robustness to judgment bias we collected data using a prompt that asked the LMRA to make pairwise comparisons as to which dialogue shows higher degree of ToM (instead of asking for assessment on 0-5 rating scale). We collected data on 12,000 pairwise comparisons drawing two random dialogues using gpt-oss-120b. To limit order bias, options were given neutral labels without a natural order. Next, we consider these pairwise judgments "matches" between the users who wrote them and aggregate them in an Elo-based ranking (ranking all users in the study by their ToM prompt writing capacity; based on about 37 pairwise judgments per user; min = 1164, mean = 1500, max = 1793). We find moderate-to-strong rank association between the baseline gpt-4.1 average rating and the Elo-based ranking (ρ s = 0.61; p < 2.2 × 10 -16 ). After calibrating the Elo scores to the 0-5 rating scale (via linear regression), we find moderate-to-good absolute agreement ICC(3,1) = 0.68 [95% CI: 0.64, 0.72] with gpt-4.1 scores and ICC(3,1) = 0.77 [95% CI: 0.73, 0.80] with gpt-oss-120b. These results indicate that the ToM construct can be reliably assessed using both absolute and relative judgment prompts.

I Causal Effect of Working with AI

The experimental design places users in the solo condition first and the with-AI condition second. One concern resulting from this design is that this may confound working with AI with temporal learning or fatigue effects since working with AI is always observed second. As a robustness test we leverage another feature of the Chang et al. (2025) dataset. Specifically, the design included another randomly assigned treatment condition in which some users worked on a set of questions alone for a second time, before finally working with AI in the last step. Instead of modeling only two phases (solo vs. joint) we model three phases (solo vs. solo after practicing vs. joint with AI). This model explicitly controls for and models any learning/fatigue effects. It thus avoids the confound between the AI condition and "AI is always observed second". We find consistent results with a slight negative effect of the second phase indicating fatigue (rather than learning). This indicates that our main effects somewhat underestimated the true benefit of working with AI (ρ p = 0.58, p < 0.001). This model causally identifies AI effects (from random assignment of users to AI models), phase-based learning/fatigue effects (based on phase progression with random assignment to conditions).

We also perform additional robustness tests to confirm stable estimates of individual ability using different diagnostic tests. First, MCMC diagnostics show excellent convergence (Rhat = 1.00) and very high effective sample sizes (Tail_ESS between 7,000-26,000 effective samples per parameter), confirming that posterior estimates of user-level ability and phase-specific slopes are stable and reliable, even though each user completed only a limited set of items per phase. Furthermore, posterior estimates correlate strongly with raw accuracy (r = 0.75), demonstrating that the hierarchical model produces precise, interpretable ability estimates.

J Power Analysis of Non-Additive Synergy

The conclusion that human-AI synergy is predominantly additive (Section Non-Additive Synergy in the main text) rests on a null result: the additive model showed no residual non-linearity. On its own this cannot distinguish a genuinely additive effect from a non-linear one too small for the design to detect. The following analysis quantifies that distinction by establishing how large a non-linearity we could have detected.

Specifically, we test for non-linear synergy by comparing the additive model against an expanded model that lets each user's collaborative boost vary across item-difficulty levels, and ask whether that added user-by-difficulty term explains reliable variance. We treat the fitted additive model as ground truth, and inject a user-by-difficulty-bin interaction of known variance into the jointcondition trials. We then simulate responses on the observed design, and refit both the additive and an expanded (interaction) model through the identical hierarchical estimator used in the main analysis (S = 200 parametric bootstrap simulations per effect size level). The analysis sweeps across different magnitudes of injected non-linearity to yield a detection-power curve and a minimum detectable effect. Power is the detection rate across the 200 replicates.

We find the procedure is well calibrated (false-positive rate ≤ 2% with no injected interaction) and reaches ≥ 80% power at an interaction accounting for 16% of the additive-synergy variance. These results are consistent using both 3-and 5-bin difficulty partitions. Because the estimator refits the full shrinkage pipeline on simulated data, this bound already incorporates any shrinkage-induced attenuation, so shrinkage cannot explain the non-detection of non-linearity in the observed data.

K Additional Insights into Mechanisms of Complementarity

To provide an additional window into the mechanism of complementarity we use model-based, itemlevel decomposition to explore item-level differences across difficulty and task domain. Specifically, we quantify shifts from answering incorrectly alone to answering correctly with the help of AI via posterior predictive counterfactuals from the fitted hierarchical IRT model (Eq. A1). This has the added advantage that we can compute counterfactuals for each user-question for both models (GPT-4o and Llama-3.1-8B).

On average, users switch from wrong answers to right answers on 25% of the tasks when working with Llama-3.1-8B and 29% when working with GPT-4o, indicating a substantial share of cases where collaboration moves responses from incorrect to correct. Users also switch in the opposite direction but this happens less often. Users switch from right-alone to wrong-with-AI 8.6% with GPT-4o and 12.3% with Llama-3.1-8B. Net switch rates are credibly positive for both models (GPT-4o: 20% [95% CI: 17.4%, 23%]; Llama-3.1-8B: 12.7% [95% CI: 9.8%, 15.5%]). Switch rates from wrong-to-correct answers increase monotonically with item difficulty for both models. On easy questions (1st decile) users switch 17% (Llama-3.1-8B) and 19% (GPT-4o). On hard questions (10th decile) users switch 31% (Llama-3.1-8B) and 38% (GPT-4o). Damaging switches similarly increase with question difficulty (from 7% → 10% with GPT-4o; 10% → 14% with Llama-3.1-8B). This pattern is precisely what complementarity predicts: collaboration is most beneficial on harder items and in line with our results shown in Figure 2A.

Next, we break out complementarity by user skill, cut into terciles (Table 2). Unconditionally, damage rises with user skill and gains fall. However, this gradient is fully explained by opportunity: damage requires being correct alone. Conditional on opportunity, per-opportunity gain and damage rates are statistically flat across ability terciles. Harm from AI collaboration is therefore not concentrated in any skill group; it scales with how much a user has to lose, at a uniform per-opportunity rate determined mainly by the AI arm (GPT-4o helps more than Llama-3.1-8B).

Decomposing the damage rate into exposure and susceptibility, P (harmful switch) = P (AI wrong)× P (harmful switch | AI wrong), shows that Llama-3.1-8B's higher damage rate is driven almost entirely by exposure-its advice is wrong on 58% of items versus 31% for GPT-4o-rather notably, users of the more accurate model are not more susceptible per error, contrary to the concern that greater trust induces greater over-reliance. Breaking out complementarity by domain we find the biggest complementarity for physics questions (32% (GPT-4o) and 28% (Llama-3.1-8B)), followed by math (29% (GPT-4o) and 25% (Llama-3.1-8B)), and moral reasoning (26% and 22%). Our dataset contains three questions (items) per user in the solo condition. One concern may be that three items may be insufficient to precisely estimate user-level ability. However, our hierarchical model uses partial pooling to borrow strength across users and items. Regarding measurement precision, we observe a population standard deviation of σ θ = 0.85 and a median posterior SE of 0.91. N Bivariate Partial Information Decomposition (PID)

We compute a bivariate partial information decomposition (PID) as a model-free alternative to the the IRT analyses reported above. For this we use the answer-first arm of the dataset where users answer the same question first alone and then with AI (Chang et al., 2025). Because PID requires the joint distribution of sources and target on the same units, the direct-to-AI arm cannot support this analysis as solo and joint items never overlap within an individual. PID allows us to decompose the information provided by two sources about a target into unique information provided by each source, redundant information provided by both, and synergistic information that neither source alone contains (Riedl & Rosas, 2026). For each individual-question trial we defined three binary variables: H, provisional answer correct; A, AI correct on the item (arm-matched few-shot accuracy > 0.5 for the AI model the participant was paired with); and Y , final answer correct (3,038 trials, 343 users, 396 items).

We then decompose I(H, A; Y ) into redundant, unique, and synergistic atoms using the BROJA measure (Bertschinger et al., 2014). The implementation is a convex program over distributions preserving the (H, Y ) and (A, Y ) marginals (minimum-mutual-information is identical to BROJA in our case given the Blackwell-dominated finding below). We report bias-corrected estimates by subtracting the mean of a permutation null (Y shuffled within stratum, R = 500); 95% intervals are from a user-level cluster bootstrap (B = 1, 000). We report atoms for all items and by tercile of solo item difficulty. To initialize the optimizer, we smooth empty cells (ϵ = 10 -6 ).

The PID and IRT analyses complement each other rather than being redundant. The IRT model estimates person-level latent abilities and question difficulties, propagates uncertainty, and identifies who benefits, on which items, and whether the mechanism is additive; the PID makes no such structural assumptions and instead partitions the information that human and AI carry about the final outcome into unique, redundant and synergistic components, providing a model-free test that the synergistic component is positive.

We find the synergistic atom is credibly positive overall and in the hard and medium strata (Figure A3). Synergy also accounts for a larger share of total information on hard items, consistent with the IRT finding that collaborative gains concentrate where solo performance is weakest. This positive synergistic atom is expected under the additive-logit structure recovered in the main IRT analyses.

The decomposition is inconsistent with pure substitution, under which the final answer would be a copy of the AI's and Un(A) would dominate. Instead, the users initial answer-whether correct or incorrect-strongly dictates the final outcome. Un(A) is zero because the question-level AI variable is Blackwell-dominated by the provisional answer, so the PID cannot separate the AI's contribution from the participant's. Consider this alternative: If the human simply closed their eyes, ignored their initial instinct (H), and copied the AI (A), then Y would be a copy of A. In that scenario, A would Blackwell-dominate H, and Un(H) would be zero. Because Un(A) = 0 and U n(H) > 0, the human is not mere adopting the AI's answer. Note that using the few-shot accuracy of the AI for A is only a proxy for the AI's contribution on the question. It does not fully capture the AI portion of the dialogue. The dialogue likely carries more information, thus making our findings of synergy a conservative estimate and contributing to the Un(A) ∼ 0 estimates. Detecting a credibly positive synergistic atom despite this extreme informational bottleneck provides robust evidence of true collaborative gains. Because the user decides whether to retain or revise their preliminary answer, information from the AI enters the final outcome only through that decision, which loads it onto Syn and Un(H) rather than Un(A); this pattern is what calibrated reliance predicts (Caplin et al., 2026), in which adoption is conditional on the participant's assessment of their own and the AI's likely correctness (the three-variable decomposition cannot itself separate calibrated adoption from unconditional anchoring).

Early empirical work found that users benefit from forming accurate mental models of AI(Bansal et al., 2019b;Paleja et al., 2021; Bansal et al., 2019a;Gero et al., 2020; Alipour et al., 2021), though this effect is not always consistent(Fügener et al., 2022).

The human tendency to treat nonhuman agents as humanlike and apply social heuristics and mental-state inferences to interactive systems was documented even with pre-AI software(Reeves & Nass, 1996; Nass & Moon, 2000; Weizenbaum, 1966; Waytz et al., 2010). Whether LLMs have a generalizable ToM is currently an open question, see for example(Prakash et al., 2025).

This framework stands in contrast to traditional tool use, such as working with a notebook(Clark & Chalmers, 1998). A notebook functions as a pure storage device-it cannot act independently, making its "behavior" entirely predictable. Effective notebook use relies on meta-cognitive awareness of one's own memory limitations rather than theory of mind about another agent's capabilities. As a result, individuals skilled at self-monitoring and organizing information benefit most from notebooks, not those adept at understanding and coordinating with other minds. Working with a notebook, users are not required to form a mental model of why the notebook inserts certain keywords, whether it will auto-correct technical terms, or when it might reorganize pages.
