Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). Statcheck might still miss one-sided tests that are described in another sentence, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. Abstract

Addiction recovery has become a central concept in research, treatment and recovery-oriented systems of care, but its definition remains difficult to operationalise and translate into practice. This conceptual review examines influential definitions of addiction recovery from international organisations, academic literature and lived-experience research, synthesises key sources of heterogeneity in recovery journeys, and considers recovery capital as an ecosystemic condition shaping resources, barriers and opportunities over time.

Recent Findings: Building on this synthesis, the article proposes nine core defining characteristics of addiction recovery: dynamic and non-linear; personal and idiosyncratic; multidimensional; intentional and change-oriented; centred on managing addiction-related vulnerability and suffering; hope-oriented; identity-and meaning-oriented; relational and community-based; and contextually situated and resource-sensitive. Addiction recovery is defined as a deeply personal, relational and non-linear process of meaningful change across multiple life domains, through which people manage addiction-related vulnerabilities and redefine themselves and their lives towards a more meaningful and liveable life, shaped by available resources and supports.

The proposed ecosystemic framework integrates three levels of analysis: core defining characteristics, sources of heterogeneity and recovery capital. It seeks to address conceptual tensions by incorporating addiction-related vulnerabilities, suffering, ambivalence in commitment to change, hope and the idea of a "liveable life". In doing so, the framework supports conceptual clarity while remaining sensitive to diversity, non-linearity and lived experience, and offers practical guidance for research, assessment, treatment planning, service design and recovery-oriented care.

Introduction

The recovery model emerged as a response to pathology-centred approaches and acute care models primarily oriented towards symptom reduction, abstinence and short-term stabilisation [1, 2]. This model developed in parallel within mental health and addictions, sharing common foundations while following distinct historical trajectories [3]. In mental health, the recovery movement has its roots in anti-psychiatry, survivor and service-user movements, which challenged overly medicalised models of care and advocated for human rights, personal agency and lived experience [4, 5]. In addictions, the recovery movement followed a more intermittent trajectory, with lived experience becoming more or less central across different historical periods, from early community-based initiatives to the professionalisation and institutionalisation of addiction treatment. Over time, the limitations of acute care models highlighted the need for person-centred, long-term and recovery-oriented approaches capable of responding to complex needs across multiple life domains [6].

These developments contributed to a broader understanding of recovery, centred on rights, self-determination, quality of life, community participation and the reconstruction of a meaningful life beyond illness or addiction-related problems [7, 8]. This shift was consolidated through several conceptual and institutional milestones. First, recovery in mental health was conceptualised as a deeply personal process involving new meaning beyond the effects of mental illness [7]. Second, recovery in addictions was defined as a sustained process involving individuals, families and communities in resolving alcohol and other drug problems, managing continued vulnerability and developing a healthy and meaningful life [9]. The distinction between clinical and personal recovery further broadened the concept beyond symptom remission, abstinence or return to normative functioning, emphasising instead a unique, heterogeneous and non-linear process grounded in lived experience, growth, empowerment, community participation and the pursuit of a meaningful life [3, [10] [11]. Third, the guiding principles of recovery were systematised, including the recognition that recovery encompasses the whole of a person's life, unfolds as an ongoing and non-linear journey and is not a professional intervention in itself [12]. Fourth, recovery-oriented systems of care were progressively incorporated into policies and service frameworks internationally [13] [14] [15]. This shift has been associated with two major transitions: from symptom-and pathology-centred models towards long-term recovery-oriented approaches, and from acute care models towards more sustained and continuing care models [16, 17].

As recovery has become an increasingly central goal of addiction services, research and policy, conceptual clarity has become more important [18, 19]. Recovery is no longer understood only as an outcome defined by the absence of symptoms or substance use, but as a broader process of personal, relational and community change. However, defining and operationalising addiction recovery remains complex, partly because recovery is deeply personal, idiosyncratic and characterised by multiple possible trajectories [20, 21]. Existing definitions differ in scope and emphasis, but also converge on several shared characteristics. This convergence offers an opportunity to examine common elements and unresolved tensions in order to identify the main defining characteristics of addiction recovery and developing an integrative framework that may offer practical guidance for service design, assessment and recovery-oriented care.

For this reason, the present conceptual review examines influential definitions of addiction recovery, considers key sources of heterogeneity in recovery journeys, and discusses recovery capital as an ecosystemic condition that may shape recovery processes. Building on this synthesis, the article proposes a set of core defining characteristics, an integrative definition and an ecosystemic framework of addiction recovery.

Definitions of Recovery by International Organisations

Definitions proposed by international organisations have played an important role in broadening addiction recovery beyond the cessation of substance use. The Betty Ford Institute Consensus Panel defined recovery as "a voluntarily maintained lifestyle characterised by sobriety, personal health, and citizenship" [23, p. 222]. This definition was influential because it incorporated health and citizenship alongside sobriety, linking recovery to quality of life and community participation [22].

The UK Drug Policy Commission Recovery Consensus Group [24] defined recovery as "a process characterised by voluntarily sustained control over substance use which maximises health and well-being and participation in the rights, roles, and responsibilities of society" [p. 6]. This represents a relevant shift because it introduces the notion of sustained control, thereby allowing for more diverse recovery trajectories.

The American Society of Addiction Medicine (ASAM) [25] defines recovery as "a process of sustained action that addresses the biological, psychological, social, and spiritual disturbances inherent in addiction" [p. 2]. This definition contributes a clinically oriented and multidimensional understanding of recovery, emphasising sustained effort, behavioural regulation, quality of life and holistic healing.

SAMHSA [17] defines recovery as "a process of change through which individuals improve their health and wellness, live self-directed lives, and strive to reach their full potential" [p. 1]. This definition has been especially influential because of its transdiagnostic and policy-oriented character, linking recovery to health, home, purpose, community and hope.

Overall, organisational definitions have helped to institutionalise a broader understanding of recovery by incorporating health, wellbeing, citizenship, self-direction and social participation. However, they also show an evolution from definitions centred on sobriety, to formulations based on sustained control, and finally to broader definitions that do not explicitly include substance use criteria. This broadening allows recovery to be understood across diverse pathways and in dialogue with mental health recovery, but it may also make the concept more difficult to translate into practice and operationalise. Moreover, highly aspirational definitions may risk generating idealised expectations when recovery is framed mainly around full potential or optimal wellbeing.

Definitions of Recovery by Academic Experts

Academic definitions have contributed to the conceptual and operational refinement of addiction recovery. White [9] proposed one of the most influential definitions, describing recovery as an experience that transcends the cessation of substance use and situates it within a broader process of life reconstruction at the individual, family, and community levels. This formulation is particularly relevant because it situates recovery beyond substance use outcomes and explicitly incorporates healing, vulnerability management, life reconstruction and ecological resources.

El-Guebaly [26] identified three core dimensions: 1) The resolution of substance use problems, with particular attention to chronicity and sustained management; 2) improvements in overall functioning and wellbeing; and 3) participation in meaningful social and community roles. This contribution reinforces the idea that recovery cannot be reduced to clinical improvement alone, but involves sustained management of substance-related problems, improved functioning and social participation closely connected to wellbeing and citizenship.

Kelly and Hoeppner [27] advanced the operationalisation of recovery through a biaxial model composed of remission and recovery capital. In this model, increasingly stable remission may facilitate the accumulation of recovery capital, while recovery capital may support the maintenance of remission. Their definition highlights the dynamic relationship between substance use cessation, recovery capital and quality of life, while also raising a conceptual issue: recovery capital may be both a condition supporting recovery and part of how recovery is operationalised. Francis et al. [28] extended this formulation through a social model showing how networks, appraisal of threats and resources, trauma and structural oppression shape recovery journeys. It makes visible the social and structural processes through which recovery is enabled or constrained, moving the field further away from individualised accounts of recovery.

Overall, academic definitions and models have strengthened the conceptual precision of recovery by moving beyond abstinence alone towards the sustained management of substance use, meaningful life, recovery capital, and the social and structural conditions that shape recovery journeys. This supports a more ecosystemic understanding of recovery, but also raises a key tension regarding where and how recovery capital should be integrated within the conceptualisation and operationalisation of recovery.

Definitions of Recovery from Lived Experience

Research grounded in lived experience has been essential for developing more realistic, inclusive and person-centred understandings of addiction recovery [29] [30] [31]. These accounts highlight aspects that may be less visible in clinical or policy definitions, including identity reconstruction, suffering, ambiguity, relational change, hope, safety and the everyday work of sustaining recovery.

Laudet [31] showed that recovery was often experienced as a "new life", understood as an ongoing process of growth, personal change, and reconstruction of the self. Best et al. [29] found that people in recovery described the process as continuous and as an experience that includes, but transcends, freedom from dependence, involving changes in identity, values and social networks. These studies emphasise identity-related processes and orientation towards a new life, highlighting recovery as a process of rebuilding both life and sense of self.

Neale et al. [32] added an important corrective by showing that service users often experienced recovery as closer to "coping" than to "being cured", involving suffering, fear of relapse, ambiguity and bodily, emotional and social changes. This work challenges overly idealised definitions of recovery and calls for more realistic conceptualisations that remain attentive to the difficult and painful aspects of the process.

Further lived-experience studies have described recovery as learning, growth, personal development and a "way of being", while distinguishing it from remission and quality of life [33]; as a lifelong, multidimensional and non-linear process involving improvements across multiple life areas [34]; and as an idiosyncratic, relational and enduring process oriented towards a different future, supported by time, social support and safe spaces [30]. Together, these studies underscore the relational, multidimensional and dynamic nature of recovery, as well as the need to strengthen personal and social resources to sustain it over time.

Lived-experience research has contributed to a more situated and realistic understanding of recovery by grounding it in people's realities, meanings and ambivalences. It highlights identity reconstruction, multidimensional change, relational dynamics, hope, nonlinearity and the reconstruction of a meaningful life. It also raises two important tensions: recovery should be distinguished from related concepts such as remission and quality of life, and definitions should avoid idealised accounts that obscure the suffering, vulnerability and ongoing challenges that can accompany recovery journeys.

Consensus on Core Characteristics of Addiction Recovery

Several reviews and consensus efforts have attempted to synthesise the defining characteristics of recovery. Leamy et al. [35] described personal recovery in mental health recovery as a unique and non-linear journey involving stages, struggle and gradual transformations across personal, relational and contextual dimensions. These characteristics overlap substantially with addiction recovery models, including the principles proposed by Sheedy and Whitter [14], who described recovery as a personal, holistic, and dynamic process that may follow multiple pathways and involves healing, identity reconstruction, hope, coping with stigma, and social and community support oriented towards rebuilding a meaningful life.

The Recovery Science Research Collaborative later proposed a consensus definition of recovery as "an individualized, intentional, dynamic, and relational process involving sustained efforts to improve wellness" [18, p. 183]. More recently, Klevan et al. [36] integrated findings from meta-syntheses on mental health and substance use recovery, describing recovery as emerging through dynamic interactions between the person, others and social and material contexts. Their model is articulated through the processes of being, doing and accessing, and understands recovery as an intentional and future-oriented process.

These consensus efforts suggest that addiction recovery is increasingly understood as a personal, intentional, dynamic, relational and multidimensional process, oriented towards wellbeing, meaning and the reconstruction of everyday life. Recovery is also commonly described as non-linear, sustained over time and embedded within social and material contexts.

Two key tensions remain. The first concerns how intentionality should be understood and addressed. The second concerns the need for a definition broad enough to capture diverse recovery pathways, but not so broad that it becomes difficult to apply in practice or operationalise in research. This latter tension is closely connected to another central feature of addiction recovery: its idiosyncrasy and heterogeneity [20, 37].

The Idiosyncrasy and Heterogeneity of Recovery

Building on the definitional tensions outlined above, heterogeneity should not be understood as a limitation of the recovery concept, but as one of its defining challenges.

Differences in recovery reflect both the multiple ways in which addiction is experienced and the diverse pathways through which people initiate and sustain meaningful change in their lives. Recovery therefore needs to be conceptualised in ways that are sufficiently coherent to guide research, assessment and care, while remaining flexible enough to reflect diverse lived experiences, needs and trajectories.

Gender

Gender may shape recovery trajectories through specific vulnerabilities, needs, social roles and access to resources [38, 39]. Women often face barriers related to stigma, guilt and shame, mental health difficulties, caregiving responsibilities, fear of losing child custody and exposure to interpersonal violence [40, 41]. These barriers may be intensified by intersecting forms of disadvantage, such as homelessness, criminal justice involvement, sex work or racialised stigma [42, 43]. At the same time, women may draw on specific recovery resources, including informal and relational supports, expressive or spiritual activities, digital technologies and companion animals [44, 45]. Gender highlights the need to understand recovery as shaped by social roles, stigma and unequal access to recovery opportunities.

Life Course

Recovery is also shaped by life course. Emerging adults and older adults may face different recovery needs, risks and opportunities [46]. Emerging adulthood, often situated between 18 and 29 years, is characterised by identity exploration, experimentation, instability, self-focus and a sense of being between adolescence and adulthood [47, 48]. Recovery at this stage may therefore unfold within a context of life transitions, relational instability, limited structural resources and an identity still under construction. Older adults, often situated between 50 and 65 years, experience recovery after longer substance use trajectories, greater accumulated consequences and specific life-stage challenges, including declining health, medication interactions, social isolation, retirement, bereavement and shame [49, 46]. These differences suggest that recovery needs may vary substantially across the life course.

Relational Context

The literature highlights the importance of the relational context in recovery processes, showing that the quality, size, and composition of social networks are associated with recovery capital and long-term recovery experiences [50, 51]. Partners, children, family members, peers and wider networks may provide emotional support, belonging, accountability and motivation [52]. However, social relationships are not uniformly protective; they may also generate pressure, risk or ambivalence depending on their quality, composition and timing [53].

Network diversity may be especially relevant in early recovery, whereas close and meaningful relationships may become more important as recovery consolidates [54]. These findings highlight the dynamic nature of relational contexts and the importance of assessing them carefully across the recovery process.

Structural Context

Recovery is also shaped by structural and material conditions. Access to services, the organisation of care systems, cultural responsiveness, housing, employment, income and neighbourhood conditions may influence recovery opportunities and barriers [55, 56, 39].

Economic stability, housing stability and employment stability have been linked to better psychosocial functioning, social integration and continuity in recovery [57, 58]. These findings indicate that recovery cannot be fully understood without considering the structural conditions in which it unfolds.

Severity and Consequences of Addiction

Recovery trajectories may differ according to addiction severity and the accumulation of consequences at the point of recovery initiation [59, 60]. Some people begin recovery while maintaining relatively stable housing, employment and social relationships, whereas others initiate change after significant personal, relational, material and functional losses [61]. This continuum may shape initial vulnerability, pace of change and the type and intensity of support required.

Substance Type and Relationship to Use

Substance type and the relationship people maintain with substances are also important sources of variability. Recovery trajectories may differ across alcohol, opioids and other illicit substances, not only because of pharmacological differences, but also because of the social, legal and health contexts in which use occurs [62, 63]. Complete abstinence may be central for many people, particularly in severe or long-standing dependence, whereas other trajectories may involve sustained reductions, low-risk use or medically supported pathways, depending on substance type, severity, context and personal goals [64, 65]. This reinforces the need for definitions that are sufficiently clear, but not restricted to a single pathway.

Recovery Pathways

The literature consistently shows that there are multiple pathways through which people may initiate and sustain recovery, including formal pathways (professional treatment), informal pathways (mutual aid groups, spiritual, community, or culturally grounded resources), and nonassisted pathways, commonly referred to as "natural recovery" [66, 37]. More recent work has also highlighted the relevance of employment support, recovery housing, recovery check-ups and recovery support services for sustaining long-term recovery [67]. Many people combine or move between different forms of support over time, and some achieve recovery without engaging in formal or informal treatment [64, 68].

Time in Recovery

Time in recovery is also associated with changes in recovery strengths, barriers, quality of life and wellbeing. Research indicates that people in more advanced stages of recovery report fewer housing problems, lower involvement in crime, lower illicit substance use and greater engagement in employment, education or training, while recovery capital tends to increase over time, particularly recovery strengths, and barriers may decrease more modestly and persist even in stable recovery [69, 21]. Qualitative and longitudinal research further suggests that priorities may evolve from urgent and practical needs in earlier stages towards deeper identity, relational and existential transformations as recovery becomes more consolidated [70, 30, 71]. These findings support the cumulative nature of recovery, while cautioning against the assumption that all people move through the same sequence.

Turning Points and Dynamic Change Processes

Recovery trajectories may also be shaped by turning points and dynamic push-and-pull processes, such as adverse substance-related experiences, becoming a parent, "hitting rock bottom", changes in social networks and environments, or engagement with treatment [72].

These events do not operate uniformly; their meaning and impact depend on personal history, social context, available resources and recovery stage. Similarly, pull factors towards recovery and the pains of recovery may vary across personal, social and community domains and across different stages of the process [73, 74]. Sustained change rarely appears to result from a single isolated event, but rather from the interaction between structural barriers, recovery resources and personal meaning-making [74]. This reinforces the idea that recovery is dynamic and continuously reconfigured over time.

Overall, these dimensions of heterogeneity help identify the multiple factors that may influence recovery processes across time and contexts. They also highlight the importance of a definition that can accommodate diversity, non-linearity and contextual variation. Recovery capital is particularly useful in this regard, as it helps explain how personal, social and community resources, together with barriers and unmet needs, may shape the opportunities through which recovery journeys unfold.

Recovery Capital

Recovery journeys are strongly shaped by the resources, opportunities and barriers available to each person [75, 76]. Granfield and Cloud [77] introduced the concept of recovery capital, defining it as "the sum of one's total resources that can be brought to bear in an effort to overcome alcohol and other drug dependency" [p. 179]. Grounded in an ecological perspective, recovery capital refers to the quantity, quality and accessibility of resources at individual, social and community levels that may support the initiation, maintenance and consolidation of recovery [75, 78]. More recently, it has been described as the "resources and capacities that enable growth and human flourishing" [56], reinforcing its strengths-based orientation and its close relationship with wellbeing and quality of life.

A central premise of recovery capital theory is that greater access to resources and fewer barriers are generally associated with more stable recovery [79]. This is particularly relevant because recovery capital is not equally distributed. Some individuals enter recovery with greater access to stable housing, employment, supportive relationships, health care, education and community resources, whereas others begin recovery in contexts marked by accumulated losses, social exclusion or structural disadvantage [80, 81]. In this regard, the balance between recovery capital and addiction severity may help inform the type and intensity of support required [82]. Identifying, developing and monitoring recovery capital may therefore support more personalised and recovery-oriented care planning.

Recovery capital is commonly understood as comprising three interrelated dimensions [78]. Personal recovery capital includes human and material resources, such as physical and mental health, coping skills, self-efficacy, education, vocational capacities, safe housing and stable income. Social recovery capital refers to accessible relationships and supports, including family, friends, peer networks, neighbourhoods and communities of support. It depends not only on the presence of social ties, but also on their quality, composition and actual availability, and includes participation in meaningful social and community activities that reinforce belonging [83]. Community recovery capital includes structural and community-level resources, such as access to health and social services, diverse treatment options, housing, employment, educational opportunities, recovery-supportive policies and safe community environments [84, 85]. Beyond these dimensions, authors converge on several key characteristics of recovery capital [79]: a) it encompasses personal, social, and structural factors; b) it is grounded in a strengths-based approach oriented towards generating resources and capacities to initiate and sustain recovery; c) its components interact synergistically; d) it is dynamic and varies over time and across life circumstances; and e) individuals possess different levels of recovery capital, which influence their recovery journeys.

Importantly, recovery capital also includes barriers and unmet needs. Cloud and Granfield [86] introduced the notion of a positive-negative continuum, noting that, alongside resources, there are barriers or forms of negative recovery capital that may constrain recovery.

These include social networks characterised by active substance use, unsafe environments, discrimination, stigma, poverty, housing instability and limited employment opportunities.

Although debate remains as to whether these barriers should be understood as part of recovery capital or as a distinct domain, their inclusion is important because it prevents recovery from being understood only through strengths and resources [79].

Overall, recovery capital offers a useful ecosystemic lens for understanding how recovery journeys are enabled, constrained and transformed over time. It helps make visible the role of social and structural inequalities, while also supporting strengths-based and personalised approaches to care. From this perspective, assessing recovery capital may help monitor strengths and barriers, inform clinical decision-making and support recovery planning [75].

Integrative Synthesis: Core Characteristics, Integrative Definition and Ecosystemic

Framework

The literature reviewed above points towards an understanding of addiction recovery as a multidimensional, dynamic and contextually situated process, characterised by idiosyncrasy, non-linearity and substantial variation across people and over time. This creates a central challenge: recovery needs to be defined broadly enough to reflect lived experience and diverse trajectories, but clearly enough to guide research, assessment and practice. If defined too narrowly, personal, relational and contextual dimensions may be overlooked; if defined too broadly, recovery may become difficult to operationalise or translate into care planning and service design. Building on this review, the present synthesis proposes three interrelated contributions: a set of core defining characteristics of addiction recovery, an integrative definition, and a conceptual framework that situates recovery within the personal, relational and structural conditions in which it unfolds.

Core defining characteristics of addiction recovery

1. Dynamic and non-linear: Recovery involves change, learning, adaptation and transformation over time, in interaction with other people and the broader context. Progress, setbacks, relapse, stagnation and growth may coexist within the same process, while needs, resources and priorities are reorganised.

2. Personal and idiosyncratic.

Each recovery process is unique and shaped by the person's history, values, motivations, needs and context. Within each process, different life moments and stages may be expressed in distinct ways, reflecting the diversity of possible recovery trajectories. Multiple pathways to recovery exist, involving different ways of relating to substances.

3. Multidimensional: Recovery involves meaningful changes across multiple life domains,

including the relationship with substance use, symptom management, mental and physical health, relationships, everyday functioning, purpose, and social and community participation.

It does not imply perfection or complete resolution in all areas, but rather a sufficiently significant changes through which life becomes more coherent, meaningful and liveable.

4. Intentional and change-oriented.

Recovery involves some degree of intentionality and orientation towards change, expressed through efforts to commit to new ways of living. This commitment may coexist with ambivalence, doubt, fear, frustration, hopelessness and fluctuations in motivation.

5. Centred on managing addiction-related vulnerability and suffering.

Recovery involves the ongoing management of addiction-related vulnerabilities and difficulties, including distress, suffering, uncertainty, fear, craving, ambivalence, self-deception, high-risk situations, relapse risk and broader substance use-related challenges.

6. Hope-oriented: Recovery is sustained, at least in part, by the possibility of a different future and by the belief that meaningful change remains possible. Hope can act as a driving force for facing the inherent challenges of recovery and sustaining orientation towards a different future.

7. Identity and meaning-oriented. Recovery entails a redefinition of identity beyond addiction-related problems. For some people, this may involve reconstructing a damaged sense of self; for others, recovering a lost self, reconnecting with who they feel they are, or becoming someone they were previously unable to be. In all cases, identity, values and personal meaning orient the recovery process towards a meaningful life.

8. Relational and community-based. Recovery unfolds in interaction with others and within communities. Social connection, support, meaningful relationships, stigma, belonging and social roles may shape how recovery is experienced and sustained. 9. Contextually situated and resource-sensitive. Recovery is shaped by structural and contextual conditions, including access to services, safe and stable environments, education, employment opportunities and recovery-supportive settings. The availability of material and social resources may influence both the initiation and long-term maintenance of recovery.

Integrative definition of addiction recovery

Addiction recovery is a deeply personal, relational and non-linear process of meaningful change across multiple life domains, through which people manage addiction-related vulnerabilities and redefine themselves and their lives towards a more meaningful and liveable life, shaped by available resources and supports.

Integrative Ecosystemic Framework of Addiction Recovery

An integrative conceptual framework is proposed that brings together three interconnected levels of analysis: the defining characteristics of recovery, the main sources of heterogeneity in recovery journeys, and recovery capital as an ecosystemic condition that may structure opportunities, barriers and supports over time (See Figure 1). The framework represents addiction recovery as a deeply personal, relational, dynamic and contextually situated process in which the management of addiction-related vulnerabilities coexists with hope for the possibility of a different life. It recognises that recovery may begin from different starting points shaped by age, gender, culture, accumulated consequences, addiction severity, substance type, previous recovery experiences, relational support and structural resources, influencing the form, pace and direction of change. Within each recovery journey, progress, setbacks, relapse, stagnation and growth may coexist across multiple life domains, including substance use, health, relationships, community and everyday functioning. Recovery is understood as a process oriented towards new ways of living, involving the redefinition of the self, social roles and the search for a more meaningful and liveable life, while unfolding within a changing configuration of personal, social and structural strengths and barriers.

The framework is intended to support a more comprehensive and practically useful understanding of addiction recovery while remaining sensitive to diversity, non-linearity and lived experience. It offers a practical guide to the key defining characteristics of addiction recovery and broadens the focus from abstinence or control over substance use to the sustained management of addiction-related vulnerabilities, including craving, high-risk situations, relapse risk and the broader ways in which addiction may shape everyday life and decisions. It also seeks to avoid an overly idealised view of recovery by recognising its difficult, painful and non-linear character, including sustained challenges, uncertainty, anxiety, fear, suffering and hopelessness. It highlights intentionality through commitment to change, while recognising that this commitment may fluctuate over time, and recognises hope as a driving force that may help people remain oriented towards a different life. Finally, it emphasises multidimensionality and the idea of a "liveable life", understanding recovery as involving meaningful changes in relevant life domains and the building of a personal, meaningful and manageable life, rather than requiring perfect or complete transformation in every area.

Implications for Research, Assessment, Service Design and Recovery-Oriented Care

The proposed framework may have several implications for addiction recovery research, assessment, treatment design and service planning. By integrating common elements across previous definitions and addressing some of their conceptual tensions, the framework may help respond to a persistent challenge in the field: the coexistence of multiple definitions of recovery with different levels of scope, emphasis and operational clarity. In this sense, the framework seeks to balance breadth and operational clarity. It is flexible enough to accommodate the complexity and diversity of recovery, while remaining concrete enough to support operationalisation. This allows it to be adapted to different research aims, levels of analysis and recovery profiles. Finally, the framework may inform service design and policy by highlighting the need for recovery-oriented systems of care that are sufficiently coordinated, flexible and sustained over time. Such systems should be able to respond to the non-linear nature of recovery, maintain hope across the process and support life reconstruction at personal, relational and structural levels. The timing, intensity, continuity and eligibility criteria for support and resources may therefore need to be reconsidered, so that services can accompany ambivalence, uncertainty, suffering, fluctuating commitment and deeper changes related to identity and meaning-making.

Conclusion

Overall, the proposed definition and framework aim to offer a viable and practical conceptual guide for understanding, assessing and supporting addiction recovery. By synthesising common elements across previous definitions, the framework seeks to reduce conceptual fragmentation and provide a clearer basis for research, intervention development and recovery-oriented service planning, while remaining sensitive to the complexity, diversity and lived experience of recovery journeys.
