To check Non-Significant P Value Check

What we found

We found 5 non-significant p values that should be checked for appropriate interpretation.

Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. Full text

Abstract

In decision-making, option values can arise from past experiences with rewards and punishments (experiential) or from explicit descriptions of outcomes and probabilities (descriptive). While most studies have examined experiential and descriptive options separately, real-world decisions, such as deciding between a restaurant we visited before and one known through online reviews, often entail choosing between the two. Recently, Garcia et al. (2023) revealed that experiential options are systematically neglected in such hybrid choices. Yet, the cause of this experiential neglect remains unknown. To test whether this phenomenon results from representational differences between experiential and descriptive options, we manipulated the learning architecture, test-phase feedback, stimulus format, and feedback format over five experiments (n = 375). Participants showed reduced sensitivity to experiential losses relative to gains. Importantly, experiential neglect persisted across all conditions, eliminating these hypotheses as explanations of this phenomenon. Rather, we propose that neglect may stem from retrieval-based uncertainty in experiential valuation. Together, these findings provide evidence for a cognitive constraint on the integration of experiential and described values in decision-making.

Introduction

In everyday life, as in the laboratory, the subjective values that guide decision-making derive from two main sources of information: experience and description. Experiential values are learned from past outcomes, whereas descriptive ones are informed by explicit information about decision-relevant variables, such as probabilities and rewards. Real-world scenarios rarely involve only a single source of information (Hertwig et Erev 2009; Weiss-Cohen et al. 2016), as people routinely need to arbitrate between what they have learned from experience and what they know through external symbolic information. Consider, for instance, choosing between a restaurant already visited in the past and one discovered through online reviews. How experiential and descriptive values compete in such hybrid choices stands as a fundamental question.

However, most existing studies have examined experiential and descriptive decisions in isolation (Hertwig et Erev 2009; Hertwig et al. 2004; Barron et Erev 2003; Erev et al. 2017; Rakow et Newell 2010) which limits the possibility of testing this question in situations where subjective values are constructed in different ways. A recent study by Garcia et al. (2023) addressed this issue by using an original hybrid decision-making paradigm in which participants chose between learned experiential options and explicitly described ones. They found that individuals systematically underweight the options whose value is learned from experience -a phenomenon referred to as experiential value neglect. Importantly, Garcia et al. (2023) ruled out alternative explanations for this neglect, including insufficient learning, lack of incentives, or extreme ambiguity aversion. Yet, despite robust evidence for experiential value neglect, the mechanisms underlying the phenomenon remain poorly understood.

One possibility is that experiential neglect arises from representational differences between experiential and descriptive values. According to this view, experiential and descriptive values may not be encoded in comparable representational spaces (Walasek et Brown 2024; Garcia et al. 2023). Alternatively, experiential neglect may reflect constraints intrinsic to memory-based representations (Giguère et Love 2013).

There is a need to distinguish between these compet-Preprint, version of 13 November 2026.

ing accounts. Specifically, the present study focuses on testing whether the phenomenon stems from representational asymmetries between experiential and descriptive values. Among possible computational sources of representational differences, we examined whether experiential neglect arises from policy-based learning that prevents direct comparison with descriptive values (Bennett, Niv, et Langdon 2021; Hayden et Niv 2021); from distorted representations of descriptive option values (Prelec 1998; Kahneman et Tversky 1979; Tversky et Kahneman 1992); from the greater perceptual salience of symbolic representations (Bordalo, Gennaioli, et Shleifer 2013; Zilker et Pachur 2022a , 2022b , 2023; Krajbich, Armel, et Rangel 2010; Smith et Krajbich 2019); or from a representational incompatibility between experiential and descriptive values (Hertwig et Erev 2009; Stewart, Chater, et Brown 2006).

To address these questions, we independently manipulated key features of both the learning and the description components of the task. Namely, we varied (i) the learning architecture, (ii) the test-phase feedback, (iii) the stimulus representation, and (iv) the feedback representation.

Across all manipulations, the value of experiential options, as inferred from choice patterns, exerted a reduced influence on choices between experiential and descriptive options. Crucially, a declarative rating task (stated preferences) directly elicited the value of experiential options. It confirmed that experiential values are correctly learned and can be accurately recovered. This data further substantiates the idea of a genuine neglect of learned value information in hybrid choices.

Overall, we propose that experiential neglect stems from intrinsic features of memory-based representations, namely their perceived lower reliability relative to perceptually available options, rather than structural representational differences between options.

Finally, to further test the generalizability of the experiential neglect pattern, we adapted our analytical framework to assess the effect of the outcome valence dimension. Indeed, both description-based and experience-based decision-making research shows important asymmetries in how values are constructed in the gain and loss domains (Kahneman et Tversky 1984; Palminteri et Pessiglione 2017; Palminteri et al. 2015). We therefore tested whether experiential value neglect generalized across this valence dimension, but also whether experiential values were learned and retrieved symmetrically in the gain and loss domains. We found that experiential value neglect was present in each valence domain, and that negative experiential values were less accurately retrieved compared to positive ones -a result consistent with established asymmetric behavioral patterns across valence domains (Tversky et Kahneman 1992; Ruggeri et al. 2020; Yechiam et Hochman 2013; Palminteri et Lebreton 2022).

Together, these findings deepen our understanding of the cognitive constraints involved in integrating ex-periential and descriptive value information during decision-making.

Results

Experimental design

All our experiments build on the empirical and analytical framework from Garcia et al. (2023). Participants faced two types of options: experiential options (E-options) presented as eight abstract cues associated with different probability of winning points, and descriptive (or symbolic) options (S-options) symbolically depicting probabilities as pie-charts (Figure 1A). As illustrated in Figure 1B, the experiment unfolded in four parts. In the Learning (LE) phase, participants repeatedly chose between pairs of E-options and received information about both the obtained and the forgone outcome (complete feedback; +1/-1) on every trial, allowing them to learn the underlying gain probabilities associated with each cue.

Next, in the Test phase, participants made decisions without feedback, across 3 different modalities. In the Experiential-Symbolic (ES) modality, previously learned E-options were paired with S-options, creating hybrid choice contexts. In the Experiential-Experiential (EE) modality, choices were restricted to pairs of Eoptions. Finally, the experiment concludes with the Stated Probability (SP) modality, in which participants directly reported the gain probability of each E-option on a continuous probabilistic scale.

Our main aim was to assess whether E-option values were systematically less influential in choice when competing against S-options. To test this, we compared the subjective valuations of E-options inferred from the ES modality to those inferred in the EE and SP modalities.

For the choice-based modalities (ES and EE), we estimated the subjective value of each E-option from participants' choices. Specifically, we examined how often a given E-option was chosen against alternatives of varying value (either S-options in ES or other E-options in EE), and fitted these choice rates with logistic functions (Figure 1C). The midpoint (λ) of each fitted curve corresponds to the value of the alternative for which participants were equally likely to choose either option, that is, the indifference point. This indifference point served as an estimate of the participant's subjective valuation of the E-option. In the SP modality, subjective valuations were taken directly from participants' reported probabilities.

Because outcome magnitudes were fixed (+1/-1), differences in option value were entirely determined by gain probabilities. For all modalities, we therefore examined how subjective values of the E-options scaled with their true underlying probabilities by extracting the slope of a linear fit across options (Figure 1D). The resulting slope indexes probability sensitivity, which reflects how strongly experiential information influences decisions. Critically, smaller slope estimates in the ES modality relative to the EE modality indicate experien- tial neglect, that is, E-options are downweighted in hybrid choices. We additionally compared ES slopes to those obtained in the SP modality to test for the same trend.

This analysis was conducted across five experiments in which we manipulated four key features of the learning and representational structure of options (Figure 1E). Namely, we varied the learning architecture in Experiment 1, the test-phase feedback provided during hybrid choices in Experiment 2, the stimulus representation in Experiment 3, and the feedback representation in Experiment 4.

Neglect persists when training discourages pair-specific policies

Previous research suggests that experiential options may be learned in a value-free, policy-based manner (Bennett et al. 2021; Hayden et Niv 2021). In this case, choices are guided by a learned policy that selects the better option in a given context, without computing explicit intermediate values for each option (Vlaev et al. 2011). In contrast, symbolic options provide optionspecific value representation that can be directly compared across contexts. Because policy-based learning lacks these intermediate values, experiential options could be disadvantaged in hybrid choice, where individuals may rely on directly comparable symbolic values. If experiential neglect were driven by this policybased learning, promoting option-specific learning of experiential values should reduce or eliminate this effect. To test this, we manipulated the learning architecture in Experiment 1. We compared a baseline learning phase (LE), in which participants trained on fixed pairs of E-options (likely to prompt a policy-based, contextual, learning strategy), with a manipulated LE in which participants trained on all possible pairwise combinations of E-options (referred to as the non-fixed pairs condition; less likely to promote a policy-based learning strategy). Presenting all combinations prevents pairspecific learning policies, as each option must be evaluated across multiple relational contexts, promoting gen- eralized learning.

We verified that participants successfully learned to choose the best options, which was supported by choice accuracy being above chance levels (Figure S1 in Supplementary Materials). We then estimated E-option subjective values from participants' choices in the ES and EE modalities using logistic indifference points (Figure 2A-B), and quantified how these values scaled with objective probabilities using linear regression slopes (Figure 2C). We compared ES slopes with those from the EE and SP modalities to assess experiential neglect across conditions (Figure 2D).

To quantify experiential neglect, we applied Model M1, which compares ES and EE slope estimates across conditions using a linear mixed-effects framework (see Methods). The modality effect, which gives slope difference between ES and EE within the fixed-pairs LE condition, was significantly negative (β = -0.257, SE = 0.036, z = -7.206, p < 0.001, 95% CI [-0.326, -0.187]), confirming the underweighting of E-options in hybrid choices within the baseline learning condition. Critically, the ES-EE slope difference also remained significantly negative in the non-fixed pairs condition (β = -0.280, SE = 0.036, z = -7.872, p < 0.001, 95% CI [-0.350, -0.211]), confirming that experiential neglect persisted under nonfixed training, in which learning generalized across relational contexts. The magnitude of experiential neglect did not differ significantly between fixed and non-fixed pair learning structures (modality × learning condition interaction: β = -0.024, SE = 0.050, z = -0.471, p = 0.638, 95% CI [-0.122, 0.075]). Complete statistical results can be found in Supplementary Materials (Table S2A).

Next, we examined the SP modality, which provides a direct elicitation of stated probabilities of E-option values that does not rely on an inference from choice behavior. Because SP is performed at the end of the experiment, it also provides information about the retention and accessibility of learned experiential values. We found that SP slopes were significantly higher than ES ones, despite the SP elicitation task occurring after the ES one (see Table S3A in Supplementary Materials). This suggests that experiential neglect emerges despite experiential values remaining accessible after ES choice.

Altogether, these results indicate that shifting from a learning environment that allows policy-learning to one that discourages pair-specific policies, favoring optionvalue learning, does not eliminate the underweighting of experiential options in hybrid decisions.

Neglect persists when test-phase feedback can correct valuation distortions

Another possibility is that experiential neglect reflects inaccurate or distorted subjective representations of option values. Extensive literature shows that people distort probability distributions nonlinearly for both experiential and descriptive options, although differently (Prelec 1998; Kahneman et Tversky 1979), suggesting that subjective valuations may be imperfectly represented. Garcia and colleagues (Garcia et al. 2023) addressed this issue by enhancing experiential learning through a more extensive learning phase, but distortions may persist despite additional exposure. Critically, symbolic options are only introduced during the test phase, which prevents participants from refining their valuations beforehand. This is particularly problematic as description-based choice probabilities are known to be distorted, with small probabilities being overestimated and large ones underestimated (Tversky et Kahneman 1992). This could create asymmetries between experiential and symbolic options in hybrid choices, thereby contributing to experiential neglect. To test this account, Experiment 2 introduced outcome feedback during the test phase to attenuate potential representational distortion. We contrasted our original baseline condition ('without feedback') with a condition in which participants received outcome feedback (+1 or -1) after each decision in both ES and EE modalities (feedback condition). Crucially, this outcome feedback was complete (i.e. provided for both chosen and unchosen options) and provided for both E-and S-options, allowing not only further updating of experiential values but also correction of putatively distorted subjective representations of symbolic probabilities. If experiential neglect were driven by biased valuation of symbolic options, providing corrective feedback should reduce or eliminate the asymmetry.

As in Experiment 1, participants in both conditions demonstrated successful learning during the initial phase, with choice accuracy exceeding chance levels (Figure S1 in Supplementary Materials). We replicated our analytical strategy aimed at estimating valuescaling slopes between modalities (Figure 3A). Using Model M1, we again observed a significant negative modality effect (β = -0.307, SE = 0.040, z = -7.625, p < 0.001, 95% CI [-0.386, -0.228]), confirming experiential neglect in the no-feedback condition. Critically, the ES-EE slope difference remained significantly negative in the feedback condition (β = -0.269, SE = 0.040, z = -6.680, p < 0.001, 95% CI [-0.348, -0.190]), indicating that experiential options continued to be underweighted even when complete outcome feedback was provided during hybrid choices. Experiential neglect did not vary significantly across feedback conditions (modality × feedback condition interaction: β = 0.038, SE = 0.057, z = 0.668, p = 0.504, 95% CI [-0.074, 0.150]). See Table S2B in the Supplementary Materials for complete results. A similar pattern was found when comparing the ES and the SP slopes (see Table S3B in Supplementary Materials).

These findings suggest that experiential neglect is not explained by distortions that test-phase feedback could correct.

Neglect persists when the salience of symbolic information is reduced

A further hypothesis is that symbolic options may dominate choice simply because they are more salient. Symbolic options explicitly convey probability distributions in a visually clear and aesthetically appealing format, which may preferentially capture attention and shape the allocation of cognitive resources during decisionmaking (Yantis et Egeth 1999; Desimone et Duncan 1995; Treisman et Gelade 1980). Contemporary models of choice, including attentional drift-diffusion and related evidence-accumulation frameworks, posit that attention dynamically modulates the weight of information in value construction, such that attended options exert a disproportionate influence on evidence accumulation and choice (Krajbich et al. 2010; Smith et Krajbich 2019; Zilker et Pachur 2022a , 2022b , 2023). Under this view, symbolic representations may bias the decision process by receiving greater attentional weighting, thereby amplifying their contribution to the decision variable, while experiential information exerts a weaker influence. In Experiment 3, we therefore changed the visual representation of S-option lotteries to reduce their visual complexity (and consequently their saliency). We tested two new representation conditions: one where explicit probability information was removed from the original colored pie-charts (called pie-charts), and another where S-options were presented as textual descriptions of probabilities without visual cues (denoted numerical). These manipulations are intended to reduce, respectively, the encoding precision and attentional salience of the probabilistic information of S-options, which in turn should increase the weight of experiential options in choice.

As in previous experiments, participants correctly learned E-option values (Figure S1 in Supplementary Materials). We then estimated the E-value scaling slope across modalities (Figure 3B).

Using Model M1, we again observed a significant negative modality effect in the numerical condition (β = -0.202, SE = 0.062, z = -3.255, p < 0.01, 95% CI [-0.323, -0.080]), indicating experiential neglect. Critically, the ES-EE slope difference remained significant in the piechart condition (β = -0.133, SE = 0.062, z = -2.154, p < 0.05, 95% CI [-0.255, -0.012]), confirming that experiential neglect persisted despite reduced perceptual clarity of symbolic options. The magnitude of experiential neglect did not significantly vary between the visual formats of S-options (modality × symbolic representation interaction: β = 0.068, SE = 0.088, z = 0.779, p = 0.436, 95% CI [-0.103, 0.240]). Complete results are provided in Table S2C of the Supplementary Materials. The same effect was observed for the ES-SP comparison (see Table S3C in Supplementary Materials).

Thus, deteriorating the precision or the salience of the probabilistic information of S-options did not eliminate the underweighting of experienced values.

Neglect persists when experiential and symbolic outcome formats are aligned

Finally, experiential and symbolic options differ not only in how their values are acquired (learned vs described), but also in the very nature of the representation through which those values are encoded. Experiential options are learned through sequences of trial-bytrial individual outcomes, from which participants can internally construct an average or probability estimate (Stewart et al. 2006). In contrast, symbolic options are represented as explicit lotteries, directly encoding full probability distributions in a structured format. Such representational asymmetry has been suggested as a partial explanation of the description-experience gap (Hertwig et Erev 2009) and may create a format incompatibility at decision time when the two kinds of options are compared directly. In hybrid choices, experiential values may need to be translated from an internally constructed summary into a lottery-like format in order to be compared with symbolic options. This translation process may impose a cognitive cost rendering experiential information less accessible, and thereby reducing its influence on choice, which we tested in experiments 4A and 4B. We compared our baseline condition in which trial-level E-option outcomes were presented as numerical values (reward condition), with a new condition in which E-outcomes were instead displayed as pie charts (lottery condition, Soukupova, Garcia, et Palminteri 2026). The lottery condition aligns the representational format of experiential feedback with the S-option lottery format, which allows the two options to be encoded using a shared representation. Note that the lottery condition doesn't remove the learning component as participants still need to associate these probabilities to abstract cues. If experiential neglect arises from a translation cost, this manipulation should reduce or eliminate its effect. To test this across different learning architectures (see Experiment 1), we designed an Experiment 4A which implements fixed pairs during the learning phase, and an Experiment 4B which implements non-fixed pairs. Once again, participants correctly learned E-option values in the LE phase, and to a same degree in experiments 4A and 4B (Figure S1 in Supplementary Materials). We evaluated the value-scaling slopes for both experiments (Figure 3C and 3D respectively for Exp 4A and 4B).

We then applied Model M1 separately to Experiments 4A and 4B to assess experiential neglect (see Methods).

In both experiments, the ES-EE slope difference within the reward condition was significantly negative (4A: β = -0.209, SE = 0.045, z = -4.668, p < 0.001, 95% CI [-0.296, -0.121]; 4B: β = -0.198, SE = 0.058, z = -3.442, p < 0.001, 95% CI [-0.311, -0.085]), indicating reduced weighting of experiential values in hybrid choices. The ES-EE slope difference remained significantly negative in the lottery condition in both experiments, as confirmed by the linear contrast (4A: β = -0.217, SE = 0.045, z = -4.857, p < 0.001, 95% CI [-0.305, -0.129]; 4B: β = -0.175, SE = 0.058, z = -3.040, p < 0.01, 95% CI [-0.288, -0.062]), indicating robust experiential neglect even when outcome representations were aligned across modalities. Replacing reward-based feedback with lottery-based feedback did not significantly modulate the magnitude of experiential neglect in either experiment (4A: β = -0.008, SE = 0.063, z = -0.134, p = 0.894, 95% CI [-0.132, 0.115]; 4B: β = 0.023, SE = 0.081, z = 0.284, p = 0.777, 95% CI [-0.136, 0.183]). Thus, aligning the representation format of experiential and symbolic options did not eliminate the underweighting of experiential values. See Tables S2D and S2E in the Supplementary Materials for full results. Comparable results emerged when comparing the ES and the SP slopes (see Tables S3D and S3E in Supplementary Materials).

Experiential Neglect across Experiments

Across our five experiments, we reported highly consistent patterns of evidence supporting experiential neglect. To further check the robustness of the phenomenon beyond single experiment-level, we performed random-effects meta-analyses on conditionlevel estimates (see Methods). The meta-analytical estimate of the difference between ES and EE scaling slopes confirms a highly robust and consistent negative effect across experiments (Figure 4, left; β = -0.239, SE = 0.016, z = -5.326 p < 0.001, 95% CI [-0.275, -0.204]).

Crucially, a comparable meta-analytic pattern emerged when comparing the ES value-scaling slopes to the SP ones (Figure 4, right; β = -0.371, SE = 0.021, z = -5.528, p < 0.001, 95% CI [-0.420, -0.323]). Importantly, a paired comparison of the two slope-difference metrics, across the 10 experimental conditions, revealed that experiential neglect was significantly larger in the ES-SP slope difference than in the ES-EE one (paired t(9) = 6.586, p < 0.001). See Supplementary Materials (Tables S4) for full results. This is expected as choice-free elicitation in the SP modality produces more precise probability estimates than choice-based elicitation (the latter incurring additional noise) thereby accentuating the slope differences.

Overall, these results confirm that experiential neglect is a robust and general phenomenon across experiments and elicitation methods.

Slope estimates in valence domains

To test for this, we fitted linear regression separately within each valence domain instead of over all probabilities (Figure 5A), for each experiment. Discussion

Making sense of experiential neglect with retrieval-based uncertainty

Beyond demonstrating the robustness of this phenomenon, the central focus of our study is to understand what causes experiential neglect. One possibility was that it arises due to representational differences between experiential and descriptive values. To address this, we systematically tested four major classes of explanations across five experiments. Our findings suggest that experiential neglect is not explained by policybased learning (Experiment 1), distorted valuation of symbolic options (Experiment 2), the greater perceptual salience of symbolic options (Experiment 3) or incompatible outcome formats between experiential and symbolic options (Experiment 4A and 4B).

With these alternatives unable to account for neglect, we suggest that experiential neglect arises from another intrinsic property of experiential options. One remaining distinguishing feature is that experiential values must be retrieved from memory at the time of choice, whereas symbolic option values are immediately available. As retrieval from memory is a limited process that can constrain decision making (Giguère et Love 2013), experiential neglect might arise at that stage.

A first possibility is that experiential neglect reflects a retrieval cost: participants may disregard experiential options because retrieving their values incurs a cognitive cost (Bordalo et al. 2020; Dukas 1999), making explicitly described symbolic options more attractive to rely on. This account predicts that experiential neglect should primarily arise when retrieval itself can be avoided, like in hybrid choices. However, the ES and EE trials were interleaved, with their order randomized while holding the experiential option constant. Thus, the value of an experiential option could be retrieved during the EE choices (as suggested by high accuracy in those trials) and maintained in working memory for the subsequent ES choices with no additional retrieval cost. Yet experiential neglect still emerges, suggesting that avoidance of retrieval per se is unlikely to be the sole driver of the effect.

We instead propose that experiential neglect arises from subjective uncertainty associated with the retrieval of experiential values. Crucially, this uncertainty does not imply that the value is incompletely learned or in-accurately stored. Rather, even when experiential values are correctly learned, they may be associated with reduced confidence at retrieval. Participants may therefore discount experiential information because they are less confident in the reliability or precision of retrieved value estimates. In hybrid decision-making contexts, such uncertainty can lead to a down-weighting of experiential information (Lee et Hare 2023), especially when it competes with symbolic options whose attributes are explicitly displayed and therefore perceived as more certain. Importantly, this does not imply that symbolic valuation is objectively noise-free. Constructing symbolic values can also involve substantial cognitive uncertainty and computational noise (Woodford 2012; Enke et Graeber 2023; Oprea 2024). However, the immediate external availability of symbolic attributes may nevertheless increase subjective confidence in their reliability relative to experiential options, even if both formats are objectively noisy.

Several aspects of our results help characterize the role of retrieval-based uncertainty in experiential value neglect. First, experiential value neglect does not stem from poor memory quality or noisy value representations. If it did, participants should perform poorly when relying solely on experiential values. Instead, experiential options are evaluated relatively accurately in EE and SP modalities. Second, because experiential values may be associated with subjective uncertainty at retrieval and thus be treated as ambiguous, participants may be ambiguity averse and avoid them (Fox et Tversky 1995). However, experiential neglect cannot be reduced to a general aversion to ambiguity. Our results do not indicate that experiential options are chosen less overall as they would if participants were ambiguity averse. Rather, their values are less influential in hybrid choices, leading to decisions that are suboptimal given the experiential value: overselection of experiential options when the symbolic option has a negative expected value, and underselection when it has a positive expected value.

We therefore propose that experiential neglect reflects underconfidence in experiential value estimates rather than imprecision or ambiguity per se. In our task, it is difficult to fully dissociate underconfidence arising during learning from underconfidence arising during retrieval.

Based on the interpretations developed above, we propose that experiential neglect is a pervasive feature of hybrid choices in which experiential values must be retrieved from memory at the time of decision. Under this view, neglect should emerge specifically when experiential options compete with symbolic options whose values are immediately available. Empirical investigations of such hybrid choice settings remain relatively scarce. One notable exception is a recent study by Tiede, Gaissmaier, et Pachur (2025), which did not report systematic neglect of experiential values in hybrid choices. At first glance, this result may appear to con-flict with our findings. However, a closer examination of their experimental design reveals an important methodological difference in how experiential values are constructed. In their task, participants choose between a symbolic option and an experiential option whose value is learned within the same trial through outcome sampling. Specifically, before making their choice, participants can freely sample outcomes from the experiential option as many times as they wish, directly observing its payoff distribution. Only after this free sampling phase do they decide between the experiential and the symbolic option. As a result, experiential values are acquired and can be immediately used for choice, substantially minimizing the uncertainty associated with memory retrieval. The contrast between their findings and ours does not contradict the existence of experiential neglect, and it is consistent with retrieval-based uncertainty contributing to it.

Having demonstrated the robustness of experiential neglect in this lab experiment, a crucial question remains of whether this phenomenon generalizes in real world scenarios. In our controlled experimental design, participants can trust the objective and reliable nature of the descriptive information. However, in real-life settings, descriptive information is rarely as precise or controlled (Hertwig et Erev 2009). Some descriptive information like real-life gambling lotteries or medication side-effects statistics mirror our experiment's controlled information, but others introduce social and contextual uncertainty. For example, restaurant ratings rely on others' subjective experiences, and technical metrics such as nutrition scores are often based on expert assessments. If experiential neglect is driven by a general aversion to perceived unreliable information, will the opposite pattern emerge in naturalistic contexts including other sources of uncertainty? Will the distrust of socially derived descriptive information outweigh retrieval-based uncertainty for experiential information? Future work should assess whether experiential neglect persists in more ecological settings where descriptive information has some level of social construction (e.g. "The previous 500 participants reported that this lottery has an 80% chance of giving a point and a 20% chance of losing a point.").

Common currency under computational constraints

A long-standing view, known as the common currency hypothesis, proposes that option values converge within a central valuation system (Montague et Berns 2002; Rangel et Hare 2010; Rangel, Camerer, et Montague 2008), supported by neuroimaging evidence of overlapping value representations in ventromedial prefrontal cortex and ventral striatum (Bartra, McGuire, et Kable 2013; Clithero et Rangel 2014; Fouragnan, Retzler, et Philiastides 2018; Kable et Glimcher 2007). A strong common currency interpretation holds that all values, whether learned through experience or supplied descriptively, are projected onto a single internal scale enabling direct comparison across option types (Levy et Glimcher 2012; Kable et Glimcher 2009). A weaker interpretation is that hybrid choices rely on crossrepresentational alignment or partial normalization rather than a fully shared currency (Hayden et Niv 2021; Vlaev et al. 2011; Walasek et Brown 2024).

However, the persistence of experiential value neglect does not necessarily imply that experiential and symbolic values lack a common representational format. Rather, it suggests that values originating from different informational systems may enter the decision process with different levels of subjective reliability, accessibility, or computational priority. The core issue, then, is not whether values can be compared, but how the decision system integrates heterogeneous value representations under cognitive and computational constraints. Attentional allocation (Roe, Busemeyer, et Townsend 2001; Busemeyer et al. 2019), uncertainty (Daw, Niv, et Dayan 2005; Lee, Shimojo, et O'Doherty 2014), and resource demands (Kool, Gershman, et Cushman 2017; Shenhav, Botvinick, et Cohen 2013) may trigger heuristic or priority-based mechanisms rather than fully symmetric integration.

Under this interpretation, a shared representational scale may be sufficient for within-format comparison while remaining insufficient to guarantee symmetric integration across heterogeneous informational systems. The present findings therefore suggest that hybrid choices involve not only option-level valuation, but also meta-level arbitration (Sripada 2026; Lieder et al. 2018) among systems differing in retrieval demands, subjective reliability, and computational accessibility. In such frameworks, decision-making may not only depend on the estimated value of available options, but also on the inferred reliability of the systems generating those values. In this sense, experiential and descriptive information may be encoded in partially comparable formats, while their influence on choice remains asymmetrically weighted according to the accessibility and subjective reliability of the underlying representations at the time of decision.

Valence effect on experiential valuation

In addition to our general experiential value neglect findings, our results also reveal two distinct but complementary effects of valence on experiential valuation. First, experiential neglect was observed in both gain and loss domains. Although this effect was more robust at the meta-analytic level than when examined separately within individual experimental conditions, its presence across domains indicates that experiential neglect is not specific to either gains or losses. Instead, the tendency to underweight experiential values in hybrid choices appears to be a general feature of experiential decisionmaking, seemingly independent of outcome valence. Second, we observed a systematic asymmetry between gain and loss domains. Across all modalities, experiential information associated on average with losses was systematically less influential than equivalent gainrelated information.

At first glance, this finding appears to run counter to the intuition behind Prospect Theory, which posits that "losses loom larger than gains" and are therefore weighted more strongly in decision-making (Kahneman et Tversky 1984 , 1979). However, it is important to note that loss aversion primarily concerns asymmetries in the subjective magnitude of outcomes, rather than sensitivity to probability information. In our task, outcome magnitudes were fixed (+1 or -1) such that only probabilities varied. The observed effect therefore can be better conceptualized as a form of loss probability underweighting, rather than reduced aversion to losses per se.

This asymmetry aligns with broader evidence for a positivity bias in learning and decision-making (Palminteri et Lebreton 2022; Sharot et Garrett 2016; Eil et Rao 2011), whereby positive information is integrated more efficiently than negative information. Nonetheless, an important limitation of our design is that gains and losses were manipulated only through their probabilities, not their magnitudes. As a result, we cannot determine whether the observed loss underweighting arises from distortions in probability weighting, differences in subjective outcome valuation, or an interaction between the two. Disentangling these mechanisms will require future work that independently manipulates both outcome magnitudes and probabilities across valence domains.

Methods

Experimental Design

This study builds on the methodology of Garcia et al. (2023) across five experiments, each introducing a targeted manipulation. Across all experiments, participants encountered two types of options: experiential options (E-options) represented as eight abstract cues with probabilistic outcomes or symbolic options (S-options) presented as eleven pie charts explicitly depicting gain probabilities. The gain probabilities associated with Eoptions were 0.1, 0.2, 0.3, 0.4, 0.6, 0.7, 0.8 and 0.9 whilst the S-options additionally had 0, 0.5 and 1. The additional symbolic probabilities were included to fully span the probability space and facilitate estimation of choice functions. In both types of options, displayed in Figure 1A, gains (+1 point) and losses (-1 point) occur according to these associated probabilities.

As shown in Figure 1B, the first phase, named Learning (LE) phase, presented participants with pairs of E-options. On each trial, participants selected one Eoption and received complete feedback (+1 or -1) allowing them to gradually infer the underlying probability value attached. All possible pairwise combinations of options were presented twice, resulting in a total of 112 trials. The presentation order followed a structured block design: within each block, one E-option is held constant while the second E-option varies. Once all combinations within a block were shown, the task proceeded to the next block, where a different option is held constant. This block structure ensured repeated exposure while preserving randomized presentation and minimizing sequential dependencies. The order of blocks and the order of trials within each block were randomized, furthermore, the left/right display of options were also randomized.

Following learning, participants completed a test phase without feedback, comprising three modalities. In the Experiential-Symbolic (ES) modality, participants chose between E-options learned in the LE phase and S-options, creating hybrid decision contexts. In the Experiential-Experiential (EE) modality, choices were restricted to pairs of E-options. All possible pairwise combinations were presented only once. ES and EE trials were interleaved within the same block structure, with one E-option held constant per block while the other varied across trials. Block order, trial order, and left/right positioning were randomized. In total, participants completed 88 ES trials and 56 EE trials.

Finally, the experiment concluded with the Stated Probability (SP) modality, which employed a different elicitation method. In this modality, participants were asked to estimate the gain probability associated with each E-option by answering the question: "What are the odds this symbol gives a +1?". Responses were provided using a continuous slider ranging from 0% to 100% in 5% increments. The initial position of the slider was randomized across trials (discretely among 25, 30, 35, 40, 45, 50, 55, 60, 65, and 70) to reduce anchoring effects. The SP modality comprised 8 trials, with each E-option presented once in a randomized order.

Analysis method

To check for experiential neglect, we compared the subjective value of E-options in the ES modality to the one in EE modality. We additionally compared the ES E-option valuation to the one from the SP modality.

Finding subjective valuations.

We first inferred the subjective value of the different E-options. To do so, we studied the probability of choosing a specific E-option over an S-option (for ES modality) or over another E-option (for EE modality). As portrayed in Figure 1C, we fitted logistic functions (see Equation 1) to the choice rate regarding that E-option for each participant.

Equation 1: -λ) The logistic function included two free parameters: the sensitivity β parameter and the λ midpoint. These parameters were estimated individually for each participant using maximum likelihood estimation (MLE) estimation by minimizing the negative logposterior probability using the L-BFGS-B algorithm. This was done with Python's (v3.12.15) minimize function (method: "L-BFGS-B", maximum iterations: 10,000), from scipy.optimize module (scipy v1.18.1). Initial parameter values were set to 1 (β) and 0.5 (λ), with constraints of β ∈ [0.01, 30] and λ ∈ (0, 1). We also tested maximum a posteriori (MAP) estimation with a weak Gamma prior on β (shape α = 2, scale θ = 3), but this did not substantially improve parameter recovery. Thus, we retained the MLE approach. Parameter recovery results for both methods are provided in Supplementary Materials (Figures S13) and fitting scripts can be found on https://github.com/caropio/RandC_Followup_Analysis.

The fitted midpoint of the logistic curve served as the indifference point of that E-option, the point at which a participant is equally likely to choose the E-option or its alternative. This midpoint thus reflects the subjective valuation of that E-option for that specific participant. We estimated these indifference points for all E-options in both the ES and EE modalities. For the SP modality, the subjective values are taken directly from the participants' stated probabilities.

Comparing subjective valuations to infer choice weighting.

We then examined the inferred subjective values of E-options as a function of their objective underlying probabilities. We fitted a linear function across E-options and extracted the slope (see Figure 1D). This slope provides a measure of probability sensitivity, indexing the degree to which differences in objective probabilities are translated into differences in subjective value. Because subjective values are inferred from choice behavior, the slope serves as an indirect behavioral proxy for the effective weighting of experiential probability information in decision-making. Under standard revealed-preference assumptions (McFadden 1974; Train 2003), the more weight a variable carries in option values, the more choices change as the variable changes. Thus, information that plays a larger role in choice should give rise to systematic choice differences as it varies. Accordingly, a slope close to zero indicates low probability sensitivity, such that differences in experiential probabilities carry little informative value for choice, reflecting weak weighting of experiential information. Conversely, a slope close to one indicates that experiential probability information is translated into highly sensitive subjective valuation which in turn can meaningfully inform choice. Crucially, we compared the slope estimates of the ES and EE modalities within each participant to test whether the same experiential information is used differently depending on context. A smaller slope estimate in the ES modality relative to the EE one indicates experiential neglect, reflecting a reduced weighting of experiential values in choices where they compete with symbolic options. We additionally compared ES slopes to those obtained in the SP modality to test for the same pattern.

Statistical tests

All statistical analyses used an alpha level of 0.05 for statistical tests and were conducted using Python v3.12.15.

Linear mixed-effects models (LMMs) were fitted using the mixedlm function from the statsmodels.formula.api module (statsmodels v0.15.0). Fixed-effect significance was assessed using z-statistics (approximate Wald tests). When testing effects were not directly estimated by the model, linear contrasts were constructed by specifying contrast vectors over the fixed-effect coefficients (including main effects and interaction terms) and evaluated using the model's .t_test() method, which computes Wald tests of linear hypotheses over the fixed-effect coefficients.

To derive meta-analytical estimates, we conducted random-effects meta-analyses using experimentand condition-level regression results (estimate and variance). Random-effects models were chosen to account for expected heterogeneity across experiments and conditions. Between-experiment variance was estimated using restricted maximum likelihood (REML). Meta-analyses were implemented using the meta_regression() function (method = "reml") of the pymare package (v0.0.13).

Some statistical results reported in figures were obtained using paired t-tests, with data grouped either by participant or by experimental condition, depending on the analysis. These tests were performed using the ttest_rel function from the scipy.stats module (scipy v1.18.1).

All statistical results are reported using: estimates (β), standard errors (SE), z-statistics (z) for LMMs and metaanalytic aggregations, Student's t-statistics t and degrees of freedom df (t(df)) for paired comparisons, p-values (p), 95% confidence intervals (CI).

All statistical analysis scripts can be found on https://github.com/caropio/RandC_Followup_Analysis.

The Valence coefficient β2 captures the difference in slope estimates between the loss and gain domains within the session0 condition, indicating the relative probability sensitivity for losses compared with gains, in that condition. The interaction term captures whether the loss-gain slope difference is larger or smaller in the session1 condition compared to the ses-sion0 one. The sum of the valence and interaction coefficients captures the slope difference between loss and gain within the session1 condition (which was tested using constructed linear contrasts), and thus a negative co-efficient would indicate lower probability sensitivity for losses compared with gains in that condition.

Session manipulation

• Exp 1: session0 corresponds to 'fixed pairs' condition, and session1 to 'non-fixed pairs' condition • Exp 2: session0 corresponds to 'without feedback' condition, and session1 to 'with feedback' condition • Exp 3: session0 corresponds to 'numerical lottery' condition, and session1 to 'pie-chart lottery' condition • Exp 4A: session0 corresponds to 'reward outcome' condition, and session1 to 'lottery outcome' condition • Exp 4B: session0 corresponds to 'reward outcome' condition, and session1 to 'lottery outcome' condition
