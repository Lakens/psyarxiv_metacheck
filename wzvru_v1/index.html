Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

Metacheck does not yet analyze automatically whether sentences which include non-significant p-values are correct, but we recommend manually checking the sentences below for possible misinterpreted non-significant p values.

Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard), also Internal Review Board - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term); not IEC followed by a number, which is a technical standard - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee/Commission, in the singular or plural - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee, Bioethics Committee/Commission, Ethikkommission - Ethical Review Authority/Unit/Office/Agency - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Human Research Protection Office/Program, Office for Human Subjects Protection - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee(s) on/for (Medical and Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt - Ethics review that was not needed: Ethics review was not required, did not require further ethical review, exempt from formal human research ethics review, no new institutional ethics review

A template line the authors have not filled in (“[Add ethics approval … here.]”) is not a statement.

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

When a statement is expected

The module sorts each paper by where its data came from (the internal .data_source(); the signs and how the rule was tested are described in R/data-source.R): - own: the authors collected data from people or animals, for this paper, in an earlier study of their own, or (a protocol) will collect them; - existing_records: records about individual people that the authors obtained for this study, such as hospital records or a cohort; - existing_unclear: existing data about people from a source the module could not identify; - open_resource: an open resource any researcher can use, such as UK Biobank or NHANES; - none: no data about people or animals (a review, simulation, commentary, theory, tutorial, coding of published papers, or figures about groups); - uncertain: the signs were missing or pointed both ways; - no_text: the paper has no text.

A statement is expected for own, existing_records and existing_unclear. A paper without a statement in these classes, or in uncertain, is flagged. The module reads the text, and the results of power, stat_p_exact, stat_check, repo_check, data_check and prereg_check when they ran before it (run it after them); without stat_p_exact, it finds the p-values in the text.

On 199 PsyArXiv and medRxiv preprints labelled after the rule was fixed, the module decided right whether a statement is needed for 86.9% (the earlier live-data search: 83.4%). Its main errors: other people’s studies described in the words of a new study, records and specimens described only as counts, and open resources the paper does not call open.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

Overall, among all instances flagged as power statements, 90.6% were correct (positive predictive value).

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download information from other preregistration sites, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 685 tests with 34 instances of inconsistent reporting, Statcheck correctly detected 34 of them, and incorrectly identified 26. Therefore 0% of true instances were missed, and 43% of detections were false positives. See https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/ for more details of the validation.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on 226 Psychological Science papers with 5,019 hand-coded p-values, 438 of them imprecise (scienceverse/validation, branch stat_p_exact). Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). Of the misses, 13 are starred table notes (“*p < .05”) that the module leaves out on purpose and 11 are p-values it did not find; of the wrong reports, 20 are p-values the ground truth does not contain (such as “ps < .05”) and 6 use ≤ or ≥. These figures measure agreement with the hand-coding, which has known errors in both directions (scienceverse/validation#48).

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. A TRO uses them for each trov:TransparentResearchPerformance. * run_results$exit_status: the exit status of the process that ran the script (0 = ended normally); NA when the process did not report one (a time limit, or Docker failing to start the container). * artifacts: one row per file, with stage (deposit: as downloaded from the repository, hashed from the downloaded file; before_run: the run folder just before the first script ran, after metacheck’s own changes, such as rewritten paths and unpacked archives; after_run: only the files the scripts made (status new), changed (changed) or removed (removed), as an unchanged file is the one listed before the run), path (relative to the run folder, as valid UTF-8, a byte of another encoding written as <xx>), size (bytes), sha256, mime_type (by extension, NA when unknown), source (the file’s address in the repository, for deposit). The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). Files metacheck itself copied or made between passes (see modifications, output_copied, file_placed) show in after_run too. A TRO uses the rows as trov:ResearchArtifacts and each stage as a trov:ArtifactArrangement. * modifications$sha256_before, modifications$sha256_after: the hash of the changed script as downloaded and as it ran; NA when the script is not at the same path in both (a script made from an R Markdown file). * environment: the system that ran the code (trov:TransparentResearchSystem in a TRO): metacheck_version, started_at, ended_at, host (R version, platform, operating system), sandbox, network_isolated (TRUE only when every Docker run had --network none; the process sandbox does not stop the network), internet, timeout_per_script, total_timeout, python_local (the Python run outside Docker and its version), and docker: runs (for each kind of run, r_run, r_install, python_run, python_install, the image and the docker run arguments, such as --network none, --read-only, --cap-drop ALL, no-new-privileges, the process limit and the memory and processor limits) and images (for each image, its ID and digests from docker image inspect, its creation time and the R and Python versions its environment declares).

It is NA for files run inside another script, files that are not an analysis, files not read, and a Docker failure. summary_table counts each group (repro_ran_n, repro_code_error_n, repro_dependency_n, repro_time_n, repro_not_checkable_n), and the report’s first sentence gives them. The traffic light reflects the static readiness signals by default; a script in code_error that stopped with an error makes it red, as a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure, and a script in one of the other three groups keeps it from being green. Calls that need a screen, which the run does not have (dev.print(), dev.copy2pdf(), dev.copy2eps(), savePlot(), windows(), win.graph(), x11(), quartz(), View()), are replaced before the run by calls that need none, which modifications records as screen_call_replaced. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

Background. Regular exercise confers benefits for motor symptoms and quality of life in Parkinson's disease; however, long-term adherence remains a substantial challenge. Ever-Steady is a web-based application, co-produced with people with Parkinson's, that combines personalised goal setting, conversational activity logging with an artificial intelligence (AI) coaching companion ("Mr Steady"), weekly progress summaries, and curated educational and follow-along exercise content.

Objective. To evaluate the feasibility and acceptability of Ever-Steady and to explore preliminary changes in exercise motivation and Parkinson's-specific quality of life.

Methods. A single-arm, pre-post, mixed-methods feasibility study was conducted. Adults with Parkinson's disease were recruited through a community-based therapy centre in Kent, United Kingdom, and were asked to use the application for eight weeks. The Behavioural Regulation in Exercise Questionnaire (BREQ-3) and the 39-item Parkinson's Disease Questionnaire (PDQ-39) were administered at baseline and follow-up. Acceptability was assessed using a post-intervention evaluation questionnaire and semi-structured interviews, which were analysed using reflexive thematic analysis.

Results. Nine participants were enrolled; one withdrew before using the application, and all eight who commenced the intervention completed follow-up (100% retention; mean age 69.9 years). Seven participants reported using the application on at least four days per week, seven agreed that it integrated readily into daily life, six would recommend it to another person with Parkinson's, and five reported an increase in exercise, with none reporting a decrease. Intrinsic motivation for exercise increased by 0.41 points on a 0 to 4 scale (95% CI 0.09 to 0.72; dz = 1.08), whereas controlled forms of motivation were unchanged. The PDQ-39 Summary Index improved by 1.5 points in the full sample (non-significant) and by 5.2 points (p = .004) in a sensitivity analysis excluding one participant whose follow-up responses were inconsistent with their concurrent evaluation and interview data. Five themes were generated: making activity visible; from intention to action; a companion that listens; recognising effort; and fitting around life with Parkinson's.

Ever-Steady was feasible to deliver and acceptable to the majority of participants, and its use was associated with a large, exploratory increase in intrinsic motivation for exercise. Participants identified specific refinements, namely effort-sensitive feedback, persistent goal visibility, reliable voice interaction, wearable integration and peer connection, which will inform the next development cycle and support progression to a larger controlled evaluation.

1. Introduction

Exercise is among the best-supported non-pharmacological interventions for Parkinson's disease. A Cochrane network meta-analysis of 154 randomised controlled trials reported beneficial effects on motor severity and quality of life for most forms of exercise relative to no exercise, with little evidence of differences between exercise modalities [1]. The principal clinical challenge, therefore, lies less in the selection of an exercise prescription than in supporting people to sustain it over time.

Long-term engagement with exercise is difficult to achieve in this population. People with Parkinson's report low outcome expectations, lack of time and fear of falling as barriers to exercise [2], and the evidence base on adherence is itself limited: a 2024 review of 45 controlled studies of non-pharmacological interventions found that fewer than one quarter reported high-quality adherence data aligned with intervention goals [3]. In a United Kingdom survey of 405 people with Parkinson's, 54% reported having received no exercise intervention since diagnosis, yet 90% expressed willingness to participate in exercise education, with online video among the preferred formats [4]. These findings indicate a marked disparity between demand for exercise support and its availability.

Digital technologies offer a means of extending support beyond the clinic, although the design of that support appears to be critical. A large, multifaceted behavioural programme to promote physical activity in Parkinson's disease did not improve its primary outcome [5], whereas homebased aerobic exercise supported by a motivational application and remote supervision produced clinically meaningful motor benefit [6]. The addition of a mobile health application to an exercise programme appeared to confer greatest benefit on sedentary participants [7], and an animated virtual exercise coach achieved complete retention and high satisfaction over one month [8]. Conversational agents represent a growing but still immature area of healthcare research, with few controlled evaluations of efficacy or safety [9]. Large language models now permit open, natural dialogue with such agents. This creates an opportunity to deliver personalised, reflective coaching at scale, together with an obligation to demonstrate that such coaching is acceptable and safe for older adults living with a long-term neurological condition.

Self-determination theory provides a framework for specifying what such coaching should target. The theory distinguishes autonomous motivation, in which a behaviour is undertaken because it is enjoyable, personally valued or integrated with one's identity, from controlled motivation, in which it is undertaken in response to guilt or external pressure [10]. Autonomous motivation, and intrinsic motivation in particular, is consistently associated with sustained participation in exercise [11]. An intervention that strengthens enjoyment and personal value, rather than adding pressure, should therefore be better placed to support long-term adherence.

Ever-Steady was developed at the Parkinson's Centre for Integrated Therapy (PCIT), Kent, to apply these principles, and was co-produced with people with Parkinson's from the outset of its development. The application pairs personal goal setting with an AI coaching companion, Mr Steady, with which users speak or type in order to log activity, reflect on it and receive encouragement. The present paper reports the first feasibility study of Ever-Steady. The study aimed to assess (1) feasibility, with respect to recruitment, retention, engagement and data completeness; (2) acceptability, from the perspective of people with Parkinson's; and (3) preliminary change in exercise motivation and Parkinson's-specific quality of life, in order to inform the design of a larger trial.

2. Methods

>Methods

2.1 Design and setting

This was a single-arm, pre-post, mixed-methods feasibility study, reported with reference to the CONSORT extension for pilot and feasibility trials where applicable to an uncontrolled design [12]. The study was conducted at PCIT, a community-based therapy centre for people with Parkinson's in Kent, United Kingdom.

2.2 Ethical approval

The study received approval from the University of Kent School of Psychology Research Ethics Committee (ID 2026177426566910482). All participants provided written informed consent. Data were pseudonymised at the point of collection, and voice input was transcribed in real time without retention of audio recordings.

2.3 Participants

Eligible participants had a confirmed diagnosis of Parkinson's disease, were aged 18 years or over, had access to a smartphone, tablet or computer, were able to provide informed consent, and were medically suitable for light-to-moderate exercise. Suitability was determined using a structured health readiness screen addressing cardiovascular, Parkinson's-specific and musculoskeletal risk. Individuals with severe combined auditory and visual impairment that would preclude use of the voice interface were excluded. Participants were recruited consecutively from individuals attending PCIT. Consistent with the feasibility aims of the study, no formal sample size calculation was undertaken; the target was a small cohort sufficient to estimate retention and acceptability and to provide adequate information power for the qualitative analysis [13].

2.4 Intervention

Ever-Steady is a browser-based application optimised for smartphones and tablets, with large text, high-contrast colours and simplified navigation. During an in-person baseline session, each participant completed an onboarding process (current activity level, motor difficulties, a personal exercise goal expressed in their own words, anticipated barriers, motivators and preferences), established an initial SMART (specific, measurable, achievable, relevant and time-bound) goal with a researcher, and received training in use of the application. Participants were asked to use the application on approximately five days per week for eight weeks. The principal components were as follows: • Conversational logging.

Participants described their activity to Mr Steady by voice or text.

The system extracted activity type, duration and perceived effort, and then posed a brief, personalised reflective question.

• Adaptive encouragement. Rule-based monitoring of recent logs adjusted the content of messages; for example, a lighter session was suggested after several consecutive days of high-intensity activity, a smaller goal when self-rated confidence declined, and an alternative activity when enjoyment was repeatedly low.

• Feedback. Progress charts, a count of consecutive active days, a points-based growth metaphor (progressing from seedling to tree) and an automatically generated weekly narrative summary.

• Library. Educational articles on exercise and non-drug therapies, together with categorised follow-along exercise videos that could be saved and scheduled within a personal plan.

• Self-monitoring and sharing. Optional daily ratings of symptoms and mood, and a one-page summary in PDF format for participants to share with their clinical team.

• Read-aloud function. All coaching messages could be played aloud in a natural-sounding synthesised voice.

Responses from Mr Steady were generated by a large language model operating under a fixed set of instructions written by the research team. These instructions confined the agent to behavioural exercise coaching, prohibited medical, pharmacological or diagnostic advice, required the redirection of such queries to the participant's clinical team, and specified the response to language indicative of distress or crisis. Participants were informed at consent, and within the application, that Mr Steady was not a clinician. In keeping with an iterative development model, the application was updated during the study in response to participant feedback; for example, an explicit choice of light, moderate or vigorous effort was added to the logging process.

2.5 Measures

Feasibility was assessed in terms of recruitment, retention to follow-up, completeness of questionnaire data and self-reported frequency of application use.

Acceptability was assessed using a study-specific post-intervention evaluation questionnaire addressing overall satisfaction, perceived helpfulness, intention to continue use and to recommend the application, six usability items, experience of Mr Steady, perceived behavioural impact, educational materials, perceived change in five domains (mood, energy, confidence, routine and balance) and perceived burden. Items were rated on five-point Likert or categorical scales. The questionnaire addresses several constructs of the Theoretical Framework of Acceptability [14], including affective attitude, burden, perceived effectiveness and intervention coherence.

Exercise motivation was measured using the BREQ-3, which incorporates integrated regulation items [15] into the BREQ-2 [16]. Its 24 items are rated from 0 to 4 and scored as six subscales (amotivation, external, introjected, identified, integrated and intrinsic regulation), each calculated as the mean of four items.

Parkinson's-specific quality of life was measured using the PDQ-39 [17], which yields eight dimension scores ranging from 0 to 100 and a Summary Index (SI) [18], with higher scores indicating poorer quality of life. The published minimally important difference for the SI is 1.6 points [19].

Participant experience was explored through semi-structured interviews at follow-up (approximately 30 minutes), conducted in person or remotely by members of the research team.

The topic guide addressed overall experience, motivation and behaviour change, goal setting, interaction with Mr Steady, educational content, barriers and facilitators, emotional impact, safety and boundaries, and suggested improvements. Interviews were audio-recorded and transcribed.

2.6 Quantitative analysis

Questionnaire responses were transcribed manually from the completed forms. Feasibility and acceptability data are reported descriptively as frequencies, with 95% Wilson confidence intervals for key proportions. Change in BREQ-3 and PDQ-39 scores from baseline to follow-up was analysed in participants with data at both time points using paired-samples t-tests, with Wilcoxon signed-rank tests reported alongside in view of the small sample and non-normal distribution of difference scores. Mean change, its 95% confidence interval and the within-participant effect size (dz ) are reported as the primary quantities of interest. Given the exploratory aims of the study, p values are interpreted descriptively, and no correction for multiple comparisons was applied. A Relative Autonomy Index was not computed, because the evidence does not support the evenly spaced motivational continuum that its weighting assumes [20]; subscales are therefore reported separately. Amotivation and external regulation are reported descriptively only, because the majority of scores, and almost all change scores, were zero. PDQ-39 item 28 was treated as not applicable where so indicated, in accordance with the user manual.

The follow-up PDQ-39 of one participant was markedly inconsistent with that participant's evaluation questionnaire and interview (Section 3.4). The full-sample analysis is therefore reported as the primary analysis, and a sensitivity analysis excluding this participant is reported alongside it.

2.7 Qualitative analysis

Interview transcripts were analysed using reflexive thematic analysis [21, 22]. One researcher (OOC) undertook repeated reading of each transcript and coded the data line by line, generating initial and subsequently developed codes. The codes were reviewed by a second researcher (AS), and candidate themes were generated, reviewed against the complete data set and refined through discussion. The analysis was primarily inductive, with self-determination theory employed as a sensitising framework during theme development. Disconfirming cases, in particular the accounts of participants who disengaged, were actively sought and are reported within the relevant themes. Members of the team responsible for developing the application also conducted interviews. This was acknowledged reflexively, and participants were explicitly invited to describe aspects of the application that had not worked well.

2.8 Patient and public involvement and co-production

Ever-Steady was co-produced with people with Parkinson's from the earliest stage of its development, rather than designed by researchers and subsequently tested on users. Two patient and public involvement (PPI) focus groups were convened, each comprising 8 to 10 people with Parkinson's. Members contributed directly to the design, content and functionality of the application, so that its features reflected the priorities, preferences and everyday experiences of the people it is intended to support. Members asked for a warm, empathetic and non-competitive environment, noting that comparison with people at different stages of the condition could provoke performance anxiety; the application therefore contains no leaderboards or normative comparisons. They requested visual progress tracking and exercise options targeting specific needs, such as boxing for intensity and yoga for flexibility. Members also emphasised that physical capacity and mood fluctuate with medication cycles and symptom severity, and that a rigid schedule would therefore be unsuitable; in response, goals were made adjustable to the user's capacity on a given day. Finally, members informed the character of the conversational coach, which was designed to act as a supportive personal trainer that is neither overly human nor overly robotic. This collaborative approach was extended into the evaluation phase: the application was refined iteratively during the feasibility study in response to participant feedback (Section 2.4), and the interview data reported here will inform the next cycle of co-design.

3. Results

>Results

3.1 Recruitment, retention and engagement

Nine individuals were enrolled. One participant completed baseline assessments but withdrew before using the application. All eight participants who commenced the intervention completed follow-up, corresponding to a retention rate of 100% (95% CI 68% to 100%). All eight completers returned the BREQ-3, PDQ-39 and evaluation questionnaire and participated in an interview, yielding complete data for every outcome. Item-level missingness was minimal: two BREQ-3 items on which two responses had been marked were treated as missing, and three participants indicated that PDQ-39 item 28 was not applicable. Follow-up assessments were completed at the end of the eight-week intervention period. Seven of the eight participants (88%, 95% CI 53% to 98%) reported using the application on at least four days per week, consistent with the requested frequency of approximately five days. One participant reported discontinuing use after approximately three weeks, following a period of travel and pain. No participant reported seeking medical advice from Mr Steady, and no serious adverse events were reported to the research team.

3.2 Acceptability

Ratings of the application were predominantly favourable (Table 2). Six of the eight participants would recommend it to another person with Parkinson's, seven agreed that it integrated readily into daily life, and five reported exercising more since commencing use, with none reporting a reduction. Six participants reported improvement in at least one of mood, energy, confidence, routine or balance, and no participant reported deterioration in any domain. The median rating across the usability items was 4 (agree); ease of use, clarity of layout and confidence in independent use were each endorsed by six of the eight participants. Responses were polarised rather than uniformly moderate. Six participants gave positive or mixed ratings across most items, whereas two reported low satisfaction and did not intend to continue using the application. The accounts of these two participants are examined in the qualitative findings, as they identify specific and remediable causes of disengagement. The most frequently endorsed criticism concerned the time required for logging, reported by half of the sample, which the planned integration of wearable devices is intended to address.

3.3 Exercise motivation

Intrinsic motivation, the form of motivation most closely associated with sustained exercise, increased by 0.41 points on the 0 to 4 scale (95% CI 0.09 to 0.72; t-test p = .02; Wilcoxon p = .05; dz = 1.08), representing a large within-participant effect (Figure 1; Table 3). Six of the eight participants scored higher at follow-up, one scored lower and one remained at the scale maximum. Integrated regulation, the other fully autonomous form of motivation, changed in the same direction (+0.31; dz = 0.38). Controlled forms of motivation (external and introjected regulation) and amotivation were essentially unchanged, suggesting that use of the application was associated with greater enjoyment of exercise without an increase in perceived pressure. Identified regulation was close to the scale ceiling at baseline (3.55 of 4), which limited the scope for change. As an exploratory estimate derived from eight participants, the change in intrinsic motivation would not withstand correction for six comparisons and requires confirmation in a larger sample.

3.4 Parkinson's-specific quality of life

Six of the eight participants showed an improvement in the PDQ-39 Summary Index. In the full sample, the Summary Index improved by 1.52 points (p = .70; Wilcoxon p = .25), with lower (better) scores on six of the eight dimensions (Table 4). This mean was substantially influenced by one participant (ES-3), whose follow-up questionnaire recorded responses of "always" or "cannot do at all" on 32 of 39 items, corresponding to a deterioration of 24.5 points in the Summary Index relative to a considerably milder baseline profile. In the follow-up evaluation questionnaire, the same participant reported daily use of the application, a substantial increase in exercise and marked improvement in all five domains assessed, and described the application as "brilliant" at interview. This pattern is more consistent with a response error, such as reversal of the response scale, than with a genuine change in quality of life.

When this participant was excluded in a sensitivity analysis, the Summary Index improved by 5.24 points (p = .004), exceeding the minimally important difference of 1.6 points [19] by a factor of more than three, with improvement on seven of the eight dimensions and statistically significant gains in emotional well-being (-8.93 points, p = .037) and cognition (-7.14 points, p = .030). Both analyses are reported because the decision to exclude this participant was made after inspection of the data; the full-sample estimate therefore remains the primary result.

3.5 Qualitative findings

Five themes were generated from the eight interviews (Table 5). Quotations have been lightly edited for readability: ellipses indicate omitted words, square brackets indicate clarifications or probable transcription errors, and the names of third parties have been removed.

Theme 1: Making activity visible

The most widely shared benefit was that recording activity rendered it visible. For several participants, this altered their appraisal of how active they were: "It made me think I didn't do enough" (ES-1); "[It makes] me more aware of the exercises I've been doing" (ES-7).

Logging also broadened participants' conception of what constituted exercise. ES-7, who works as a carer, began to record the physical demands of caring: "going up and down the stairs fetching and caring for him ... All that is exercise." She proposed that the application refer to "physical activity" rather than "exercise". ES-8 described the application as "a little [nudge] just to realise that even minor exercise could be recorded, which I didn't tend to do", and ES-11 recorded gardening and housework on the basis that "I feel that is exercising, it's very good exercise." The act of recording appeared to be intrinsically rewarding: "you felt you've done something good, so it's worth recording" (ES-8). For ES-7, the log exerted a constructive sense of obligation: "it bugs me if I haven't done it. So that's a good ... motivation and impetus."

Theme 2: From intention to action

For a subset of participants, the application facilitated the translation of intention into new behaviour. ES-1 had set an initial goal of swimming a single length: "I did do a whole length without stopping ... and I managed to actually swim for about 40 minutes." She subsequently took out a gym membership and persuaded someone close to her to join: "it's made me more motivated to do stuff and ... to get [him] to actually commit and pay each month." She also reported that "it has made me walk a bit more", spending less time seated. ES-11 enrolled in a balance class, and ES-3 attributed his decisions to exercise directly to the application: "I already decided [to] exercise, but only because of the app."

The application also supported self-regulation. On reviewing the week, ES-7 identified missed yoga sessions and completed an additional recorded class "just to make up the lost sessions". ES-3 went further, attributing the stability of his medication regimen to exercising with the application. This represents a personal belief that the present study cannot evaluate, but it illustrates the significance that the application held for him.

Among participants with well-established exercise routines, the application appeared to consolidate rather than alter behaviour: "it reinforced a direction I was traveling in already" (ES-6). ES-9 and ES-7 described their exercise as governed by existing classes or by their own reasoning. These accounts suggest that the greatest potential benefit may lie among individuals who are less active at the outset.

Theme 3: A companion that listens

Mr Steady was the feature most frequently discussed, and the quality of the interaction largely determined participants' overall appraisal of the application. When the interaction functioned well, the companion was experienced as warm, intelligent and personal. ES-1 valued "having somebody there to greet you ... in the mornings" and observed of its name and manner that "it's nice, it's a feel good factor." ES-6 described it as "human sounding. I was quite impressed by it", and ES-3 as "very supportive. Very helpful." ES-8 noted that the companion queried an implausible entry, asking whether "4 months" was intended to read "4 miles": "Which I thought was quite clever." Attitudes could also become more favourable over time: ES-8 initially found the 9 am prompt "intrusive and annoying" but, after one or two weeks, "began to quite appreciate it ... It was just a useful reminder."

Conversely, when the interaction failed, engagement declined. ES-4 perceived the companion as unresponsive to his circumstances: "It wasn't listening to me ... It wanted to tell me", adding that "it didn't become a friend." Other difficulties were technical in nature and remediable: slow responses ("He takes too long to think", ES-1), a voice function that did not respond on one device ("I click on it. Nothing happens", ES-7), and switching between voice and text input, which was awkward in the presence of others (ES-9). The boundaries of the companion's role were clearly understood. No participant reported asking medical questions, and ES-1 noted that its role had been "very clear ... from the start."

Theme 4: Recognising effort

Participants sought feedback that reflected effort as they had experienced it, and early automated effort classifications did not always achieve this. ES-11 "got a bit disheartened when I felt I've really given [it] my all" and the application classified the session as light activity, and ES-6 felt that it tended to "rate everything the same". For ES-4, who was walking slowly with crutches during a period of pain, this generated a perception that the application "would have mocked me for what I was doing." In response to this feedback, an explicit choice of light, moderate or vigorous effort was introduced during the study.

Participants also indicated that the growth metaphor and goals required greater tangibility. Two participants did not progress beyond the first growth stage ("I'm still a seedling", ES-1) and requested smaller, visible increments; ES-1 cited her brother's cycling application, which counts down the distance remaining to a target. Most participants were unable to recall the goal established at baseline and wished the application to keep it in view: "It should have reminded me" (ES-3); "maybe I needed a bit more reinforcement" (ES-6). Participants further proposed rewards and stickers (ES-1) and aspirational, more demanding exercises to be unlocked (ES-3).

Accountability was experienced as double-edged. ES-11 "logged into it more out of guilt and motivation", and ES-1 described feeling "bad if I haven't done anything". Both regarded this as broadly beneficial; however, the experience of ES-4 demonstrates that the same mechanism may become a source of blame when it is not accompanied by recognition. Collectively, these accounts indicate that feedback should acknowledge effort relative to the individual's capacity on a given day.

Theme 5: Fitting around life with Parkinson's

The home-based format of the application was valued as a means of overcoming practical barriers: "If you can't get out and get transport. I can do it at home" (ES-1). ES-6 regarded the follow-along library as "a bit of a replacement for classes" when in-person attendance was not possible, and ES-11 wished to have discovered the option to save favourite videos earlier.

Use of the application was shaped by circumstances beyond it. A summer heatwave interrupted exercise for several participants (ES-6, ES-9, ES-11), and competing commitments limited the time available for the library: "Time is the precious" (ES-7). Participants recognised individual differences in receptiveness to digital self-monitoring. ES-11 considered that "it depends on your personality type", but anticipated that the application would suit people who enjoy technology, particularly those recently diagnosed, and concluded that "it's got lots of potential." ES-9 described a general tendency to abandon tracking applications after a few days.

Participants were explicit about the conditions that would sustain long-term use. Automatic logging via a wearable device was the most frequent request: "if it can be done automatically in form of a watch that would be fantastic" (ES-7), and ES-3 asked to take part in the planned wearable study. Peer connection was the second most frequent request: ES-3 described a group chat for users as "the end product", ES-7 explained that WhatsApp groups already sustained her attendance at classes, and ES-9 considered that motivation requires "an exercise buddy". A brief introductory walkthrough highlighting the application's features was also requested (ES-1, ES-6).

4. Discussion

>Discussion

4.1 Principal findings

This first evaluation indicates that an AI-supported conversational coaching application can be delivered to older adults with Parkinson's disease with high retention, regular use and predominantly favourable appraisal. All eight participants who commenced the intervention completed follow-up, seven reported using the application on at least four days per week, and six would recommend it to others with Parkinson's.

The most notable preliminary finding concerns motivation. Intrinsic motivation for exercise increased with a large within-participant effect, whereas guilt-based and externally controlled motivation did not change. Within self-determination theory, this profile is the one most strongly associated with sustained exercise behaviour [10, 11]. The qualitative findings offer a plausible account of this change: logging and reflection rendered activity visible and rewarding (Theme 1), and for several participants this was accompanied by new or extended activity, ranging from a 40-minute swim to enrolment in a balance class (Theme 2). The quality-of-life findings are less certain but are directionally consistent: the Summary Index improved in the full sample, and by more than three times the minimally important difference once a probable response error was taken into account.

Goal-setting theory offers a complementary interpretation of these findings [23]. The theory holds that goals influence behaviour when feedback keeps them salient, and the application fulfilled one part of this process more fully than the other. Daily logging exposed discrepancies between intended and actual activity and prompted corrective action, as when participants scheduled additional sessions to compensate for those missed. However, most participants were unable to recall the SMART goal established at baseline. That benefits were nonetheless observed suggests that selfmonitoring, and the accountability associated with it, was the principal active ingredient, and that strengthening goal reinforcement offers a clear opportunity to augment the effects of the intervention.

These findings compare favourably with previous digital interventions in Parkinson's disease. Retention matched the 100% reported for a virtual exercise coach over one month [8], here over twice that period and with an open-ended language-model companion rather than scripted dialogue. Consistent with an earlier mobile health trial in which benefit was concentrated among sedentary participants [7], participants who already exercised regularly described consolidation rather than change. This suggests that individuals who are less active, or recently diagnosed, may derive the greatest benefit.

4.2 Implications for intervention design

The qualitative findings translate directly into a development agenda, several elements of which are already under way.

1. Recognition of effort rather than volume alone. Feedback that misjudged effort was the principal source of discouragement and, in one case, contributed to disengagement. Selfrated effort was introduced during the study; feedback should additionally acknowledge effort relative to the individual's capacity on a given day, particularly during symptom fluctuation, pain or illness. 2. Persistent visibility of goals. Most participants were unable to recall their baseline goal.

Goals should be displayed on the home screen, revisited within conversations and divided into visible incremental steps. 3. Reliable responsiveness of the companion. Response latency and a voice function failure on one device were the principal technical barriers. Reliable voice interaction, with a clear choice between voice and text input, is a prerequisite for the companion to function as intended. 4. Reduction of logging burden. Half of the sample considered logging time-consuming, and wearable integration was the most frequently requested feature. Integration with consumer wearable devices is in development for the next study. 5. Peer connection and structured onboarding. Participants requested a moderated group chat among users and a brief walkthrough to highlight features such as saved videos and weekly summaries.

The two participants who disengaged are informative rather than anomalous. Their difficulties, namely a sense of not being heard, misjudged effort and awkward switching between voice and text, correspond to the first three priorities above. When asked how he would redesign the application, one of these participants stated that it "needs to be very personal. It needs to be encouraging", which reflects the intended direction of the next version.

4.3 Strengths and limitations

A principal strength of this work is that the application was co-produced with people with Parkinson's, through two PPI focus groups of 8 to 10 members each, from the earliest stage of its development; this is likely to have contributed to the high retention and favourable acceptability observed. The study also combined validated outcome measures, a structured acceptability questionnaire and in-depth interviews with every participant who completed follow-up, and disconfirming accounts were explicitly incorporated into the qualitative analysis. Outcome data were complete for all completers.

The findings should nevertheless be regarded as preliminary. The sample was small and drawn from a single centre whose attendees were already engaged with exercise, and the absence of a control group means that observed changes cannot be attributed to the intervention; regression to the mean, seasonal effects and expectancy may each have contributed. The intrinsic motivation finding is one of six exploratory comparisons and would not withstand correction for multiplicity. The PDQ-39 sensitivity analysis excluded a participant after inspection of the data. Application use was selfreported rather than derived from system logs, and the application was modified during the study in response to feedback. Interviews were conducted by members of the development team, which may have encouraged favourable accounts, although participants were explicitly invited to criticise the application and several did so in detail.

4.4 Implications for future research

The next study should be a larger, controlled feasibility trial of the revised, wearable-integrated application with pre-specified progression criteria. On the basis of the present data, appropriate criteria would include retention of at least 80%, objectively logged use on at least three days per week in at least 70% of participants, and the absence of serious application-related adverse events. Intrinsic motivation (BREQ-3) should be pre-specified as the primary efficacy-related outcome for a subsequent definitive trial, with the PDQ-39 Summary Index as a key secondary outcome. Recruitment should extend beyond a single therapy centre, including through NHS movement disorder services, in order to reach less active and more diverse participants. Application use should be captured from system logs.

4.5 Conclusions

Ever-Steady, an application co-produced with people with Parkinson's, was feasible to deliver and acceptable to the majority of those who used it. Over the study period, its use was associated with a large increase in intrinsic motivation for exercise without any increase in pressure or guilt, and with participant-reported increases in activity, confidence, routine and balance. Participants valued the AI companion when it was responsive and articulated clearly how it could be improved. These findings support continued development of the intervention and its evaluation in a larger controlled study.
