How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard), also Internal Review Board - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term); not IEC followed by a number, which is a technical standard - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee/Commission, in the singular or plural - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee, Bioethics Committee/Commission, Ethikkommission - Ethical Review Authority/Unit/Office/Agency - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Human Research Protection Office/Program, Office for Human Subjects Protection - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee(s) on/for (Medical and Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt - Ethics review that was not needed: Ethics review was not required, did not require further ethical review, exempt from formal human research ethics review, no new institutional ethics review

A template line the authors have not filled in (“[Add ethics approval … here.]”) is not a statement.

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. A TRO uses them for each trov:TransparentResearchPerformance. * run_results$exit_status: the exit status of the process that ran the script (0 = ended normally); NA when the process did not report one (a time limit, or Docker failing to start the container). * artifacts: one row per file, with stage (deposit: as downloaded from the repository, hashed from the downloaded file; before_run: the run folder just before the first script ran, after metacheck’s own changes, such as rewritten paths and unpacked archives; after_run: only the files the scripts made (status new), changed (changed) or removed (removed), as an unchanged file is the one listed before the run), path (relative to the run folder, as valid UTF-8, a byte of another encoding written as <xx>), size (bytes), sha256, mime_type (by extension, NA when unknown), source (the file’s address in the repository, for deposit). The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). Files metacheck itself copied or made between passes (see modifications, output_copied, file_placed) show in after_run too. A TRO uses the rows as trov:ResearchArtifacts and each stage as a trov:ArtifactArrangement. * modifications$sha256_before, modifications$sha256_after: the hash of the changed script as downloaded and as it ran; NA when the script is not at the same path in both (a script made from an R Markdown file). * environment: the system that ran the code (trov:TransparentResearchSystem in a TRO): metacheck_version, started_at, ended_at, host (R version, platform, operating system), sandbox, network_isolated (TRUE only when every Docker run had --network none; the process sandbox does not stop the network), internet, timeout_per_script, total_timeout, python_local (the Python run outside Docker and its version), and docker: runs (for each kind of run, r_run, r_install, python_run, python_install, the image and the docker run arguments, such as --network none, --read-only, --cap-drop ALL, no-new-privileges, the process limit and the memory and processor limits) and images (for each image, its ID and digests from docker image inspect, its creation time and the R and Python versions its environment declares).

run_results$checked says what the run of each script shows about the code, in five groups, so that a script that does not reproduce is kept apart from one the check could not test: ran (it ran to the end); code_error (it did not reproduce: it stopped with an error of the code or the deposit, could not be read, or needs a file the deposit does not hold); dependency (a package or program was not available: a package that could not be installed or was not loaded, a program outside R and Python, a system library, or R crashing in a package’s compiled code); time (it did not finish within the time limit for one script, or was not started because the time for all scripts had run out); and not_checkable (it could not be checked automatically: it needs a person, the internet, a key or another setting of the authors’ computer, one platform such as Google Colab, Windows or OpenSAFELY, more memory than the run allows, a file that was not downloaded, a screen, or a program that runs it, or it is a file of a package of the code that Python runs only as a module of that package). It is NA for files run inside another script, files that are not an analysis, files not read, and a Docker failure. summary_table counts each group (repro_ran_n, repro_code_error_n, repro_dependency_n, repro_time_n, repro_not_checkable_n), and the report’s first sentence gives them. The traffic light reflects the static readiness signals by default; a script in code_error that stopped with an error makes it red, as a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure, and a script in one of the other three groups keeps it from being green. Calls that need a screen, which the run does not have (dev.print(), dev.copy2pdf(), dev.copy2eps(), savePlot(), windows(), win.graph(), x11(), quartz(), View()), are replaced before the run by calls that need none, which modifications records as screen_call_replaced. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

Individuals' beliefs about stress have been linked to health and performance. The stress optimization model proposes regulatory strategies through which these beliefs may shape stress responses, but evidence for these mechanisms comes largely from laboratory research.

We investigated whether these mechanisms generalize to everyday life in a 14-day ecological momentary assessment study of N = 173 university students (86% women, 𝑀 𝑎𝑔𝑒 = 22.6).

Multilevel models examined associations of stress beliefs with stress regulation strategies, affect and somatic symptoms. Stress beliefs were associated with several regulation strategies on the between-person level, but explained little variability in how regulation changed with increasing stress intensity. Associations with affective and somatic responses were limited.

Thus, laboratory-derived stress-belief mechanisms appear to generalize only partially to everyday life. We discuss advantages of laboratory and daily-life research on this topic.

Interpretation is limited by a homogenous sample, potentially missed event-contingent assessments and independently modeling regulatory processes.

From Lab to Life: Stress Beliefs and the Stress Optimization Model in Everyday Stress

Stress is an evolutionary survival mechanism designed to facilitate coping with situational demands (Dhabhar, 2018). It triggers a cascade of psychological and physiological stress processes, which provide us with the resources to meet situational demands (Jamieson et al., 2018). Stress is thus associated with a number of beneficial outcomes such as increased productivity or personal initiative (Dienstbier, 1989; Fay & Sonnentag, 2002). However, stress has also been linked to a wide range of detrimental effects on performance, mental and physical health (Gilboa et al., 2008; Turner et al., 2020), which contributes to its negative image in public perception. Whether stress empowers or harms people seems not only to depend on characteristics of the stressor, like its intensity or duration (Malykhin et al., 2025), but also on characteristics of the person such as generalized stress beliefs (Crum et al., 2020).

This assumption has already been tested in number of laboratory studies (e.g., Crum et al., 2017; Jamieson et al., 2010 Jamieson et al., , 2012)). It is unclear, however, to what extend these principles also apply to the real world, as daily stressors and natural stress beliefs fundamentally differ from experimentally manipulated beliefs and artificially induced stress in the lab.

Stress Beliefs and the Stress Optimization Model

Stress beliefs -also referred to as stress mindsets -describe people's generalized expectations and assumptions about the nature of stress and its consequences (e.g., "stress is harmful" vs. "stress is enhancing"; Jamieson et al., 2018). They have been associated with health, well-being, and performance across various populations (Jamieson et al., 2018).

Experimental studies suggest that these associations may not be merely correlational: Altering how people think about stress can change how they respond to stressful experiences, for example, in terms of affect and physiological well-being (e.g., Crum et al., 2017; Jamieson et al., 2010 Jamieson et al., , 2012)). These findings raise the question: Through which mechanisms do stress beliefs shape individuals' stress responses when encountering stressful situations?

The Stress Optimization Model (Crum et al., 2020) provides a framework for understanding these mechanisms by proposing that stress beliefs can alter the goals that guide stress regulation. The model identifies four domains of stress regulation -Situation Selection, Attentional Deployment, Cognitive Change and Response Modulation -which can be used in ways aimed at optimizing or avoiding stress (see Table 1, Crum et al., 2020). Through these regulatory pathways, stress beliefs are hypothesized to shape individuals' stress responses, thereby contributing to downstream differences in health, well-being, and performance (Crum et al., 2020).

From Laboratory Evidence to Everyday Stress

Recent research has provided empirical support for the mechanisms proposed by the Stress Optimization Model. However, a substantial part of the evidence stems from laboratory studies, in which participants are randomly assigned to either an experimental group, in which stress beliefs are manipulated, or a control group, which receives no treatment or a placebo treatment (e.g., Hangen et al., 2019; Jamieson et al., 2010 Jamieson et al., , 2012;; Yeager et al., 2022).

Participants are then instructed to complete some kind of performance task where stress is artificially induced and groups are compared with respect to various outcomes such as stress regulation strategies, affect or the physiological stress response. Manipulations of stress beliefs range from simple instructions or information about stress (e.g., Beltzer et al., 2014) to more elaborate stress mindset interventions (Yeager et al., 2022).

Laboratory evidence suggests that stress beliefs can shape several of the regulatory processes proposed by the Stress Optimization Model. More positive stress beliefs have been associated with reduced attentional deployment on negative aspects and enhance attentional deployment on positive aspects of a stressful situation (Kilby & Sherman, 2016; Yeager et al., 2022), evaluating the stress response as more helpful (Yeager et al., 2022) and approaching stressful situations (Crum et al., 2013). A complementary line of laboratory research suggests that at least some of these regulatory processes can, in turn, causally influence downstream stress responses such as affective and physiological responses to laboratory stressors (Beltzer et al., 2014; Brooks, 2014; Crum et al., 2017; Jamieson et al., 2010 Jamieson et al., , 2012)). Combined, these findings provide support for both links in the proposed pathway: Generalized stress beliefs appear to shape regulatory processes, and experimentally altering some of these processes can influence subsequent stress responses. However, these links have largely been studied under controlled laboratory conditions.

Evidence that stress beliefs are relevant under naturally occurring stressors is accumulating. EMA and daily-diary studies suggest that stress beliefs are associated with affective responses to everyday stressors (Daniels et al., 2006; Laferton et al., 2020 Laferton et al., , 2026) ) and health outcomes such as somatic symptoms (Fischer et al., 2016; Jin et al. 2026).

Considerably less is known about the regulatory processes that might account for these associations in everyday life. Only two field studies (Casper et al., 2017; Laferton et al., 2026) examined components of the stress regulation strategies described in the stress optimization model with mixed results. These studies, however, only focused on single expressions of regulatory domains and also show limitations in terms of power and the assessment of stressors (Laferton et al., 2026).

The current study

Taken together, laboratory research provides initial support for the regulatory pathways proposed by the stress optimization model, but important gaps remain as the different regulatory domains have not been investigated together and most evidence stems from laboratory studies. A critical next step is to examine whether the effects of stress beliefs on stress regulation strategies and downstream stress responses are also evident in everyday life.

Accordingly, the present study had two main aims. First, we sought to test whether the regulatory processes proposed by the stress optimization model generalize to naturally occurring stressors. We examine effects on all regulatory domains including both optimizingand avoidance-oriented expressions. Second, we sought to replicate and extend previous findings on stress responses by examining associations of stress beliefs with positive and negative affect and somatic symptoms in daily life. For both aims, we not only investigate whether individuals with different stress beliefs differ in their typical regulatory patterns and stress responses, but also whether stress beliefs moderate how these processes change with stress intensity. In contrast to previous field studies, we combine time-and event-contingent assessments of daily stressors to increase the ecological validity of our findings.

Methods

Study Design and Participants

University students of six German universities were invited to participate in an EMA study about stress perception via e-mail lists, research participation platforms or notifications in lectures between March 2024 and September 2024. Inclusion criteria were age ≥18 years, German language comprehension and provision of written informed consent. People who participated in another intervention study were excluded. Participation was voluntary and as compensation for their efforts, participants had the chance to win gift certificates and to receive course credits for their research participation. After giving informed consent, participants were invited to complete a baseline questionnaire containing demographics, and various person-and health-related variables. From the following day, participants underwent a 14-day EMA period on daily stressors, during which they received three time-contingent prompts per day at randomly selected times within predefined morning (8 a.m. -12:20 p.m.), afternoon (12:20 p.m. -4:40 p.m.), and evening (4:40 p.m. -9 p.m.) time windows. In addition to these scheduled assessments, participants could initiate an event-contingent assessment whenever they experienced a stressor. To provide a common frame of reference for identifying stressful events, participants were given the following definition of stress: "Being stressed means that you are in a situation that is personally meaningful to you and whose management places physical and/or psychological demands on you. This may involve everyday, one-time, recurring, or extraordinary situations or events.

Examples of potentially stressful situations include hurrying to arrive at work on time; exerting oneself during an exam to achieve the best possible grade; working faster or longer to meet a deadline; performing at one's best in an athletic competition in order to win; meeting new people; experiencing an interpersonal conflict; a separation; illness or injury; being threatened, insulted, discriminated against, or ignored; bereavement; financial difficulties; or moving." All assessments were conducted via the smartphone application m-path (http://www.m-path.io/). The definition and examples were presented to all participants at study enrollment and remained accessible in the study app throughout the EMA period. The study was approved by the local review boards of the [blinded for review]. All procedures performed were in accordance with the ethical standards of the institutional research committees and with the 1946 Helsinki declaration and its later amendments.

The sample size was determined with a power-analysis based on the effect sizes observed in a former 7-day EMA study (Laferton et al., 2026). We aimed for a power of 80% to detect cross-level interaction effects.

The data collection was ended when the sample size approached the target number of 190 -200 participants and only few new participants could be recruited. Of the 177 students who participated in the study, 4 had to be excluded for not providing data on stress beliefs at baseline resulting in a final sample of N = 173 participants.

Most of them (86.1 %) identified as female with a mean age of 22.6 years (SD = 4.79).

Despite their status as students, many participants (52.5 %) reported working more than 5 hours a week. Less than half of them (40.4 %) were in a relationship with only 6 participants having one or more children. With respect to the EMA period, participants completed 44 EMA assessments on average over the 14 days (SD = 3.5). The mean stress intensity score was 5.6 (SD = 7.4). All sample characteristics can be found in Table 2.

Measures

Baseline

Beliefs about Stress. Stress beliefs were measured with the Beliefs About Stress Scale (BASS; Laferton et al., 2018). This self-report inventory captures negative stress beliefs (BASS-N, eight items, example: "Being stressed affects my health in the short term"), positive stress beliefs (BASS-P, four items, example: "Being stressed enables me to work in a more focused manner") and controllability beliefs (BASS-C, three items, example: "Being stressed is something I am able to influence through my actions"). The Items are answered on a 4-point scale from 1 (completely disagree) to 5 (definitely agree). The BASS has been psychometrically validated, reliabilities were α = .75 for BASS-N, α = .85 for BASS-P and α = .74 for BASS-C.

Ecological Momentary Assessments

Daily Stressors. Stress intensity was assessed with the Brief Daily Stressors Screening Tool (BDSST, Scholten et al., 2020). The BDSST contains 10 items covering stressors from different life domains including work, social, and health problems. Participants are instructed to rate the strain from daily stressors (e.g., "Difficulties with family obligations (e.g., household chores, caregiving, raising children, school)") at the moment (event-contingent assessments) or since the last assessment (time-contingent assessments) on a 4-point scale from 1 (not at all) to 4 (very strong). The reliability of the total (sum) score was α = .84, higher values indicate a higher stress intensity.

Stress Regulation Strategies.

Stress regulation strategies were assessed based on the stress optimization model ( Crum et al., 2020) Response Modulation was assessed by asking participants to what extent they had attempted to reduce their feeling of stress (e.g., through relaxation techniques, eating, smoking, or alcohol) and to what extent they had attempted to utilize the energy associated with feeling stressed (e.g., to work faster, more focused, or for longer, or to respond more quickly). Situation Selection was assessed by asking participants to what extent they had attempted to avoid the stressful situation and to what extent they had attempted to face or resolve it. These four items were rated on a 5-point scale from 1 (not at all) to 5 (extremely).

Affective Stress Response.

The affective stress response was measured with the 10item short form of the Positive and Negative Affect Schedule (PANAS-SF, Watson et al., 1988). Positive affect (e.g, "inspired") and negative affect (e.g., "hostile") were assessed with five items each. Participants reported how they were feeling on a 5-point scale from 1 (very little or not at all) to 5 (extremely). Total (mean) scores of negative affect and positive affect showed reliabilities of α = .80 and α = .86, respectively. Somatic Symptoms. Somatic Symptoms were assessed with the eight item Somatic Symptoms Scale (SSS-8, Gierk et al., 2014). Participants were asked to what extent they felt affected by the listed symptoms (e.g., "Headaches"). Items are answered on a 5-point scale from 0 (not at all) to 4 (very strong). The reliability of the total (sum) score was α = .84, higher values indicate stronger somatic symptoms.

Statistical Analyses

To investigate how beliefs about stress affect the use of stress regulation strategies and the affective and somatic stress response, we ran multilevel analyses with time points of EMA assessments nested in persons. In the first step, intra class correlations (ICCs) were calculated for all outcomes (stress regulation strategies, affect and somatic symptoms). In the second step, we examined whether random slope models of the time-varying predictor stress intensity fitted the data better than random intercept models to empirically test for slope variation between persons. If model comparisons indicated significant slope variation, we fitted the final model including the time-invariant predictor stress beliefs and the cross-level interaction between stress intensity and stress beliefs. Following the recommendations of Enders & Tofighi (2007), the within-person predictor stress intensity was person-mean centered to allow an unconfounded interpretation of within-person effects and cross-level interactions. To also consider associations on the between-person level, we additionally included the personspecific mean stress score and the stress beliefs × mean stress interaction. All between-person variables were grand-mean centered to ease the interpretation of interaction effects. Separate models were estimated for each combination of BASS subscales (BASS-N, BASS-P and BASS-C) and outcomes. Based on an alpha-level of .05, we applied Bonferroni adjustment per research question in accordance with disjunction hypothesis testing (Rubin, 2021). The significance levels for each research question can be found in Supplemental Material A. All analyses were carried out in R using the packages lme4 (Bates et al., 2015) and lmerTest (Kuznetsova et al., 2017).

Exploratory Analyses

In addition to the analyses concerning our main research questions, we examined whether the associations between stress beliefs, stress regulation strategies and stress responses differ between stressor domains, particularly work-and social stress. For these analyses, the items "Dissatisfaction with studies/work" and "Difficulties with (side) jobs" were aggregated into a work stress score, whereas the items "Problems with close persons" and "Problems with other people" were aggregated into a social stress score. We ran the same analyses as for the total stress score, but for each domain separately. These domain-specific analyses are treated as (preregistered) exploratory analyses as this classification of stressors based on the present scale is not common practice. Hence, the results should be interpreted with caution and replicated using independent data.

Results

In a first step, we calculated the ICCs for all outcomes, which can be found in results

Tables 3 and 4. The ICCs for stress regulation strategies ranged from .32 (Situation Selection: Approach) to .39 (Response Modulation: Reduce). ICCs for the affective and somatic stress responses were comparable with .45 for negative affect, .39 for positive affect and .36 for somatic symptoms. In the next step we tested for between-person variance in associations between stress intensity and the respective outcome by comparing random-intercept and random-slope models. The random slope models fitted the data better for all outcomes indicating significant slope variation, hence, we fitted the final models including betweenperson variables and (cross-level) interaction terms. Results of models examining effects of stress and stress beliefs on stress regulation strategies can be found in Table 3. On the within-person level, stress was positively associated with the use of most of the stress regulation strategies, meaning that higher stress intensity was associated with a greater use of the respective strategy. Only the use of the optimizing strategies of Attentional Deployment (on positive aspects of the situation) and Cognitive Change (evaluation the stress response as helpful) were not dependent on stress intensity. The person-specific stress mean also indicated effects on the use of multiple (avoiding) strategies, specifically on attentional deployment on the negative aspects of the situation, evaluating the stress reaction as harmful and on avoiding stressful situations. That is, a higher person specific mean stress level was associated with a higher level of using the respective strategy.

Stress beliefs showed various associations with the use of stress regulation strategies on the between-person level, meaning that stress beliefs were associated with the overall level of using the respective strategy. All dimensions of the BASS were associated with attentional deployment on the positive aspects of a situation and evaluating the stress reaction as helpful.

While negative stress beliefs showed a negative effect on these optimizing stress regulation strategies, positive stress beliefs and controllability beliefs were associated with a greater use of these strategies. Moreover, negative stress beliefs were associated with a higher level of evaluating the stress reaction as harmful and trying to reduce the stress response. Higher controllability beliefs additionally showed a positive effect on the overall level of approaching stressful situations.

The cross-level interaction between stress beliefs and within-person stress intensity was non-significant with respect to most of the stress regulation strategies, with one exception: Negative stress beliefs moderated the association between stress and the tendency to approach stressful situations, that is, with more negative stress beliefs the association between stress intensity and approaching stressful situations was less positive (or more negative), which means that individuals use this strategy less the more intense stress they experience when holding negative stress beliefs. This effect is depicted in Figure 1. The only significant interaction on the between-person level of mean-stress and stress beliefs also emerged for negative stress beliefs and approaching stressful situations. That is, persons with a high mean stress level and negative stress beliefs tend to have a higher overall level of approaching stressful situations.

Results of models examining effects of stress and stress beliefs on the affective stress response and somatic symptoms can be found in Table 4. Stress on the within-and betweenperson level indicated effects on negative affect and on somatic symptoms. With higher stress intensity, individuals experienced more negative affect and stronger somatic symptoms. The same was true for the between-person level: Persons with a higher mean-stress level showed a higher overall level of negative affect and somatic symptoms. The only effect for beliefs about stress was found with respect to positive affect on the between-person level. Individuals with stronger controllability beliefs showed a higher overall level of positive affect. No crosslevel interactions or interactions on the between-person level were found with respect to the affective or somatic stress response.

Exploratory analyses

In our preregistered exploratory analyses, we examined whether the effects of stress differed between stressor domains, specifically between social and work stress. The results can be found in Supplemental Material B (Table B.1 to B.4). With respect to stress regulation strategies, a quite similar effect pattern was found for social stress and work stress. The only clear difference was found for the within-person effects of stress intensity. Social stress showed negative effects on attentional deployment on the positive aspects of stressful situations, which was not found for work stress. That is, people experiencing more intense social stress (not work stress) tend to focus less on the positive aspects of stressful situations. Conversely, work stress showed an effect on the response modulation strategy utilization of the stress response, which was not found for social stress. With higher work stress intensity, people tend to utilize their stress response more strongly. For work stress, we also found a significant cross-level interaction with positive stress beliefs on evaluating the stress response as harmful. That is, people with more positive stress beliefs show a less positive association between work stress intensity and evaluating the stress response as harmful. This effect was not found for social stress. With respect the affective and somatic stress response, no differences were found between effect patterns of work stress and social stress.

Discussion

The present study examined whether regulatory processes proposed by the stress optimization model (Crum et al., 2020) generalize to naturally occurring stressful experiences in everyday life. The findings provide partial support for this generalization. Stress beliefs were systematically associated with several regulatory processes at the between-person level, consistent with the proposition that generalized stress beliefs are reflected in how individuals approach and regulate stressful experiences. Individuals with stronger negative stress beliefs tended to report less optimizing regulation and more avoidance-oriented regulation, whereas positive and controllability beliefs showed associations in the opposite direction for several regulatory domains, which is in line with findings of laboratory studies (Crum et al., 2013; Kilby & Sherman, 2016 , Yeager et al., 2022). At the same time, there was little evidence that stress beliefs explained how regulation changed as stress intensity increased. This absence of cross-level interactions cannot simply be attributed to a lack of individual variability in stress reactivity: Models allowing the association between stress intensity and the respective outcomes to vary between individuals consistently provided a better fit. Thus, individuals differed in how their regulation changed as stress became more intense, but stress beliefs explained little of this variability.

Compared to the first aim of this study, evidence regarding stress responses was more limited. As expected, higher stress was associated with greater negative affect and stronger somatic symptoms at both the within-and between-person level. However, stress beliefs showed few associations with these downstream responses. In this regard, our findings provide less support than previous laboratory studies in which manipulated stress beliefs or stress-response appraisals have altered affective and physiological responses to acute stressors (Crum et al., 2013; Laferton et al., 2023; Yeager et al., 2022), and field studies indicating an association of stress beliefs and affective and somatic stress responses (Laferton et al., 2020 (Laferton et al., , 2026;; Fischer et al., 2026). This discrepancy may be explained by various factors: First, we used an EMA design combining event-and time-contingent assessments, while previous field studies mostly relied on time-contingent assessments. While stress regulation strategies (for which we found multiple effects) unfold directly in the stressful situation, the affective and somatic stress response might take more time and may thus not be reported in eventcontingent assessments. Second, effects of stress beliefs may depend on the type of stressor.

Previous studies mainly focused on social and work stress (e.g., Laferton et al., 2026), whereas the present study assessed a broader range of everyday stressors.

Laboratory versus Field Evidence

Taken together, the pattern of findings illustrates both the value and the limits of translating stress-belief research from controlled experiments to everyday life. Laboratory studies typically make a particular interpretation of stress salient immediately before a stressor, whereas "natural" stress beliefs develop across many years of experiences. Similarly, in laboratory studies, stressors itself are closely controlled and standardized across participants. Such designs are essential for identifying causal effects. Everyday stressors, on the other hand, differ substantially in content, intensity, duration and personal consequences.

The regulatory processes that emerge across this heterogeneous set of experiences may differ from those elicited by a single standardized laboratory challenge.

In this regard, the EMA approach also reveals a distinction that is difficult to isolate in experimental designs: Repeated assessments allow to separate differences between individuals from within-person effects. In the present study, this distinction proved theoretically consequential: Stress beliefs showed several associations with typical regulatory patterns, but explained little of the variation in how regulation changed with stress intensity. Generalizing psychological mechanisms from the laboratory to everyday life may therefore require more than asking whether the direction of an experimental effect can be replicated, it also requires specifying the level at which the proposed mechanism operates. Laboratory settings, however, also offer some advantages regarding the assessment of regulatory processes. As the researcher defines the stressor, stress exposure is orthogonal to the stress mindset and stress evaluation by design. In EMA studies, especially in eventcontingent designs, the reporting of stressors may be confounded with stress beliefs and stressor evaluations. Moreover, the exact timing of stressors can be controlled. While some of the stress regulation strategies refer to the reactive phase following a stressor (e.g., Response Modification), Situation Selection is an anticipatory process which should be ideally measured right before or in the moment the stressor occurs and is thus hard to capture in EMA studies.

Limitations

Some limitations confine the interpretation of our results. First, our university-student sample was relatively homogeneous in age, gender, education, and life context, which very likely narrows the range of stressors experienced by participants and limits generalizability.

Second, our EMA design involved event-contingent assessments, which is advantageous in terms of ecological validity (Shiffman et al., 2008). However, in particularly stressful situations people may not always be able or willing to answer a survey. Although the timecontingent assessments allow us to capture stressful events that may have been missed due to situational burdens, they involve a certain degree of retrospection, which may introduce recall biases in the assessment. Third, our analyses considered the regulatory strategies independently, although regulatory processes are likely to interact across time: For instance, approaching a stressful situation creates opportunities for subsequent appraisal and response modulation. Modeling these temporal sequences were beyond the scope of the current study but should be clearly addressed in future research.

Conclusion

The present study provides a nuanced analysis of how mechanisms derived largely from laboratory stress-belief research translate into everyday life. While some laboratoryderived associations are evident in everyday life, we also identify important boundaries to this generalization. Future studies may aim for combining experimental modification of stress beliefs with intensive assessments in daily life to integrate and systematically compare their findings.

For each domain, we assessed the optimizing and the avoiding tendencies separately instead of combining them into a single bipolar score. Attentional Deployment was operationalized through threat-versus-challenge appraisal, reflecting attention to threatening versus potentially beneficial aspects of the stressful situation. Appraisal were assessed using items adapted from the Stress Appraisals of Acute Stress (SAAS; Mendes et al., 2007), following the adaptation used by Yeager et al. (2022). Participants rated the statements "I felt threatened by this situation" and "I felt that the situation challenged me in a positive way" on a 7-point scale ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 7 (strongly agree). Cognitive Change was operationalized through participants' appraisal of their bodily stress response as harmful versus helpful for performance. Participants indicated their agreement with the statements "I felt that my body's stress responses impaired my performance in the situation" and "I felt that my body's stress responses supported my performance in the situation" on a 5-point scale ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 5 (strongly agree).
