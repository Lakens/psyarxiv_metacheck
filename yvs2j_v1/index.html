This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard), also Internal Review Board - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term); not IEC followed by a number, which is a technical standard - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee/Commission, in the singular or plural - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee, Bioethics Committee/Commission, Ethikkommission - Ethical Review Authority/Unit/Office/Agency - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Human Research Protection Office/Program, Office for Human Subjects Protection - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee(s) on/for (Medical and Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. When a statement is expected

The module sorts each paper by where its data came from (the internal .data_source(); the signs and how the rule was tested are described in R/data-source.R): - own: the authors collected data from people or animals, for this paper, in an earlier study of their own, or (a protocol) will collect them; - existing_records: records about individual people that the authors obtained for this study, such as hospital records or a cohort; - existing_unclear: existing data about people from a source the module could not identify; - open_resource: an open resource any researcher can use, such as UK Biobank or NHANES; - none: no data about people or animals (a review, simulation, commentary, theory, tutorial, coding of published papers, or figures about groups); - uncertain: the signs were missing or pointed both ways; - no_text: the paper has no text.

The module reads the text, and the results of power, stat_p_exact, stat_check, repo_check, data_check and prereg_check when they ran before it (run it after them); without stat_p_exact, it finds the p-values in the text.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. A TRO uses them for each trov:TransparentResearchPerformance. * run_results$exit_status: the exit status of the process that ran the script (0 = ended normally); NA when the process did not report one (a time limit, or Docker failing to start the container). * artifacts: one row per file, with stage (deposit: as downloaded from the repository, hashed from the downloaded file; before_run: the run folder just before the first script ran, after metacheck’s own changes, such as rewritten paths and unpacked archives; after_run: only the files the scripts made (status new), changed (changed) or removed (removed), as an unchanged file is the one listed before the run), path (relative to the run folder, as valid UTF-8, a byte of another encoding written as <xx>), size (bytes), sha256, mime_type (by extension, NA when unknown), source (the file’s address in the repository, for deposit). The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). Files metacheck itself copied or made between passes (see modifications, output_copied, file_placed) show in after_run too. A TRO uses the rows as trov:ResearchArtifacts and each stage as a trov:ArtifactArrangement. * modifications$sha256_before, modifications$sha256_after: the hash of the changed script as downloaded and as it ran; NA when the script is not at the same path in both (a script made from an R Markdown file). * environment: the system that ran the code (trov:TransparentResearchSystem in a TRO): metacheck_version, started_at, ended_at, host (R version, platform, operating system), sandbox, network_isolated (TRUE only when every Docker run had --network none; the process sandbox does not stop the network), internet, timeout_per_script, total_timeout, python_local (the Python run outside Docker and its version), and docker: runs (for each kind of run, r_run, r_install, python_run, python_install, the image and the docker run arguments, such as --network none, --read-only, --cap-drop ALL, no-new-privileges, the process limit and the memory and processor limits) and images (for each image, its ID and digests from docker image inspect, its creation time and the R and Python versions its environment declares).

run_results$checked says what the run of each script shows about the code, in five groups, so that a script that does not reproduce is kept apart from one the check could not test: ran (it ran to the end); code_error (it did not reproduce: it stopped with an error of the code or the deposit, could not be read, or needs a file the deposit does not hold); dependency (a package or program was not available: a package that could not be installed or was not loaded, a program outside R and Python, a system library, or R crashing in a package’s compiled code); time (it did not finish within the time limit for one script, or was not started because the time for all scripts had run out); and not_checkable (it could not be checked automatically: it needs a person, the internet, a key or another setting of the authors’ computer, one platform such as Google Colab, Windows or OpenSAFELY, more memory than the run allows, a file that was not downloaded, a screen, or a program that runs it, or it is a file of a package of the code that Python runs only as a module of that package). It is NA for files run inside another script, files that are not an analysis, files not read, and a Docker failure. summary_table counts each group (repro_ran_n, repro_code_error_n, repro_dependency_n, repro_time_n, repro_not_checkable_n), and the report’s first sentence gives them. The traffic light reflects the static readiness signals by default; a script in code_error that stopped with an error makes it red, as a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure, and a script in one of the other three groups keeps it from being green. Calls that need a screen, which the run does not have (dev.print(), dev.copy2pdf(), dev.copy2eps(), savePlot(), windows(), win.graph(), x11(), quartz(), View()), are replaced before the run by calls that need none, which modifications records as screen_call_replaced. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

Jail-based mental health interventions are delivered under conditions of short and unpredictable stays, making treatment engagement difficult to evaluate. Although Dialectical Behavior Therapy (DBT) has demonstrated promise in correctional settings, little is known about how engagement changes over time in jail-based DBT. Participants (N = 325; 84.9% male) were enrolled in a six-week, jail-adapted DBT skills group as part of a larger randomized controlled trial. Facilitators provided weekly ratings of attendance, in-session participation, homework completion, and diary card completion. Latent Profile Growth Modeling identified engagement trajectories among participants who attended at least three sessions (N = 232), and mixed-effects models examined whether profile membership predicted changes in mental health symptoms and coping. Participants attended an average of approximately 6 of 12 sessions, and 40.2% completed the full cycle. Noncompletion occurred through multiple routes, including release or transfer and the program's three-miss attendance criterion. Three trajectories were identified: Declining Engagement (37.5%), High-Stable Engagement (29.7%), and High Early Engagement with Drop-Off (32.8%). The High-Stable group had the highest attendance and completion rate. The Declining group showed lower in-session participation, homework completion, and diary card completion beginning early in treatment, whereas the High Early Engagement with Drop-Off group remained relatively engaged across these indicators before a later decline in attendance. Noncompletion in the latter group was primarily associated with release or transfer. Profile membership was not associated with differential changes in symptoms or coping, although mental health symptoms and maladaptive coping decreased overall. These findings show that repeated engagement measures can distinguish early, broad declines in participation from later interruptions in attendance that would be obscured by completion status alone.

Introduction

Over 12 million Americans are booked into local jails annually (Turney & Conner, 2019; Zeng, 2020). Jail stays are typically brief and unpredictable, yet many individuals cycle repeatedly through these settings and present with substantial mental health needs (Fox et al., 2019; James & Glaze, 2006; Trestman et al., 2007). Jails are often under-resourced and disrupted in ways that complicate treatment delivery and evaluation, but the high prevalence of mental health problems creates a clear need for intervention, and prior studies indicate that many jail residents are willing to participate when treatment is offered (James & Glaze, 2006; Moore et al., 2018). Because structural and motivational factors both shape engagement in these settings, understanding how engagement and dropout unfold over time can inform how correctional treatments are adapted to different participation patterns. Dialectical Behavior Therapy (DBT) has been proposed as a promising intervention for psychological adjustment in correctional settings (Steb et al., 2026). DBT was originally developed to treat borderline personality disorder and suicidal behaviors (Linehan, 1993) and emphasizes cognitive-behavioral principles, mindfulness, and skills for emotion regulation and interpersonal effectiveness. Standard, "full-model" DBT includes individual therapy, group skills training, between-session coaching, and a consultation team, but correctional settings typically deliver modified or skills-focused formats due to institutional constraints (Tomlinson & Hoaken, 2017). Preliminary evaluations in forensic and correctional settings link DBT to reductions in institutional infractions, impulsivity, and self-harm (Nee & Farman, 2005; Shelton et al., 2009; Steb et al., 2026), but much less is known about DBT adaptations in shorter-term detention settings such as jails (Moore et al., 2018; Verona et al., 2025), and few studies have examined the therapeutic processes underlying DBT's effectiveness in correctional contexts.

Engagement in treatment is central to DBT's theoretical and practical framework: the intervention depends on active skill practice, in-session participation, and between-session assignments such as diary card use and homework completion, which are theorized to support skill acquisition and independent application of DBT skills (Linehan, 1993; Neacsiu et al., 2010).

Across the broader psychotherapy literature, engagement has been conceptualized as spanning multiple domains, including behavioral factors such as attendance, cognitive factors such as active participation and retention of material, and relational factors such as the therapeutic alliance (Holdsworth et al., 2014). Much of this literature, however, operationalizes engagement dichotomously (e.g., completers versus non-completers) or examines dropout as a function of baseline characteristics (Sharf et al., 2010; de Haan et al., 2013; Imel et al., 2013)-approaches that are useful but may obscure meaningful variation in how participation unfolds over time.

A growing body of psychotherapy research conceptualizes engagement as a process that changes over treatment, rather than a fixed outcome such as completion or dropout (Fernandez et al., 2015; Morgan-Lopez & Fals-Stewart, 2007), and as multidimensional, spanning attendance, in-session participation, homework completion, and other treatment activities (Holdsworth et al., 2014). Participants with similar attendance may therefore still differ in how actively and consistently they engage, with different implications for outcomes. Longitudinal, personcentered approaches can capture these differences, including whether participation remains stable, declines early, declines later, or becomes irregular (Collins, 2006; Jung & Wickrama, 2008).

Prior studies illustrate several distinct ways that person-centered analyses can improve the study of treatment engagement. Morgan-Lopez and Fals-Stewart (2007) identified distinct attendance profiles, including early dropout and irregular attendance, using a latent class pattern-mixture model in a trial comparing rolling group and individual alcohol treatment. In their initial analyses, participants assigned to rolling group treatment appeared to show better alcohol-use outcomes than those assigned to individual treatment; however, this treatment-condition difference was no longer significant after attendance-profile membership was modeled. This finding suggests that conclusions about treatment effectiveness can change when different patterns of attendance are considered. Roedelof et al. (2013) used longitudinal latent class analysis to identify high-, medium-, and low-engagement profiles among adolescents receiving case management for severe psychiatric problems. The high-and medium-engagement groups appeared similar early in treatment but later diverged, and improvement in global functioning was observed only in the high-engagement group. Thus, the profiles identified differences in clinical course that would not have been apparent from an early engagement rating alone. Gros et al. (2023) used growth mixture modeling to identify symptom-response trajectories among veterans receiving transdiagnostic behavior therapy and then examined whether treatment participation differed across those trajectories. Some of the nonresponders showed lower insession participation and homework engagement than responders, indicating that specific forms of active involvement may distinguish treatment response in ways that attendance alone does not capture. Finally, Aschbacher et al. (2023) used longitudinal cluster analysis to identify sustained, immediate, early, and late disengagement in a therapist-supported digital intervention. This study is relevant to the present findings because our engagement profiles were also distinguished in part by the timing of disengagement. Specifically, the Declining Engagement profile showed early reductions across multiple forms of participation, whereas the High Early Engagement with Drop-Off profile remained comparatively engaged through the first several weeks before later attendance declined. Thus, Aschbacher et al. (2023) provides a useful example of how repeated engagement data can identify distinct windows of disengagement that may call for different forms of support. Together, these studies demonstrate that person-centered approaches can clarify when disengagement begins, distinguish specific forms of participation beyond attendance, and show how engagement patterns may shape conclusions about treatment response.

These types of analyses may be particularly useful in correctional settings, where inconsistent participation can reflect both individual engagement and disruptions outside the participant's control. Meta-analytic estimates indicate attrition rates of approximately 25% to 35%, with substantial variability across programs and settings (McMurran & Theodosi, 2007; Olver et al., 2011). Structural barriers, including early release, transfers, and limited continuity of care, may interrupt treatment and create different patterns of participation even among individuals who are willing to engage (Morgan et al., 2012). Completion status may therefore conflate disengagement from treatment with loss of access to treatment and may not accurately reflect an individual's interest or motivation to participate. This distinction matters because treatment noncompletion in correctional programs has often been linked to later recidivism, but most studies treat noncompletion as a single category. McMurran and Theodosi (2007) found that offenders who started but did not complete cognitive-behavioral treatment were more likely to be reconvicted than untreated comparison groups, and Olver et al. (2011) found that attrition predicted higher general, violent, and nonviolent recidivism across offender treatment studies. Wormith and Olver (2002) also found that noncompleters in an institutional violence program had higher risk profiles and poorer treatment-process ratings than completers. However, Wormith and Olver (2002) also noted that correctional attrition can occur through different routes, including client-initiated dropout, agency-initiated removal, and administrative exits such as release or transfer. These routes are likely to differ in how closely they reflect active disengagement from treatment. For example, continued nonattendance despite ongoing access to the group may indicate a different participation process than nonattendance caused by release, transfer, or other loss of treatment access. Repeated measures of engagement may therefore help clarify whether noncompletion reflects declining participation during treatment, loss of treatment access, or both.

Current Investigation

Treatment engagement is central to the therapeutic model of DBT, which emphasizes active skills acquisition alongside between-session practices such as diary card use and homework completion. Although prior research has examined retention and participation rates for DBT-informed programs, including those conducted within forensic and correctional settings (Folk et al., 2023; Moore et al., 2018; Olver et al., 2011; Scanlon & Morgan, 2025), no study has investigated week by week engagement patterns in a DBT-informed treatment within a jail.

Since observed participation in that setting may reflect both active engagement and limited opportunity to attend treatment (Morgan et al., 2012), modeling week-by-week engagement across multiple domains offers an opportunity to characterize observed participation more precisely than completion status alone, while also evaluating the limits of these indicators in a highly unstable treatment context.

The present pre-registered study addresses gaps in the literature by examining weekly retention patterns and applying person-centered trajectory modeling to identify profiles of observed participation within a 6-week, twice-weekly DBT skills group for jail residents. In addition to characterizing engagement in a jail-based DBT program, we examined whether trajectory membership was associated with short-term psychological outcomes. Specific aims were to: (1) describe weekly retention and documented pathways to non-completion in a jailbased DBT skills group conducted with a large sample of participants; (2) empirically identify latent trajectories of a composite index of observed treatment participation, including attendance, session participation, homework completion, and diary card use; and (3) examine whether engagement trajectories are associated with short-term changes in maladaptive coping, adaptive coping, and mental health symptom severity.

Hypotheses were based on the limited treatment literature. First, we hypothesized a threeprofile solution characterized by relatively high, moderate, and low observed engagement, consistent with prior trajectory-based research (e.g., Roedelof et al., 2013). At the same time, given the limited application of latent profile growth modeling to therapeutic engagement-and the absence of prior studies in jail settings-we anticipated that engagement patterns could differ from those identified in other populations. Second, prior work on engagement-outcome relationships indicates that higher engagement profiles should be associated with better outcomes (e.g., Morgan-Lopez & Fals-Stewart, 2007), although this has not been tested in a jail-based treatment where structural barriers may attenuate such relationships.

Methods

Transparency and Openness

This study is part of a larger research project to evaluate programs for facilitating reentry and reducing recidivism in jails. The Institutional Review Board of the authors' affiliated institution approved the study. The present study hypotheses and analytic plan were preregistered on the Open Science Framework (OSF) prior to data analysis (https://osf.io/j2k45/overview?view_only=c03b47de7e99463f8451488655220c92). The preregistration includes detailed analytic plans, outcome variables, hypotheses, and data exclusions. Deviations from the preregistration are documented in the supplemental materials and include exploratory analyses comparing engagement trajectory profiles to minimally engaged (<3 sessions) participants. Upon completion of the parent project, anonymized data will be uploaded to the National Archive of Criminal Justice Data (NACJD), as per grant requirements.

Participants

As part of the larger study, 1,571 individuals incarcerated at the jail were initially screened, 665 eligible participants completed a baseline risk/needs assessment and assessment battery, and 640 individuals were randomized into one of four treatment/service conditions. The current study focuses on individuals randomized to receive the 6-week/12-session DBT skills group, totaling N = 325. Participants randomized to DBT had an average age of 37.24 years (SD = 10.72) and most identified as male (84.9%), not Hispanic or Latino (85.2%), and White (67.6%), followed by Black/African American (11.8%), Mixed race (11.7%), Native American (2.5%), Asian (0.3%), and Other (6.2%). Regarding education, 42.6% had a high school diploma or GED, 26.5% had completed some college, and 22.5% reported some high school education.

Consistent with the preregistered analytic plan, participants were included in the of latent profile growth modeling (LPGM) analyses (Aim 2) if they attended at least three sessions (N = 232). This a priori threshold ensured sufficient repeated engagement data to characterize withintreatment change rather than an isolated rating followed by nonattendance (Curran et al., 2010).

Participants excluded from the LPGM, who attended fewer than three sessions, provided insufficient repeated engagement data for longitudinal modeling; however, they were retained as for retention and descriptive analyses (Aim 1) and included as a minimally engaged group in exploratory outcome comparisons (Aim 3). Sociodemographic comparisons between participants included in the LPGM analyses and those who attended fewer than three sessions indicated no systematic differences on demographic, official criminal record, or psychosocial risk characteristics, as reported in Supplemental Table A. These results suggest limited sampling bias when using the subsample for LPGM analyses. See Figure 1 for participant flow across the three study aims and the analytic samples used for each analysis.

Power Analysis

Power estimation was primarily guided by the requirements of latent profile growth modeling, the central analytic approach for Aim 2. Traditional power calculations for LPGM are complex because they depend on factors such as the number of latent classes, indicator quality, class separation, and model specification. However, simulation studies suggest that samples of approximately 200-300 participants are generally adequate for identifying three to four classes when classification quality is acceptable and classes are sufficiently separated (Nylund-Gibson & Masyn, 2016). Thus, the LPGM analytic sample of 232 participants was considered sufficient for estimating engagement trajectories among participants with adequate repeated participation data.

For Aim 3, an a priori power analysis was conducted using G*Power 3.1 (Faul et al., 2007) to estimate the sample size needed to detect a Time × Participation Group interaction across two assessment points and four participation groups. Assuming a small-to-moderate effect size (f = .20), α = .05, and 80% power, the analysis indicated that approximately 280 participants would be required. The analytic samples for all three outcomes exceeded this threshold, ranging from 305 to 308 participants.

Procedures and DBT Intervention

As part of the larger reentry research project, individuals were recruited upon booking into jail or during housing pod announcements and flyers (see Verona et al., 2025, for Participants assigned to either DBT-receiving condition were enrolled in the DBT skills group, which began within several days. Groups were delivered in a rolling format twice weekly for 90 minutes over a 6-week period, for up to 12 sessions total. In contrast to typical DBT programs, which often use a "four-miss rule" for treatment discontinuation (Linehan, 2015), the jail-based program implemented a "three-miss rule," wherein participants who missed three consecutive sessions were removed from the group due to the shortened nature of the program.

Participants who missed three consecutive sessions were categorized as non-completers under the program's attendance policy.

The curriculum followed the four core DBT modules-mindfulness, emotion regulation, distress tolerance, and interpersonal effectiveness-with adaptations for the jail setting, including simplified language and jail-relevant examples. Homework was assigned after each session and reviewed at the start of the next session, and weekly diary cards were expected to be completed each day and turned in once per week. Additional details regarding the clinician credentials and training, development and jail-specific adaptation, and structure of the program are provided in Verona et al. (2025). Across the larger trial, 29 group sessions were formally evaluated using the DBT Skills Group Integrity Checklist (McKay et al., 2016). Ratings indicated strong adherence to both relational components (M = 2.87, SD = 0.22) and technical components (M = 2.78, SD = 0.25) of DBT delivery.

Indices of Treatment Engagement

After each DBT group session, facilitators completed the Group Leader Participant Rating Form, a structured observational tool adapted from prior frameworks used to assess behavioral engagement in forensic and correctional interventions (Drieschner et al., 2004; Sturgess et al., 2016). The form assessed four observable engagement domains: attendance, diary card completion, homework completion, and in-session participation. Attendance was coded as0 = absent or1 = present. Diary card and homework completion were rated on a five-point scale based on effort and completeness:0 = none,1 = limited,2 = average,3 = good, and4 = excellent. In-session participation was rated on the same five-point scale and reflected observable behaviors such as contributing to group discussion, responding to facilitator prompts, participating in skills practice, or providing peer feedback. Higher ratings reflected more active and consistent participation during the session. Because ratings were based on facilitator observation and completed assignments, these indicators captured observable treatment participation rather than internal aspects of engagement, such as motivation, perceived relevance of treatment, or therapeutic alliance.

Outcome Measures

Attempts were made to complete post-treatment assessments close to 10 weeks following baseline, within 4 weeks of each participant's final assigned DBT group session, or before participant release or transfer from the facility. Data Analysis Plan

For Aim 1, descriptive statistics in the full sample of DBT participants (N = 325) were used to characterize retention, attendance, and noncompletion patterns among all participants assigned to DBT. For Aim 2, latent profile growth modeling (LPGM) was used to identify trajectories of observed treatment participation among participants who attended at least three DBT sessions (N = 232). Before conducting the LPGM, we conducted an exploratory factor analysis (EFA) of the weekly engagement indicators. As shown in Supplemental Table B, a onefactor solution was retained as the most parsimonious and theoretically coherent model.

Accordingly, a weekly composite indicator of observed treatment participation was created by averaging attendance, diary-card completion, homework completion, and in-session participation within each week. Weekly composite scores across the six-week intervention were then analyzed in R Version 4.5.2 (R Core Team, 2025) using the lcmm package Version 2.2.2 (Proust-Lima et al., 2017). Latent class mixed models were estimated using the hlme() function. Linear and quadratic models with increasing numbers of profiles were estimated and compared based on model fit, classification quality, profile size, convergence, parsimony, and substantive interpretability. Model fit was evaluated using the log-likelihood, Akaike information criterion (AIC), and Bayesian information criterion (BIC), with lower AIC and BIC values indicating better relative fit. Classification quality was evaluated using mean posterior probabilities of profile membership and the distribution of participants across profiles.

For Aim 3, participants in the full DBT sample were classified into four participation groups: a minimally engaged group consisting of participants who attended fewer than three sessions and were therefore excluded from the LPGM, and the three empirically derived trajectory profiles identified in Aim 2. Linear mixed-effects models were estimated in R using the lme4 package (Bates et al., 2015) to examine Time × Participation Group effects on mental health symptoms, maladaptive coping, and adaptive coping. The number of days between baseline and post-treatment assessments was included as a covariate to account for variability in assessment timing.

Results

Aim 1: Retention and Non-Completion Patterns

To characterize week by week treatment exposure and retention, session attendance data were analyzed for the full sample of participants assigned to DBT (N = 325), including individuals who attended fewer than three sessions. In all, 133 (40.2%) participants completed the full cycle; 80 (24.6%) met the three-miss criterion for non-completion; and 101 (31.1%) were unable to complete due to release or transfer from the facility. A small number were no longer able to attend due to safety or institutional issues, such as disciplinary holds, or other reasons (n = 11; 3.4%). On average, participants attended approximately 6 of 12 DBT sessions (M = 5.91, SD = 4.19), with 167 participants (51.4%) completing 6 or more sessions. These patterns indicate that treatment exposure varied substantially across participants and that non-completion occurred through multiple routes. In fact, participants who completed the full cycle and those who did not were not different on measured demographic, official record, or psychosocial risk characteristics (see Supplemental Materials); thus, completion rates seem to be affected by more than individual-level factors in this sample.

Discussion

Despite growing evidence supporting the effectiveness of DBT in forensic and correctional settings, many DBT outcome studies provide limited information about participant engagement, often reporting overall completion rates without examining how observed participation unfolds over time. This lack of detail complicates efforts to interpret treatment engagement, particularly in jail environments where treatment exposure may be interrupted by release, transfer, or other institutional disruptions. To address this gap, the present study used repeated behavioral indicators to characterize observed engagement in a brief jail-based DBT skills group and to examine whether participation patterns were associated with short-term changes in coping and mental health symptoms.

In Aim 1, findings showed that many participants received at least partial exposure to jail-based DBT, although full program completion remained difficult. Approximately 40% completed the full cycle, and just over half attended six or more of the twelve sessions.

Noncompletion occurred through multiple observable routes, particularly release or transfer (31.1%) and missed sessions (24.6%). These patterns are broadly consistent with prior jail-based DBT research documenting substantial attrition amid unpredictable jail dynamics, including early release, disciplinary restrictions, logistical conflicts, staffing issues, and inconsistent transport (Moore et al., 2018). Interestingly, completion was not significantly predicted by the baseline individual characteristics examined, suggesting that institutional disruptions and treatment processes themselves affected changes in participation over time. This highlights how using completion/noncompletion status alone obscures more subtle differences in engagement, such as distinguishing participants who gradually disengaged from those whose treatment was interrupted. Aim 2 addressed this limitation by identifying longitudinal patterns across multiple indicators of engagement.

Engagement Trajectories

In Aim 2, we identified three patterns of participation among individuals who attended at least three sessions of the six-week DBT skills group: High-Stable Engagement, High Early Engagement with Drop-Off, and Declining Engagement. This result is consistent with prior studies showing that treatment participation is not simply high or low, but can remain stable, decline early, or drop later in treatment (Aschbacher et al., 2023; Roedelof et al., 2013). The present study extends this work to a jail-based DBT program, where a decline in engagement may reflect reduced engagement while still in custody, minimal participation while attending sessions, or loss of access to treatment because of release or transfer.

The profiles were related to treatment completion, but they were not simply another way of separating completers from non-completers. Nearly all participants in the High-Stable group completed treatment, as did about ¾ of the High Early Engagement with Drop-off profile. The latter group showed decreases in engagement later in treatment mostly due to release or transfer rather than from repeated absences or non-participation. In contrast, the Declining group had a much lower completion rate, engagement was evident earlier in treatment, and noncompletion resulted from both release or transfer and repeated absences. Thus, a later drop in attendance (as seen in the High Early Engagement with Drop-off profile) may not mean the same thing as an early decline in active participation (as showed by the Declining profile). Although prior studies have shown that treatment can be interrupted by release, disciplinary restrictions, and other institutional barriers even when participants remain interested in treatment (Moore et al., 2018; Sturgess et al., 2016), the current study empirically distinguishes individuals experiencing differential routes to treatment noncompletion, which can better inform strategies to increase treatment exposure and engagement.

The individual engagement indicators further clarified these differences. The High-Stable and High Early Engagement with Drop-Off groups scored similarly on in-session participation, homework completion, and diary-card completion. The Declining group, however, showed lower attendance, participation, and diary-card completion beginning in Week 2, with differences in homework completion emerging by Week 3. By Week 4, the Declining group was lower than both other profiles across all four indicators. This pattern resembles Roedelof et al. (2013), in which initially similar groups later diverged across several forms of engagement. It is also consistent with Gros et al. (2023), whose findings showed that in-session participation and homework engagement distinguished some treatment responders from nonresponders, underscoring that attendance alone does not fully capture treatment involvement.

The timing and form of disengagement may therefore have different programmatic implications. Aschbacher et al. (2023) suggested that participants who disengage early may need additional support establishing consistent treatment routines, whereas those who disengage later may be responding to new stressors or difficulty applying treatment content. Although these possibilities were proposed in their study of a digital intervention, the distinction is relevant to jail-based treatment, where early reductions across several forms of participation may warrant prompt individual follow-up after an initial missed session or incomplete assignment. In contrast, the High Early Engagement with Drop-Off profile suggests that later attendance loss may occur despite otherwise active participation and may reflect interrupted access rather than waning involvement. For these participants, the more relevant programmatic response may be to introduce core DBT concepts and relevant skills early in the cycle, or to increase the intensity of treatment across a shorter period of time (e.g. sessions occurring multiple times a week), so that essential material is delivered before an unexpected release or transfer. Thus, longitudinal engagement data may help programs distinguish when to prioritize early re-engagement efforts and when to adapt treatment sequencing to anticipated interruptions.

Relationship to Short-Term Outcomes

Although the engagement profiles captured meaningful differences in observed treatment participation, profile membership was not associated with differential changes in coping or mental health symptoms. This finding adds to a mixed literature on the relationship between engagement and treatment outcomes. Greater treatment engagement and completion have been associated with better clinical and behavioral outcomes in some studies, including lower recidivism and greater behavior change in correctional and forensic settings (Bourke et al., 2021; McMurran & Ward, 2010; Olver et al., 2011). However, these associations are often small to moderate and appear to vary by outcome and setting. For example, McMurran and Theodosi (2007) found that treatment noncompletion was associated with higher reconviction compared with untreated comparison groups, but the average effect was small (d = .16) and was stronger in community samples (d = .23) than institutional samples (d = .15). Similarly, Olver et al. (2011) found that treatment attrition was associated with higher general, violent, and nonviolent recidivism, with weighted effects ranging from rw = .10 to .23. A close comparison with the present study is also complicated by the fact that much of this research defined engagement as completion versus noncompletion, rather than examining week-by-week differences in attendance, participation, homework, and diary-card completion. Thus, prior findings on relationships between treatment completion and outcomes may not directly generalize to the more detailed engagement profiles examined here.

A potential complication for observing mental health and coping outcomes in the short term as a function of engagement trajectories involves the many barriers to skills practice clients encountered in the jail (see Verona et al., 2025). Indeed, DBT-related change depends not only on attending sessions and completing assignments, but also on learning, repeatedly practicing, and applying skills outside the group (Linehan, 2015; Valentine et al., 2015). Engagement is expected to support change by creating opportunities for skill acquisition and independent skill use. Jail conditions may limit those opportunities because participants have little privacy or autonomy and may have limited control over their daily routines, interpersonal contact, and access to coping resources. Even participants who attended consistently and completed treatment activities may therefore have had difficulty practicing and applying the skills often enough for differences between profiles to appear. Future studies could assess skill practice and skill use more directly, including practice outside of formal group sessions.

Another potential explanation for the Aim 3 findings is that the outcomes examined may not have captured the domains most likely to change during a brief jail-based DBT skills group.

Early DBT trials primarily focused on outcomes such as suicidal behavior, self-harm, psychiatric hospitalization, and treatment retention (Linehan et al., 1991; Linehan et al., 2006). Likewise, correctional DBT studies have more often examined behavioral outcomes such as institutional infractions, aggression, impulsivity, self-harm, and other forms of behavioral dysregulation (Moore et al., 2018; Shelton et al., 2009; Tomlinson & Hoaken, 2017). In contrast, the present study examined broad mental health symptoms and coping over a short follow-up period. Future research should examine whether engagement profiles are more strongly related to behavioral outcomes and skill use than to broad symptom measures.

A second explanation is that observed engagement in jail may partly reflect access to treatment rather than treatment response. In the present study, noncompletion occurred through several routes, including release or transfer and repeated missed sessions, and participants in the High Early Engagement with Drop-Off profile often remained actively engaged before later losing access to treatment. Consequently, lower observed engagement did not always indicate lower motivation or poorer treatment response. Instead, institutional factors such as release, transfer, court appearances, or lockdowns may weaken the relationship between engagement and short-term outcomes.

Limitations, Future Directions, and Implications

Several limitations warrant consideration. First, the study was conducted within a single jail facility, limiting generalizability to carceral settings with different populations, operations, resources, and programming structures. Replication across diverse jail settings is needed to determine whether similar engagement profiles emerge and whether relationships between observed engagement and treatment outcomes vary according to treatment stability, program structure, or continuity of care. Second, the available data did not include systematic information about the reasons for individual missed sessions. This limits interpretation of both the three-miss attendance category and the engagement profiles more broadly. In correctional settings, treatment participation may be shaped by both participant-level factors and restricted access to treatment (Morgan et al., 2012; Sturgess et al., 2016). The profiles should therefore be interpreted as patterns of observed engagement rather than direct indicators of why disengagement occurred. Future research should pair repeated engagement ratings with prospective information about treatment availability and reasons for nonattendance, as well as measures of therapeutic alliance, internal and external motivation, and perceived treatment relevance. These data would help clarify whether a given pattern primarily reflects participantlevel barriers, institutional constraints, or both.

Despite these limitations, the present study makes two important contributions. First, it demonstrates that noncompletion in jail-based treatment is not a uniform event. Participants who showed early declines across several treatment activities differed from those whose participation was later interrupted, often in the context of release or transfer, despite both showing more noncompletion than the High Stable Engagement profile. These patterns have different programmatic implications: early declines may warrant prompt engagement support, whereas later interruptions highlight the importance of delivering essential material before treatment is unexpectedly discontinued.

Second, the findings have implications for how correctional programs measure and report engagement. Completion status alone combines participants who disengage across several aspects of treatment with those whose participation ends because treatment is no longer available. Participants in our study followed distinct patterns that differed in when engagement declined, which aspects of participation were affected, and routes to noncompletion (e.g., repeated absences, release and transfer). These differences matter because early declines across several forms of participation may call for prompt engagement-focused follow-up, whereas later interruptions among participants who had otherwise remained actively involved highlight the importance of delivering core skills early or in a more intense fashion across a shorter time frame.

At the same time, engagement profiles were not associated with differential short-term changes in coping or mental health symptoms. In jail settings, observed participation may reflect access to treatment or opportunities to practice skills, as much as motivation or treatment response and should not be interpreted as a direct measure of clinical benefit. Future research should combine repeated measures of engagement with information about treatment availability, reasons for missed sessions, and outcomes more directly targeted by DBT (e.g., institutional infractions, acute crises). More broadly, these findings support treating engagement as a dynamic treatment process that can inform program delivery, while recognizing that understanding why participation changes requires information beyond attendance and facilitator ratings alone.
