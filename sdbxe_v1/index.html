Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download information from other preregistration sites, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). Statcheck might still miss one-sided tests that are described in another sentence, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on 226 Psychological Science papers with 5,019 hand-coded p-values, 438 of them imprecise (scienceverse/validation, branch stat_p_exact). Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). Of the misses, 13 are starred table notes (“*p < .05”) that the module leaves out on purpose and 11 are p-values it did not find; of the wrong reports, 20 are p-values the ground truth does not contain (such as “ps < .05”) and 6 use ≤ or ≥. These figures measure agreement with the hand-coding, which has known errors in both directions (scienceverse/validation#48).

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. A TRO uses them for each trov:TransparentResearchPerformance. * run_results$exit_status: the exit status of the process that ran the script (0 = ended normally); NA when the process did not report one (a time limit, or Docker failing to start the container). * artifacts: one row per file, with stage (deposit: as downloaded from the repository, hashed from the downloaded file; before_run: the run folder just before the first script ran, after metacheck’s own changes, such as rewritten paths and unpacked archives; after_run: only the files the scripts made (status new), changed (changed) or removed (removed), as an unchanged file is the one listed before the run), path (relative to the run folder, as valid UTF-8, a byte of another encoding written as <xx>), size (bytes), sha256, mime_type (by extension, NA when unknown), source (the file’s address in the repository, for deposit). The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). Files metacheck itself copied or made between passes (see modifications, output_copied, file_placed) show in after_run too. A TRO uses the rows as trov:ResearchArtifacts and each stage as a trov:ArtifactArrangement. * modifications$sha256_before, modifications$sha256_after: the hash of the changed script as downloaded and as it ran; NA when the script is not at the same path in both (a script made from an R Markdown file). * environment: the system that ran the code (trov:TransparentResearchSystem in a TRO): metacheck_version, started_at, ended_at, host (R version, platform, operating system), sandbox, network_isolated (TRUE only when every Docker run had --network none; the process sandbox does not stop the network), internet, timeout_per_script, total_timeout, python_local (the Python run outside Docker and its version), and docker: runs (for each kind of run, r_run, r_install, python_run, python_install, the image and the docker run arguments, such as --network none, --read-only, --cap-drop ALL, no-new-privileges, the process limit and the memory and processor limits) and images (for each image, its ID and digests from docker image inspect, its creation time and the R and Python versions its environment declares).

It is NA for files run inside another script, files that are not an analysis, files not read, and a Docker failure. summary_table counts each group (repro_ran_n, repro_code_error_n, repro_dependency_n, repro_time_n, repro_not_checkable_n), and the report’s first sentence gives them. Abstract

Phonological contrasts in speech are conveyed through multiple dimensions. Listeners may systematically adjust their cue weightings according to the context, such as under challenging listening conditions. However, individual listeners may also assign different perceptual weights to each dimension, giving rise to substantial individual differences.

The present study examines how native listeners of Dutch weight an acoustic cue (fundamental frequency, f0) and two visual cues (beat gestures and lip movement) when perceiving lexical stress under different degrees of acoustic degradation induced by 16talker babble noise. At the group level, f0 and beat gestures were robust cues to lexical stress, while the lip-movement effect was less consistent across conditions. Listeners systematically reduced their reliance on f0 as the noise became more severe, without a comparable increase in their reliance on either visual cue. At the individual level, listeners who relied more heavily on f0 in favorable listening conditions tended to show greater reductions in its absolute weighting under severe noise, with no evidence for a crosscondition trade-off between f0 and beat-gesture weighting; individual differences in relative cue weighting remained relatively stable across listening conditions. Taken together, these findings showcase both context-dependent adaptive flexibility and individual-specific stability in audiovisual cue weighting. More broadly, our findings suggest that dimension-selective attention principles may extend to cue-specific adjustments across sensory modalities under acoustic degradation. Specifically, downweighting of a degraded acoustic cue does not necessarily entail compensatory upweighting of visual cues, even though they remain physically intact.

1. Introduction

A major challenge for listeners in speech perception is to extract acoustic features from an unfolding speech signal and map them onto abstract phonological categories. Through this process, physical properties of the speech signal are transformed into meaningful linguistic representations. Crucially, this mapping does not typically depend on a single acoustic dimension. Instead, it simultaneously draws on multiple dimensions, or cues, whose contributions may vary across listeners and listening conditions. Previous studies on perceptual cue weighting have largely focused on acoustic information. However, the multimodal nature of speech perception makes visual cues a particularly useful test case for investigating cue weighting, as they remain physically unaffected under conditions of acoustic degradation. The present study therefore examines the weighting of one auditory and two visual cues in the perception of Dutch lexical stress, quantifying how strong an influence each dimension has on determining the perceived stress pattern. We aim to bring together two complementary aspects of cue use: how listeners adapt their cue weights to background babble noise and the extent to which individual listeners retain their own cue-weighting patterns across listening conditions.

1.1. Multidimensionality and cue redundancy in speech

A single phonological contrast is typically manifested across multiple acoustic dimensions in speech. Even a seemingly simple segmental contrast such as voicing (e.g., the /b/-/p/ contrast between rabid and rapid in English) has been associated with as many as 16 acoustic cues (Lisker, 1986), including voice onset time (VOT) and fundamental frequency (f0) at the onset of the following vowel. Likewise, the acoustic realization of suprasegmental contrasts typically involves several dimensions. In tonal languages such as Mandarin Chinese and Vietnamese, f0 is a prominent acoustic correlate of lexical tones.

However, other dimensions, such as voice quality and duration, also play a role. For certain tonal contrasts, especially those with similar f0 contours, these other dimensions become more informative than f0 (e.g., Andruski, 2006; Brunelle, 2009; Ye & Boersma, 2024; Yu & Lam, 2014).

The multidimensional nature of phonetic realization also extends to lexical stress.

Generally speaking, stressed syllables are often associated with higher mean f0 and greater f0 variation, longer duration, higher intensity, shallower spectral tilt for vowels and less vowel reduction, as compared with unstressed syllables in the same word (e.g., Kohler, 2012; Mousikou et al., 2024; Severijnen et al., 2024). However, the acoustic realization of lexical stress and the relative importance of each acoustic correlate vary across languages. For instance, unstressed syllables are often accompanied by systematic vowel reduction in English, rendering vowel quality a distinctive acoustic correlate of lexical stress (e.g., Y. Zhang et al., 2008). In contrast, lexical stress in Dutch is primarily realized through differences in mean f0, intensity and duration, while vowel quality is only a subordinate correlate (Severijnen et al., 2024). Consistent with these crosslinguistic differences, native Dutch listeners rely less on vowel quality and more on f0 than native English listeners when perceiving stress in English (Tremblay et al., 2021).

Such differences may thus pose challenges for nonnative listeners when identifying relevant acoustic dimensions and approximating nativelike perceptual weighting (Tremblay et al., 2026).

In audiovisual contexts, listeners may draw on articulatory movements of the lips (Bujok et al., 2025) and rhythmic co-speech hand gestures (i.e., beat gestures) when perceiving stress in Dutch. Often temporally aligned with prosodic prominence in speech (e.g., Leonard & Cummins, 2011; McClave, 1994), beat gestures can bias perception of lexical stress (Bosker & Peeters, 2021; Bujok et al., 2025; Ye et al., 2025): Even with identical auditory input, listeners are more likely to perceive stress on the syllable with which a beat gesture is aligned. Thus, information about phonological categorization may be available not only across multiple acoustic dimensions but also across sensory modalities.

Such multidimensionality in speech provides listeners with multiple sources of information for phonological categorization. Even when some dimensions are not sufficiently informative, a phonological contrast may still be conveyed through other dimensions. Cue redundancy may thus afford listeners the flexibility to accommodate variability in speech, which arises not only across languages but also among speakers of the same language. Although f0, duration and intensity collectively serve as main acoustic correlates of lexical stress in Dutch, individual speakers may employ different combinations of these cues and differ in their relative use (Severijnen et al., 2024). Note, however, that individual cue-weighting patterns in perception do not necessarily mirror cue use in production (Schertz et al., 2015); an individual may prioritize one cue in production but another in perception for the same contrast. Nevertheless, variability in acoustic realization means that listeners may encounter different distributions of acoustic cues even when perceiving the same contrast.

1.2. Context dependence and individual stability in cue weighting

Faced with such variability in the available information, listeners may assign different perceptual weights to different dimensions, hence a stronger influence of some cues on categorization than others. Importantly, perceptual cue weights may vary with available acoustic information in the speech input. Examining lexical-stress identification in native listeners of Southern British English, Mousikou et al. (2024) found that f0, duration and intensity all contributed to stress perception in English, whereas their relative weighting depended on the value of the other cues within a given word. At the group level, f0 and duration carried greater weight in words with little intensity difference between stressed and unstressed syllables than in words where all three cues robustly signaled stress.

Listeners can also learn to adjust their cue weighting following exposure to talker-specific distributions of f0 and intensity cues to lexical stress (Severijnen et al., 2023). Cueweighting adjustments can occur rapidly : Symons, Jasmin and Tierney (2026) demonstrated that listeners adjusted their weights for cues to phrase boundary placement upon encountering a single stimulus with an unexpected relationship between acoustic cues, although these adjustments also faded away rapidly.

Beyond acoustic cues in the speech input, task demands and listening conditions can also affect the information available to listeners and how they process it. Cue weighting can change systematically when listeners perform a distracting concurrent task (Gordon et al., 1993; Kong & Lee, 2018) or perceive speech in background noise (Mattys et al., 2009; Symons et al., 2024; W. Zhang & Steffman, 2025). When perceiving verbal foci (i.e., prosodic prominence at the sentence level), English listeners appear to rely mainly on f0 in quiet but more strongly on duration in noise (Symons et al., 2024). In summary, listeners may adjust their reliance on individual cues according to their distribution, availability and usefulness in the current speech input.

The perceptual contribution of visual cues likewise demonstrates such context dependence. Although articulatory movements of the lips can contribute to stress and tone perception, their effect appears to surface only under certain conditions, such as with limited acoustic information and with nonnative listeners (Bujok et al., 2025; Han et al., 2020). Lip movement was a reliable visual cue to Dutch word stress in a visual-only condition with muted videos, but not when acoustic information was available in an audiovisual setting (Bujok et al., 2025), highlighting the context-dependent weighting of this visual cue. When perceiving lexical tones in Mandarin Chinese, only tone-naïve nonnative speakers marginally benefited from this visual information, whereas native listeners appeared relatively insusceptible to it (Han et al., 2020). Beat gestures appear to be a more robust visual cue to lexical stress than lip movement. Their estimated effect size was much larger than that of lip movement under the same conditions, as reported by Bujok et al. (2025); nevertheless, their perceptual contribution is not entirely independent of the accompanying auditory information: the influence of beat gestures on stress perception becomes stronger when acoustic cues to lexical stress are less informative. Maran et al. (2025) further suggested that the beat-gesture effect may be moderated by the stress pattern of the audio stimuli as well as the degradation of f0 through vocoding. In a related vein, context-dependent effects have also been reported for iconic gestures in speech comprehension. Drijvers and Özyürek (2017, 2020) found that their visual benefits varied according to the severity of acoustic degradation and between native and nonnative listeners, while Saryazdi and Chambers (2022) reported stronger gestural benefits in quiet than in noise.

At the same time, individuals have their own preferred cue-use patterns, which may differ substantially between one listener and another. For instance, when presented with the same acoustic information for identifying lexical stress in a given word, some English listeners may rely more heavily on f0, whereas others may prioritize duration over f0 (Mousikou et al., 2024). These perceptual preferences may remain relatively stable across different situations. Individual differences in cue weighting have been shown to persist across repeated testing sessions (Schertz et al., 2015), even over periods of two months (Idemaru et al., 2012), across different perceptual tasks (Symons, Kachlicka, et al., 2026), and across different listening conditions (Symons et al., 2024).

These individual perceptual tendencies may also shape how listeners learn to distinguish new phonological contrasts. For instance, native English listeners who placed greater perceptual weight on pitch direction (i.e., f0 slopes) were more successful in learning to identify nonce words with superimposed pitch patterns resembling lexical tones in Mandarin Chinese than those who placed less weight on this dimension (Chandrasekaran et al., 2010).

Nonetheless, individual cue-use patterns are not necessarily immutable. Sustained exposure to a second language (L2) has been shown to induce systematic changes in cue weighting that follow developmental trajectories specific to individual learners and phonological contrasts, rather than merely reflecting random fluctuations or measurement noise (Kim et al., 2018). Conversely, improvement in L2 speech categorization has also been observed without changes in the weighting of relevant dimensions (Ingvalson et al., 2011; Iverson & Evans, 2009). Thus, individual-specific cue-weighting patterns appear to reflect relatively persistent perceptual strategies that can nevertheless be modified through experience. Such perceptual preferences may, in turn, shape and potentially constrain how individual listeners adapt to changes in the speech input.

1.3. Cue weighting within the dimension-selective attention framework

Although adaptive flexibility and individual stability may appear to represent opposing properties of perceptual cue weighting, the two are not irreconcilable. Listeners may adjust the weight assigned to each cue according to the information available in the speech input, while still retaining characteristic perceptual preferences relative to other listeners.

What mechanism, then, might underlie both the context-dependent adjustment of perceptual cue weights and the persistence of individual-specific weighting strategies?

One possibility lies in how listeners allocate attention across dimensions of the speech signal as sources of information.

According to the dimension-selective attention account, individuals selectively allocate attentional resources to the features or dimensions that are the most useful or relevant for recovering category distinctions from the input, while directing less attention to other less useful or relevant features (Francis & Nusbaum, 2002; Holt et al., 2018).

Dimensions receiving more attention may therefore exert greater influence on categorization and be weighted more heavily than dimensions receiving less attention.

Such attentional priorities may be shaped over time by listeners' linguistic experience including the cue ecology of their first languages (L1s), giving rise to relatively durable biases toward particular dimensions (Tremblay et al., 2026). From this perspective, crosslanguage differences in the perceptual weighting of cues to a nonnative phonological contrast may reflect experience-dependent differences in how attention is allocated across dimensions. Indeed, listeners tend to rely more heavily on dimensions that serve as important cues in their L1s when perceiving a nonnative contrast (e.g., Choi et al., 2019; Tremblay et al., 2021 Tremblay et al., , 2026;; Y. Zhang et al., 2008). Beyond such cross-language differences, listeners from a relatively homogenous group may also diverge in their cueweighting preferences: some rely heavily on a single primary cue, whereas others integrate information more evenly across multiple cues. Recent evidence links this variation to the ability to direct attention selectively to a specific dimension, with stronger selective attention associated with greater reliance on a single primary cue (Symons, Kachlicka, et al., 2026).

Under this account, cue weighting may be adjusted as the relative usefulness or accessibility of dimensions changes in the current input, through shifts in selective attention toward or away from certain dimensions. One circumstance that may alter this attentional prioritization is increased cognitive demand. When attentional resources are limited, listeners may find it more difficult to sustain selective focus on a single primary cue, resulting in a less strongly differentiated weighting of available dimensions.

Consistent with this prediction, previous studies have demonstrated reduced primary-cue weighting under conditions with high cognitive load (Gordon et al., 1993; Kong & Lee, 2018; W. Zhang & Steffman, 2025). However, the specific pattern of cue reweighting varies across studies and phonological contrasts. As primary-cue weight decreases, reliance on secondary cues may increase (Gordon et al., 1993), remain relatively stable (Kong & Lee, 2018) or even decrease in tandem (W. Zhang & Steffman, 2025). Thus, although increased cognitive demands can alter established cue-weighting strategies, there is no uniform redistribution of perceptual weight across cues. Together, cue weighting may reflect both relatively durable, learned attentional priorities and the capacity to adjust these priorities in response to the current input.

The selective attention framework discussed thus far has primarily concerned the weighting of different acoustic dimensions. However, dimensions relevant for speech perception are not confined to the auditory modality. As mentioned above, visual information from a speaker's lip and hand movements can also play a part in speech perception.

Although not yet applied to multimodal perception, it is plausible that selective attention may operate across dimensions distributed over sensory modalities.

Extending the selective attention framework to multimodal speech, therefore, we suggest that the perceptual weight assigned to auditory and visual cues may depend on how informative or accessible each source of information is.

Adverse listening conditions provide a particularly informative context to explore this hypothesis. In the presence of background noise, the accessibility and usefulness of acoustic information decreases as the acoustic signal deteriorates, while visual information remains physically intact. Previous studies showed that visual information can facilitate speech perception under such circumstances (e.g., Sumby & Pollack, 1954; Ross et al., 2006); this visual enhancement often becomes greater as auditory information becomes less accessible, known as inverse effectiveness (Van De Rijt et al., 2019). More recently, Bujok et al. (2026) demonstrated that beat gestures can facilitate lexical access in quiet, while an even stronger facilitatory effect was observed in speech-shaped noise (Maran et al., 2026). Stacey et al. (2020) further suggested that listeners rely less on visual information when it is degraded through blurring. These findings indicate that listeners adjust their relative reliance on auditory and visual information according to the availability and reliability of information across modalities. However, this literature largely concerned the relative dominance of auditory and visual modalities in determining categorical perceptual outcomes, rather than how the weights assigned to specific cues distributed across modalities change with listening conditions. A shift toward greater visual dominance, for example, could arise from down-weighting auditory cues, upweighting visual cues, or concurrent changes in both. It therefore remains unclear whether changes in the relative availability of auditory and visual information give rise to crossmodal cue reweighting of both auditory and visual cues and, if so, what form such reweighting takes.

1.4. The present study

To probe this question, we examined the weighting of an acoustic cue (f0) and two visual cues (beat gestures and lip movement) to Dutch lexical stress perception under different levels of 16-talker babble noise. Multi-talker babble (MTB) can interfere with speech perception through two masking effects. On the one hand, energetic masking arises from spectro-temporal overlap between the target and masker, which diminishes the spectral integrity of acoustic signals and thus impairs lexical access (Mattys et al., 2009). On the other hand, informational masking places additional cognitive demands on listeners to segregate and selectively attend to the target talker. With 16-talker babble, however, access to information from competing talkers is limited, as background talkers become increasingly unintelligible as the number of competing talkers increases (Freyman et al., 2004; Mattys et al., 2009). Babble containing a similarly large number of talkers has been shown to approach the perceptual characteristics of speech-shaped noise, where energetic masking plays the dominant role (Wang & Xu, 2020).

Specifically, we assessed the weighting of these three cues (f0, beat gestures and lip movement) through a lexical-stress categorization task. In this two-alternative forcedchoice (2AFC) task, listeners were presented with minimal stress pairs in Dutch (e.g., CONtent "things inside a container" vs. conTENT "satisfied"), where the three perceptual cues were orthogonally manipulated through audio-and video-editing. These audiovisual speech stimuli were presented under five noise conditions: no noise (quiet) and speechto-noise ratios (SNRs) of 0 (mild), -6, -12 and -18 (severe) dB. This design allowed us to assess the asymmetric effects of noise on auditory and visual information: 16-talker babble progressively degraded access to the acoustic signal as SNR decreased, while leaving the visual information physically intact.

At the group level, we examined context dependence in cue weighting and asked two questions: (1) Whether decreasing SNRs would reduce listeners' reliance on f0, a primary acoustic cue to lexical stress in Dutch, and (2) How the weighting of the two visual cues would change alongside any such reduction. From a selective attention perspective, reduced accessibility and usefulness of f0 in noise should lead listeners to direct less attention to this acoustic dimension and hence to down-weight it. However, whether listeners would direct more attention toward the two visual cues, such that degradation of an acoustic cue consequently leads to visual-cue up-weighting, remains unknown. It may depend on the usefulness and reliability of the information that beat gestures and lip movement provide for resolving lexical stress. The previous unimodal cue-weighting literature provides no uniform prediction for how secondary cues respond to MTB (Symons et al., 2024; W. Zhang & Steffman, 2025). Thus, how the two visual cues would respond to noise was an open question.

At the individual level, we further investigated listener-specific cue-weighting strategies across favorable and severely degraded listening conditions. We conducted two sets of correlational analyses, focusing on individual listeners' absolute and relative cue weighting, respectively. Absolute weights reflect the extent to which a cue influences a listener's stress pattern identification, while relative weights reflect its perceptual importance compared with other cues. The first set of correlations tested whether changes in absolute cue weights in response to challenging listening conditions were associated with their corresponding weights under favorable conditions, and whether changes in acoustic-and visual-cue weights were related, potentially indicating compensatory adjustments. The second set evaluated the stability of listeners' relative cue-weighting preferences across conditions. If individual cue-use patterns reflect relatively stable attentional priorities, listeners who rely relatively more strongly on a particular cue under favorable conditions may continue to do so under adverse conditions, despite changes in absolute cue weights. Together, these analyses allowed us to examine how adaptive changes in cue weighting coexist with relatively stable, listener-specific perceptual strategies.

2. Methods

>Methods

2.1. Participants

We recruited 64 native listeners of Dutch (48 female and 16 male; age range: 17-56 years, M = 24.1 years, SD = 8.7 years) for this 2AFC stress-pattern identification task from the Radboud University participant pool, following pre-registration (see Data availability).

All 64 participants self-reported to have normal hearing, (corrected-to-)normal vision and no (known) language deficits. Participation was on a voluntary basis and was compensated with either course credits or monetary rewards. Informed consent was collected prior to the task. This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Faculty of Social Sciences at Radboud University (project code: ECSW-LT-2026-3-25-35096).

None of the 64 participants met the three exclusion criteria, which included participant withdrawal, 30 consecutive trials without a response or with the same stress response, or the same stress response on at least 85% of all trials. Therefore, data from all participants were included in the analyses.

2.2. Stimuli

Three disyllabic minimal pairs of lexical stress in Dutch were used as stimuli in this study:

CONtent-conTENT, SERvisch-serVIES (Serbian; crockery set) and VOORruit-voorUIT (windshield; forward). The first word in each pair had stress on the first syllable, while the second word had stress on the second syllable.

2.2.1. Stimuli in quiet

Video stimuli and audio stimuli in quiet were adapted from Bujok et al. (2025). In their video stimuli, recorded with a frame rate of 50 fps, the movement of a male native speaker's upper body could be seen producing these words in isolation while making a simple beat gesture with his right hand. There were thus both lip movements as a visual articulatory cue and beat gestures as a prosodic timing cue. Videos were trimmed, with 750-ms silent intervals preceding and following target words. Beat apices (i.e., maximum extension of the right hand) were aligned with the vowel onset of either the first or the second syllable.

Through editing the face and the body separately in the videos, all possible combinations of the two visual cues had been realized in order to reach a full-factorial design. That included mismatch between beat gestures and lip movement. To minimize unnaturalness in these incongruent combinations, Bujok et al., (2025) combined the speaker's head and neck in videos producing one word in a pair (e.g., CONtent) with his body in videos producing the other word in the pair (e.g., conTENT) and adjusted misalignment in the composite videos frame by frame. Together with the congruent combinations, there were 12 video combinations (2 beat gesture timings × 2 lip movements × 3 word pairs). See Bujok et al. (2025) for further details.

Manipulation of audio in quiet had also been completed by Bujok et al. (2025).

Among the three main acoustic cues to lexical stress in Dutch, they eliminated the duration and intensity cues while retaining the f0 cue. Differences in duration and intensity between the same syllable (e.g., con-in the CONtent-conTENT pair) being stressed (as in CONtent) and unstressed (as in conTENT) in each minimal pair were eliminated by taking their average. This generated two anchor stimuli per pair that differed only in f0: one indicating initial stress and the other final stress. Five equallyspaced f0 contours were then interpolated between the two anchors (A1 for initial stress and A7 for final stress), with duration and intensity controlled in 10-ms time bins, generating a seven-step f0 continuum for each word pair. In the present experiment, we selected the two anchors as well as three intermediate steps (A3, A4 and A5) from each continuum as our f0 cues, yielding five unique audio stimuli for each of the 12 video stimuli. The baseline quiet condition thus comprised 60 audiovisual stimuli, covering all possible combinations among f0, beat gestures and lip movement across three minimal pairs.

2.2.2. Stimuli in babble noise

We then mixed all 60 mono audio stimuli with 16-talker babble noise in Praat (Boersma & Weenink, 2024) using a custom script to create stimuli under different noise levels. In order to reach the desired SNRs, we rescaled the intensity of all audio stimuli with target speech to 55 dB SPL before mixing them with noise. MTB files were generated by randomly mixing 16 unique pieces of speech materials that were at least 2500-ms long and produced in quiet by 16 female native speakers of Dutch (Bosker & Cooke, 2020).

An MTB file was randomly matched with a video stimulus. We trimmed the MTB file to match the duration of the speech from the trimmed video and smoothed its onset and offset with 400-ms fade-in and -out ramps to avoid abrupt acoustic edges. To obtain the four desired SNRs, the noise signal was scaled to a mean of 55, 61, 67 and 73 dB SPL respectively. One audio stimulus with rescaled intensity of 55 dB was mixed with the MTB file at all four SNR levels (0, -6, -12 or -18 dB) using the Combine to stereo function in Praat and then converted to mono. The mono sound files were dubbed onto corresponding videos, yielding 300 unique audiovisual stimuli.

2.3. Procedure

The experiment was run on PsychoPy® version 2024.2.4 (Peirce et al., 2019) in a soundattenuating cubicle at Donders Centre for Cognition, Radboud University. Video was presented at the center of a 24.5" screen at a refresh rate of 120 Hz. Audio was presented through headphones at a volume constant across all participants.

After reading the instructions written in Dutch, participants were familiarized with the settings of the two alternative forced-choice (2AFC) word recognition task through two practice trials under the quiet condition. These two trials consisted of another minimal stress pair in Dutch produced by the same speaker, one with all three cues to stress on the first syllable and the other with all cues to stress on the second. Then the experiment including 300 unique trials started.

All 300 test trials were pseudo-randomized across five blocks of 60 trials so that within-block variation was maximal and similar among the blocks. The order of blocks and trials within each block was fully randomized. In each trial, participants first saw a fixation cross at the screen center and two words from one minimal pair on its two sides, with the initial-stressed word always on the left, and the final-stressed one on the right.

Participants needed to click on the area around the fixation cross to watch the video. After video offset, the two words appeared again at the same locations. Participants clicked on the word they believed the speaker had produced. A trial lasted until a response was made or 3.5 seconds had elapsed. The entire experiment lasted approximately 40 minutes.

3.1. Group-level analyses

We ran three generalized linear mixed-effects regression (glmer) models with a binomial error distribution on the binary responses to investigate the group-level cue weighting among f0, beat gestures and lip movement in R 4.4.0 (R Core Team, 2024). As preregistered, the first model compared responses in the baseline quiet condition with all responses in any of the noise conditions; the second further examined whether the usage of all three stress cues in the noise conditions (i.e., excluding quiet) was modulated by SNRs in a linear pattern. In addition, we ran an exploratory analysis by comparing responses in quiet with responses at each of the four SNRs.

The three perceptual cues f0 (numeric, A1 to A7 centered and rescaled within ±0.5, with A1 the anchor point of initial stress being -0.5 and A7 the anchor point of final stress being 0.5), beat gestures (binary, beat apex on the first syllable = -0.5, on the second syllable = 0.5) and lip movement (binary, lip movement for producing words with stress on the first syllable = -0.5, on the second syllable = 0.5) were included in all three models as predictors. The models reported here included the maximal random-effects structures that permitted convergence.

3.1.1. Noise vs. quiet

To examine whether MTB influences the weighting of the three perceptual cues, the first model compared all responses in any of the trials with MTB, collapsed across all SNRs, with those in quiet. This model included the fixed effect of noise (binary, with noise coded as 0.5 and quiet as -0.5) and the three cues, along with two-way interactions between noise and each cue. Noise was coded such that the expected down-weighting of f0 in noise would be reflected in a negative interaction coefficient. There were also by-participant random intercepts and random slopes for f0 and beat gestures. This model revealed three main effects. The first was an effect of f0. Stimuli with A7, the strongest f0 cue to stress on the second syllable, were on average 35.43 times more likely (odds ratio, obtained by exponentiating the estimate 3.57) to be perceived as final-stressed words than stimuli with A1, the strongest f0 cue to stress on the first syllable.

The 95% CI for the odds ratio ranged from 25.05 to 50.12 (SE = 0.18, z = 20.16, p < .001).

The second main effect involved beat gestures. Stimuli with a beat gesture falling on the second syllable vowel onset were on average 2.93 times more likely to be perceived as final-stressed words than stimuli with a beat gesture on the first syllable. The 95% CI for the odds ratio was between 2.39 and 3.61 (β = 1.08, SE = 0.11, z = 10.18, p < .001). The last main effect was one of noise. That is, listeners were more likely to perceive finalstressed words in noise than under the baseline quiet condition; the 95% CI for the odds ratio was between 1.13 and 1.34 (β = 0.21, SE = 0.04, z = 4.74, p < .001). Lip movement (β = 0.06, SE = 0.04, z = 1.40, p = .161) was not significant. This model also revealed the expected negative interaction between f0 and noise.

The effect of f0 on stress perception was on average 4.25 times greater in quiet (coded as -0.5) than in noise. The 95% CI for the odds ratio ranged from 3.13 to 5.78 (β = -1.45, SE = 0.16, z = -9.27, p < .001). Despite not finding a main effect of lip movement, this model detected its interaction with noise. The effect of lip movement was on average 1.20 times greater in noise than in quiet. The 95% CI for the odds ratio ranged from 1.01 to 1.42 (β = 0.18, SE = 0.09, z = 2.05, p = .040). However, as the random-effects structure did not include a by-participant random slope for this interaction, this result should be interpreted with caution. The interaction between beat gestures and noise, despite its positive point estimate (β = 0.13), did not reach significance (SE = 0.09, z = 1.43, p = .154).

3.1.2. Linear modulation of SNR

Excluding data from the quiet condition, the second model tested whether the weights of all three cues were modulated by SNR in a linear pattern. This model included fixed effects of f0, beat gestures, lip movement and centered SNR (numeric, scaled between -0.5 for SNR = 0 dB, the mildest noise level, and 0.5 for SNR = -18 dB, the most severe noise level). Apart from the four predictors, there were two-and three-way interactions among f0, beat gestures and centered SNR as well as a two-way interaction between lip movement and centered SNR. In terms of random effects, there were by-participant random intercepts and random slopes for f0, beat gestures and lip movement.

With responses across all four SNRs pooled together, this model revealed main effects of all three stress cues, suggesting that all these three cues were in active use while identifying stress patterns in noise. Stimuli with f0-step A7 were on average 18.83 times more likely to be perceived as final-stressed words than stimuli with f0-step A1. The 95% CI for the odds ratio ranged from 13.68 to 25.93 (β = 2.94, SE = 0.16, z = 18.00, p < .001).

Following f0, in terms of effect size, was the beat-gesture cue. Stimuli with a beat gesture indicating final stress were on average 3.15 times more likely to be perceived as finalstressed words than stimuli with a beat gesture indicating initial stress. The 95% CI for the odds ratio was between 2.55 and 3.88 (β = 1.15, SE = 0.11, z = 10.76, p < .001).

Despite a smaller effect size compared with f0 and beat gestures, the lip-movement cue also reached significance. Stimuli with lip movement producing a final-stressed word were on average 1.16 times more likely to be perceived as final-stressed words than stimuli with lip movement producing an initial-stressed word. The 95% CI for the odds ratio ran from 1.08 to 1.25 (β = 0.15, SE = 0.04, z = 3.94, p < .001).

Apart from the three main effects, there was also a two-way interaction between f0 and SNR. The f0 effect was on average 21.66 times greater at SNR = 0 dB with mild noise than at SNR = -18 dB with severe noise. The 95% CI for the odds ratio ranged from 15.37 to 30.54 (β = -3.08, SE = 0.18, z = -17.56, p < .001). Nevertheless, the other two two-way interactions did not reach significance (beat gestures × SNR: β = 0.11, SE = 0.10, z = 1.05, p = .294; lip movement × SNR: β = 0.16, SE = 0.10, z = 1.55, p = .121), even though their coefficients were opposite in direction to the interaction between f0 and SNR.

3.1.3. Exploratory comparison between quiet and each SNR level

Including all responses, the third model compared stimuli in quiet with stimuli at each SNR. Identical to the two pre-registered models, this model included all three cues as predictors. SNR was treated as a categorical variable with five levels, with quiet being the baseline. There were two-way interactions between SNR and each of the three cues.

In addition, there were by-participant random intercepts and random slopes for f0 and beat gestures. This model showed significant simple effects of f0 and beat gestures, but not of lip movement, in the quiet condition (the reference level for SNR). Stimuli with A7 were on average 75.02 times more likely to be perceived as final-stressed words than stimuli with A1. The 95% CI for the odds ratio for the f0 cue ranged from 48.84 to 115.25 (β = 4.32, SE = 0.22, z = 19.72, p < .001). Regarding beat gestures, stimuli with a beat gesture falling on the second syllable were on average 2.76 times more likely to be perceived as final-stressed words than those with a beat on the first syllable. The 95% CI for the odds ratio ran between 2.15 and 3.54 (β = 1.02, SE = 0.13, z = 8.00, p < .001).

There were also four simple effects of SNR. That is, compared with stimuli in the baseline quiet condition, stimuli with noise were generally more likely to be perceived as having stress on the second syllable. Specifically, the 95% CI for the odds ratio was between 1.12 and 1.40 for the contrast between SNR = 0 dB and quiet (β = 0.23, SE = 0.06, z = 4.04, p < .001), between 1.07 and 1.33 for the contrast between SNR = -6 dB and quiet (β = 0.18, SE = 0.06, z = 3.14, p = .002), between 1.03 and 1.28 for the contrast between SNR = -12 dB and quiet (β = 0.14, SE = 0.05, z = 2.57, p = .010), and between 1.22 and 1.50 for the contrast between SNR = -18 dB and quiet (β = 0.30, SE = 0.05, z = 5.61, p < .001).

We found interactions between each of the three cues and SNR. The f0 effect was on average 4.47 and 24.10 times smaller at SNR = -12 and -18 dB respectively, when compared with the baseline quiet condition. The 95% CI for the odds ratio was [3.08, 6.47] for the contrast between SNR = -12 dB and quiet (β = -1.50, SE = 0.19, z = -7.91, p < .001) and [16.85, 34.47] for the contrast between SNR = -18 dB and quiet (β = -3.18, SE = 0.18, z = -17.43, p < .001). Thus, listeners rely less on f0 in severe noise than in quiet.

The effect of beat gestures on stress pattern identification was on average 1.25 times greater at SNR = -18 dB than in the baseline quiet condition. The 95% CI was [1.01, 1.55] (β = 0.23, SE = 0.11, z = 2.07, p = .038). Similarly, the effect of lip movement was on average 1.25 and 1.29 times greater at SNR = -12 and -18 dB respectively, compared with the quiet condition. The 95% CI was [1.01, 1.54] for the contrast between SNR = -12 dB and quiet (β = 0.22, SE = 0.11, z = 2.01, p = .044) and [1.05, 1.60] for contrast between SNR = -18 dB and quiet (β = 0.26, SE = 0.11, z = 2.39, p = .017). It thus appears that listeners rely more on the two visual cues in severe noise than in quiet. However, as random slopes for the interactions between either of the two visual cues and SNR were omitted to achieve model convergence, these three interactions should be interpreted with caution, given that the lower bounds of their 95% CIs for odds ratios were close to 1.

3.1.4. Summary

Taken together, group-level analyses (see Table 1) provided strong evidence for f0 and beat gestures as perceptual cues to lexical stress in Dutch, whereas the contribution of lip movement appears to be context-dependent, as it only reached statistical significance (α = .05) when responses in the quiet condition were excluded. Moreover, all three models provided consistent evidence for the down-weighting of f0 under MTB. In contrast, evidence for increased reliance on the two visual cues in noise only surfaced in the first and third models with relatively small effect sizes, suggesting that these findings were sensitive to model specification and should be interpreted with caution.

3.2. Individual-level analyses

We then compared individual cue weights under relatively favorable listening conditions (intelligible speech; quiet and SNR = 0 or -6 dB) with those under severe noise (unintelligible speech; SNR = -12 or -18 dB) to investigate individual differences in cue usage across listening conditions. This pre-registered two-way separation was based on an audio-only pilot on lexical-stress categorization, where overall accuracy was above 0.90 for quiet and for SNRs of 0 and -6 dB but below 0.85 for SNRs of -12 and -18 dB.

This division was further supported by studies examining speech perception accuracy in noise (Sumby & Pollack, 1954; Wang & Xu, 2020). For each participant, two binomial generalized linear models (glms) with the three perceptual cues as predictors were fitted separately to responses in intelligible and unintelligible speech. Regression coefficient estimates for each cue were extracted as participant-specific cue-weighting measures. A higher coefficient for a cue indicates a greater difference in the listener's categorization responses depending on whether the cue indicated initial or final stress, reflecting a stronger tendency to respond in accordance with that cue. Because the participant-specific coefficient estimates were not normally distributed, Spearman's rank correlations were used instead of Pearson's product-moment correlations.

We additionally conducted a post-hoc split-half reliability check (Tremblay et al., 2026) to assess the extent to which these participant-level coefficient estimates captured reliable between-participant differences in cue weighting rather than primarily reflecting Spearman-Brown corrected reliabilities were good for f0 in intelligible speech (.86) and for beat gestures in both intelligible (.77) and unintelligible (.75) speech, moderate for f0 in unintelligible speech (.57), and low for lip movement in both intelligible (-.06) and unintelligible (.15) speech. These results suggest that individual-level absolute cue weightings of f0 and beat gestures, indexed by their raw coefficients, reflected reasonably stable individual differences, supporting their use in subsequent correlational analyses. In contrast, the low reliability of lip movement under both listening conditions was consistent with its relatively weak contribution to stress perception at the group level. In particular, participant-specific lip-movement coefficients were close to zero for most listeners, and the group-level glmer model including responses in quiet provided no evidence for an overall lip-movement effect. Lip movement was therefore excluded from the following correlational analyses.

3.2.1. Absolute cue-weight (raw coefficients) shift

The first series of pre-registered Spearman's correlation tests was based on raw regression coefficient estimates and concerned absolute cue-weight shifts between intelligible and unintelligible speech. For f0, the shift was calculated by subtracting the raw coefficient in unintelligible speech from that in intelligible speech; for beat gestures, the shift was calculated in the opposite direction, so that a positive correlation between the two shifts would indicate a potential trade-off between these two cues. Positive values thus indicate f0 down-weighting or beat-gesture up-weighting in unintelligible speech, with larger absolute values reflecting greater magnitudes of cue-weight shifts.

The results suggested that absolute f0 weights in intelligible speech were strongly positively associated with their shift (ρ = .87, p < .001), indicating that participants with higher f0 weights under favorable listening conditions tended to show larger reductions in their use of f0 under severe noise. In contrast, the correlation between absolute beat-gesture weights in intelligible speech and its shift was weak and did not reach significance (ρ = -.23, p = .067). As shown in Figure 2b, 38 participants exhibited higher beat-gesture weights in unintelligible speech as compared with intelligible speech (points above y = 0), whereas the remaining 26 participants exhibited the opposite (points below y = 0). Thus, shifts in beat-gesture weights were not systematically related to their initial weights. There was also little association between shifts in f0 and beat-gesture weights (ρ = .13, p = .319, Figure 2c), hence no evidence for a within-listener trade-off between these two cues.

3.2.2. Relative cue-weight (normalized coefficients) stability

The second series of pre-registered tests aimed to examine the stability of participants' relative cue-weighting strategies across listening conditions. Following Holt and Lotto (2006) and Symons et al.'s (2024) approach, individual cue coefficients were normalized, such that the three normalized cue weights were nonnegative and summed to 1 under each listening condition. To obtain the relative weight for each cue, the absolute value of its coefficient was divided by the sum of the absolute coefficients for all three cues within each participant and listening condition. This normalization allowed us to assess whether listeners maintained similar relative cue-weighting profiles even when their absolute cue weights changed across listening conditions. To illustrate, if the absolute weights of all three cues decreased by 50% across conditions, their relative weights would remain unchanged.

Normalized f0 weights were strongly positively correlated between intelligible and unintelligible speech (ρ = .71, p < .001), indicating substantial cross-condition stability in relative f0 weights. 3.2.4. Summary

The individual-level correlational analyses revealed two main findings. First, greater absolute f0 weighting in intelligible speech was associated with a larger reduction in f0 weighting when shifting to unintelligible speech. That is, listeners who relied more on f0 in intelligible speech also reduced their f0 reliance to a greater extent. However, there was no corresponding pattern for beat gestures, nor evidence for a trade-off in absolute weighting between f0 and beat gestures. Second, there was clear consistency in relative cue weighting: listeners who weighted f0 or beat gestures more strongly relative to the other cues in intelligible speech also tended to weight that cue more strongly in unintelligible speech. Additionally, listeners with the same primary-cue transition pattern appeared to exhibit similar relative weighting tendencies, as reflected in their clustering within particular regions of the plots in Figure 3.

4. General discussion

The present study examined context-dependence and individual-stability in the weighting of auditory and visual cues to lexical stress perception in Dutch. We compared the usage of orthogonally manipulated f0, beat gestures and lip movement cues across different 16talker-babble-noise levels, ranging from no noise (quiet) to severe noise (SNR -18 dB).

Specifically, we built three glmer models targeting group-level context-dependence and conducted two sets of Spearman's correlational analyses targeting the individual-specific cue usage. Our results in general fit the selective attention account (Francis & Nusbaum, 2002; Holt et al., 2018) and extend this framework to cross-modality cue weighting in speech perception.

At the group level, we found main effects of f0 and beat gestures in all models, including and excluding responses in quiet, confirming that both cues contributed to stress perception across listening conditions. The substantially larger estimated effect of f0 as compared with that of beat gestures, together with their non-overlapping 95% CIs, suggested that the acoustic cue f0 plays a dominant role. The effect of lip movement, however, reached significance only in the second model (i.e., where responses in quiet were excluded). Note that the width of the 95% CI of the lip movement effect was the narrowest compared with the 95% CIs of the other two effects in all models. Therefore, null results of lip movement in the two models that included responses in quiet were probably the result of a small effect size rather than substantial uncertainty. The sensitivity of the lip-movement effect to the inclusion of responses to trials in quiet points towards its context-dependent role as a visual articulatory cue to lexical stress, echoing previous findings on its limited contribution to lexical-stress (Bujok et al., 2025) and lexical-tone perception (Han et al., 2020).

More direct evidence for context-dependent cue weighting came from interactions between the three cues and noise. The expected down-weighting of f0 with increasing noise was consistently supported across all three models. The first model revealed a significant interaction between f0 and noise, indicating a reduced f0 effect on stress identification in noise relative to in quiet. The second model further showed a significant linear interaction between f0 and SNR, with the f0 effect becoming more pronounced as SNR increased. In the third (exploratory) model comparing cue effects at each SNR with those under the baseline quiet condition, reductions in the effect of f0 reached significance at SNR = -12 dB and -18 dB, where the auditory signal was severely degraded. Thus, these findings provide robust evidence for noise-induced down-weighting of f0.

However, evidence for corresponding visual-cue up-weighting was less consistent.

The first model revealed an interaction between lip movement and noise (p = .040), indicating greater reliance on lip movement in noise than in quiet. Nonetheless, this model did not include random slopes for either factor. The second model revealed no significant linear interactions between SNR and either visual cue. Although the third model showed significantly greater effects of beat gestures at SNR = -18 dB and of lip movement at SNR = -12 dB and -18 dB in comparison with the baseline quiet condition, these three interactions had relatively small estimated effect sizes. Given the proximity of their lower bounds to the null value (i.e., odds ratio = 1) and the simplified random-effects structures required for model convergence, evidence for visual-cue up-weighting should be interpreted cautiously. Moreover, the 95% CIs for these interactions did not overlap with those reflecting f0 down-weighting at SNRs of -12 and -18 dB. Similarly, nonoverlapping 95% CIs for f0 down-weighting and the up-weighting of either visual cue were also observed in the other two models. These comparisons suggest substantially greater noise-induced modulation of f0 down-weighting than of visual-cue up-weighting.

Together, the group-level findings indicate that 16-talker babble noise primarily altered cue weighting through reducing listeners' reliance on f0, which was directly affected by acoustic degradation. This pattern is broadly compatible with the selective attention account of cue weighting change (Francis & Nusbaum, 2002; Holt et al., 2018), according to which listeners allocate greater perceptual weight to dimensions that are more useful for distinguishing phonological contrasts while reducing attention to dimensions whose utility is compromised. In the present study, the systematic reduction in f0 weighting suggests that listeners were sensitive to its reduced accessibility and adjusted their perceptual priorities accordingly.

Importantly, this adjustment was asymmetric: the robust down-weighting of the acoustic cue was not accompanied by comparably robust up-weighting of the two physically intact visual cues. Thus, it is plausible that attentional modulation may operate in a cue-specific manner, in the way that reduced availability or reliability of one dimension does not necessarily entail a simple compensatory redistribution of perceptual weights toward other dimensions. In the present study, the relatively stable weighting or modest up-weighting of beat gestures and lip movement may reflect their limited additional usefulness under adverse listening conditions, despite their physical availability. This postulation may also help explain the divergence in previous findings, in which secondary-cue weighting has been found to increase (Gordon et al., 1993; Symons et al., 2024), remain relatively stable (Kong & Lee, 2018), or decrease (W. Zhang & Steffman, 2025) under conditions taxing more attentional resources. These different patterns may reflect variation in the accessibility and reliability of specific cues across phonological contrasts and listening conditions. Beyond cue-specific properties, established individual preferences for particular cues may also constrain context-dependent adjustments, potentially limiting the extent to which listeners shift toward cues they relied on less under favorable listening conditions.

At the individual level, correlational analyses also revealed systematic differences in context-dependent weighting adjustments between f0 and beat gestures across listening conditions. Regarding absolute weights derived from raw coefficients, listeners who relied more strongly on f0, the primary cue among the three for most listeners in intelligible speech, tended to exhibit larger reductions in its weighting under severe noise.

However, no corresponding relationship was found for beat gestures, nor was there evidence for a trade-off in absolute weighting between these two cues. These findings are broadly in line with Kong and Lee (2018), who found that listeners relying more heavily on VOT, a primary cue to Korean laryngeal stop contrasts, exhibited greater reductions in its weighting under increased attentional demands. Both studies revealed a similar relationship between individual listeners' cue reliance under favorable listening conditions and its down-weighting under more challenging conditions, despite differences in the phonological contrasts examined and the sources of perceptual difficulty. Specifically, Kong and Lee (2018) examined changes in the weighting of VOT and f0 cues to Korean laryngeal stop contrasts under higher cognitive demands induced by a concurrent task, whereas our present work investigated auditory and visual cues to Dutch lexical stress contrasts under acoustic degradation induced by 16-talker babble noise. This convergence suggests that such context-dependent adjustments may arise not only under explicit attentional distraction but also through changes in the accessibility or reliability of a cue, supporting a broader application of the selective attention account beyond direct manipulations of attentional demands.

In contrast to these context-dependent changes in absolute weighting, relative f0 and beat-gesture weights manifested substantial within-listener stability across listening conditions. Listeners who assigned greater relative weight to either cue in intelligible speech tended to maintain greater relative reliance on the same cue in unintelligible speech. Moreover, relative f0 weighting in intelligible speech was negatively correlated with relative beat-gesture weighting in unintelligible speech. This provides further support for individual stability, as an otherwise positive correlation would imply that listeners switched cue-weighting strategies radically across listening conditions. Symons, Kachlicka, et al. (2026) have similarly reported stable individual cue-use patterns in the categorization of different prosodic contrasts, ranging from syllabic stress to musical beats; more importantly, they found an association between individual listeners' reliance on a primary cue and their ability to selectively direct attention to that cue. Such stability does not imply an absence of perceptual adaptation, as listeners may substantially adjust their absolute cue weights while maintaining their relative positions among listeners.

Rather, these individual-level findings suggest that context-dependent adaptation may operate within relatively stable individual cue-weighting profiles, with established preferences potentially shaping the extent and direction of adjustments rather than being overridden by a uniform cue-use strategy. In other words, listeners appear to adjust the strength of their reliance on particular cues in response to changes in the listening environment while retaining characteristic differences in how perceptual weight is distributed across cues.

Furthermore, our findings connect with broader previous findings on perceptual learning and recalibration. This vast body of literature showcased adaptive flexibility in perceiving segments and suprasegmental structures (e.g., Bertelson et al., 2003; Norris et al., 2003; Severijnen et al., 2023). More recently, Symons, Jasmin and Tierney (2026) demonstrated that listeners can rapidly adjust their cue-weighting strategies while maintaining relatively stable perceptual preferences over time. Complementing these findings, the present study shows that context-dependent adjustments in audiovisual cue weighting can coexist with substantial individual stability when acoustic information is degraded. Our findings also suggest broader applicability of the selective attention account in two respects. First, cue-specific adjustments emerged without explicit manipulation of attentional resources, suggesting that similar processes may operate when the accessibility or reliability of some cues is reduced due to signal degradation.

Second, the asymmetric adjustments observed across auditory and visual cues point towards cue-specific attention modulation while extending it across modalities.

Nevertheless, as attentional allocation was not directly measured, the proposed attentiondriven mechanism still needs to be tested. Thus, future research could examine whether individual differences in dimension-selective attention predict the extent of cross-modal cue-weighting adjustments under acoustic degradation.

Our findings can also be considered in relation to ideal-observer models (e.g., Kleinschmidt & Jaeger, 2015; Norris & McQueen, 2008), which predict that individual cues contribute additively to perceptual categorization (Bicknell et al., 2025) and are weighted according to their absolute and relative reliability (Bushong & Jaeger, 2025). In line with these models, Westwig et al. (2026) found evidence for additive integration of acoustic and semantic cues, as well as acoustic-cue down-weighting as its reliability decreased in noise. Contrary to ideal-observer predictions on reliability, however, semantic information was not progressively up-weighted as its relative reliability increased. This parallels the asymmetry between acoustic-cue down-weighting and visual-cue up-weighting observed in our study. We also found that most listeners retained their primary perceptual cue across conditions and that individual differences in relative cue weighting remained largely stable. It is thus plausible that adaptive flexibility may be constrained by persistent individual perceptual preferences, potentially leading to deviations from ideal-observer predictions. Future research could directly test the additivity assumption in audiovisual cue integration and examine whether persistent individual preferences place constraints on listeners' ability to adjust cue weights optimally to changes in cue reliability.

The present study also provides a complementary perspective to prior research on audiovisual speech enhancement. Whereas conventional measures primarily quantify the overall benefit of visual information for speech recognition (e.g., Navarra et al., 2010; Sumby & Pollack, 1954), they do not necessarily reveal how the perceptual weights of individual acoustic and visual dimensions change across listening conditions. By independently manipulating f0, beat gestures and lip movement, the present study provides a more direct means of examining these cue-specific changes. This approach revealed substantial acoustic-cue down-weighting without comparable visual-cue upweighting, alongside the persistence of individual differences in relative cue weighting.

This demonstrates understanding adaptation in audiovisual speech perception requires consideration of not only the overall contribution of visual information but also how individual cue weights change across listening conditions.

In conclusion, the present study demonstrates both context-dependent flexibility and individual stability in the perceptual weighting of f0, beat gestures and lip movement as cues to Dutch lexical stress under varying levels of 16-talker babble noise. Listeners systematically reduced their reliance on f0 as acoustic signals deteriorated, without comparably increasing their reliance on either visual cue. This finding is striking given that the visual cues were unaffected by the noise. Meanwhile, individual differences in relative cue weighting persisted across listening conditions, despite changes in absolute weighting and shifts in primary-cue preference among some listeners. Taken together, these findings suggest that adaptation in audiovisual speech perception can be asymmetric and cue-specific, but constrained by relatively stable individual perceptual preferences.
