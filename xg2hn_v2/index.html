Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard), also Internal Review Board - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term); not IEC followed by a number, which is a technical standard - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee/Commission, in the singular or plural - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee, Bioethics Committee/Commission, Ethikkommission - Ethical Review Authority/Unit/Office/Agency - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Human Research Protection Office/Program, Office for Human Subjects Protection - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee(s) on/for (Medical and Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt - Ethics review that was not needed: Ethics review was not required, did not require further ethical review, exempt from formal human research ethics review, no new institutional ethics review

A template line the authors have not filled in (“[Add ethics approval … here.]”) is not a statement.

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

When a statement is expected

The module sorts each paper by where its data came from (the internal .data_source(); the signs and how the rule was tested are described in R/data-source.R): - own: the authors collected data from people or animals, for this paper, in an earlier study of their own, or (a protocol) will collect them; - existing_records: records about individual people that the authors obtained for this study, such as hospital records or a cohort; - existing_unclear: existing data about people from a source the module could not identify; - open_resource: an open resource any researcher can use, such as UK Biobank or NHANES; - none: no data about people or animals (a review, simulation, commentary, theory, tutorial, coding of published papers, or figures about groups); - uncertain: the signs were missing or pointed both ways; - no_text: the paper has no text.

A statement is expected for own, existing_records and existing_unclear. A paper without a statement in these classes, or in uncertain, is flagged. The module reads the text, and the results of power, stat_p_exact, stat_check, repo_check, data_check and prereg_check when they ran before it (run it after them); without stat_p_exact, it finds the p-values in the text.

On 199 PsyArXiv and medRxiv preprints labelled after the rule was fixed, the module decided right whether a statement is needed for 86.9% (the earlier live-data search: 83.4%). Its main errors: other people’s studies described in the words of a new study, records and specimens described only as counts, and open resources the paper does not call open.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 685 tests with 34 instances of inconsistent reporting, Statcheck correctly detected 34 of them, and incorrectly identified 26. Therefore 0% of true instances were missed, and 43% of detections were false positives. See https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/ for more details of the validation.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on 226 Psychological Science papers with 5,019 hand-coded p-values, 438 of them imprecise (scienceverse/validation, branch stat_p_exact). Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). Of the misses, 13 are starred table notes (“*p < .05”) that the module leaves out on purpose and 11 are p-values it did not find; of the wrong reports, 20 are p-values the ground truth does not contain (such as “ps < .05”) and 6 use ≤ or ≥. How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on three hand-coded sets of papers (scienceverse/validation: 249 Psychological Science, 250 Economics and 250 MDPI papers). Of 1,783 non-significant p-values in the ground truth, version 0.4.4.3 found 1,719 (3.6% missed), and 94.8% of its 1,814 detections were in the ground truth. How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Abstract

Despite constant progress, the science of well-being remains marked by confusion, with a proliferation of competing conceptual frameworks, theoretical approaches, and measurement tools undermining both scientific progress and real-world applications in areas such as counseling, therapy, and public policy. This paper proposes an overlooked contributor to this confusion: the adoption of Philosophically Dense Concepts (PDCs) into empirical science, facilitated by academic practices that favor theoretical novelty and discourage interdisciplinary collaboration. What I call PDCs are concepts that have a long history of philosophical debate, whose term is borrowed from a philosophical tradition, and that involve considerations about norms and values. Using a conceptual genealogy of the hedonic and eudaimonic well-being traditions as a paradigmatic example, I show how such PDCs, when translated from philosophy into psychology and the social and health sciences, can become semantically fuzzy, and I argue that this contributed to inconsistent, sometimes incompatible, definitions sharing the same label, and to a proliferation of measurement tools. The paper concludes with recommendations on how to better deal with PDCs in well-being research and the emerging science of flourishing, including a template for clarifying theoretical frameworks, and with a research agenda to test the PDC-as-source-of-confusion hypothesis.

1. Why are there so many shades of well-being?

1.1. A brief introduction.

"In short, the appeal to subjective well-being, as currently used in the psychological literature, is not utterly useless, but at present, it is so riddled with conceptual confusion and normative naiveté that we had better pause and sort things out before going any further." (Nussbaum, 2008, p. 108).

This quote was written nearly twenty years ago. Has the situation improved since then? In this paper, I focus on a specific issue that was already a concern at the time and remains unresolved today. In this article, I have three aims: • First, to show that adopting what I call Philosophically Dense Concepts (PDCs) can contribute to an uncontrolled proliferation of competing and fuzzy conceptualizations and measures (Section 1).

• Second, to specify what the notion of PDCs adds to existing diagnoses (Section 1.3), and to explain, through a conceptual genealogy of eudaimonism and hedonism, why and how this occurs (Section 3).

• Third, to identify the structural conditions that favor this situation, and to offer recommendations, at both the structural and the individual level, that can easily be implemented to avoid unintentionally fostering confusion due to the use of PDCs, including a template for theoretical framework reports and a research agenda to test the PDC hypothesis (Section 4).

Let's start with a fictional situation. Imagine a young social psychologist who has just received their master's degree and is hired for a PhD in collaboration with a city administration that wants to run a well-being survey to assess its citizens' quality of life and guide its policies.

To their astonishment, the literature review turns out to be rather chaotic: there is no standardization, little consensus, and widespread disagreement, even on fundamental issues. An umbrella review of 155 measures of well-being and positive mental health found 410 distinct dimensions (Iasiello et al., 2024). A recent Delphi consensus study among experts from various fields reached over 90% agreement on six dimensions constituting well-being/positive mental-health: meaning and purpose, life satisfaction, self-acceptance, connection, autonomy, and happiness (Iasiello et al., 2026). However, the authors themselves present this taxonomy as preliminary, and it remains to be seen whether it will (and if it should) be adopted as a guideline.

Finally, whether positive mental health and well-being are synonyms or distinct concepts is not discussed in depth, although there are good reasons to think they are not the same (Wren-Lewis & Alexandrova, 2021).

What can feel even more dizzying is the number of different "traditions" of well-being. Some refers to "authentic happiness," (Seligman, 2004) others to "subjective" well-being, (Diener et al., 2018), "spiritual", "religious" and "existential" well-being (Cohen et al., 1996; Vail et al., 2026), "emotional" well-being (Park et al., 2023), "eudaimonic" and "hedonic" wellbeing, (Vittersø, 2016) or even "flourishing" (Rule et al., 2024). And for each of these frameworks, there is many instruments to measure them.

But why such diversity? One potential explanation can be found in the work of Alexandrova (2017), who argues that the diversity of well-being definitions and measurements is partly explained by what she calls contextualism: the content of well-being judgments varies depending on the practical context in which they are made. Different scientific disciplines study different contexts and purposes, which leads them to adopt distinct constructs. However, contextualism alone cannot account for the proliferation of divergent and often incompatible conceptualizations and measurement tools that exist even within a single construct. For example, Martela and Sheldon (2019) found 45 different ways to measure what is labeled as eudaimonic well-being.

Another explanation might be what is known as the "toothbrush problem": most measures are used only once or twice, and nobody else wants to use them again, as if they were personal toothbrushes, which leads to a proliferation of measures (Elson et al., 2023). However, while this may explain the proliferation of measures, it does not explain the proliferation of different, sometimes incompatible, conceptualizations, sometimes even within the same "tradition". For instance, Vittersø (2016) compiled 41 definitions of "eudaimonic well-being", some of which are mutually incompatible, a number that has only grown since.

Here, I propose an additional proximal explanatory factor to explain this proliferation of definitions and measurements: the adoption of what I call Philosophically Dense Concepts (PDCs) within empirical and theoretical science, that I define as concepts (1) with a long history of philosophical debate, (2) whose term is borrowed from a philosophical tradition, and (3) whose definition embeds assumptions not only about facts, but also about values and norms, that is, a normative or evaluative standard of what a good life is or of how one ought to live. My claim is not that PDCs are the only cause of the proliferation of well-being theories and constructs, but that philosophical density is a specific aggravating factor, over and above general conceptual vagueness. The problem I identify is therefore not only that measures multiply, but also that different, sometimes incompatible, theorizations and conceptualizations come to coexist under the same label. I formulate this claim as a hypothesis, and I propose a way to test it in Section 4.4.

1.2. What PDCs are and what they are not: an illustration.

I propose three diagnostic questions to determine whether a given concept is a PDC: • Does the concept have a long history of philosophical debate?

• Is the term, or its core component, directly borrowed from a philosophical tradition?

• Does its definition embed a normative standard (i.e., a view of what a good life is or of how one ought to live) or an evaluative standard (i.e., a view of what is or should be considered important), rather than leaving such standards to participants' own judgment? These questions do not draw a strict line between PDCs and other concepts: philosophical density is a continuum. A concept is highly dense when the answer to all three questions is "yes," and its density further increases with the weight of the traditions associated with it and with the strength of the normative and evaluative standards it embodies. In such cases, a more in-depth conceptual examination is necessary before proceeding to theorization and operationalization. If only one or two answers are affirmative, the concept is less dense: we still need to be cautious, although the risk of confusion is lower. In all cases, I recommend including a theoretical report such as the one proposed in Section 4. 1.3. What the notion of PDC adds to existing diagnoses.

The idea that the vocabulary of well-being science is confused is not new, and the notion of PDC needs to be situated with respect to at least three bodies of work: existing diagnoses of vagueness and conceptual confusion, philosophical debates on thick concepts and mixed claims, and the literature on measurement practices.

What does it add to the conceptual confusion and vagueness diagnostic?

These diagnoses fall into two categories. In psychology, some works has focused on specific tradition of well-being, for example, the vagueness of eudaimonia has been at the center of a published debate. Kashdan, Biswas-Diener and King (2008) argued that eudaimonia is not well defined and lacks consistent measurement, which led to replies by (Waterman et al., 2008) and Delle Fave and Bassi (2009). Huta and Waterman (2014) then went a step further and proposed a classification of the definitions of eudaimonia according to their category of analysis: orientations, behaviors, experiences, and functioning.

More broadly (Bringmann et al., 2022) have made the case that, although necessary part of psychological science, conceptual clarification tend to be neglected.

From the philosophical side, some works have mentioned not a problem of vagueness as such, but something that, I believe, led to more vagueness. Kristjánsson (2010) has shown that positive psychology suffers from internal divisions over what happiness is and from ambiguities regarding its relation to virtue, and it has been shown that the field struggles to acknowledge it is inherently value-laden, since it rests on evaluations of what is good for us and what makes a life go well (Prinzing, 2021).

The present paper builds on this work. It does not claim to discover that eudaimonia, for example, is used inconsistently, or that there is a general problem of conceptual clarity in psychology. These works describe the confusion, the notion of PDC is meant to explain part of it. It offers an account of one important and overlooked reason for this confusion, shows why it arose with this concept in particular, and generalizes this account to every use of PDCs.

What does it add to the debate involving thick concept and mixed claims? Second, the notion of PDC can seem close to two established philosophical notions. Thick concepts, a term that goes back to Williams (Kirchin, 2013), are concepts such as courage, cruelty, or generosity, which combine evaluation (there is a negative judgment or a positive judgement) with non-evaluative description (a simple state of fact). Mixed claims, a term introduced by Alexandrova (2017), are empirical generalizations about phenomena such as well-being whose variables are defined in relation to a normative standard (e.g., "long commutes are associated with lower well-being", where measuring well-being presupposes a view of what makes a life go well).

Should PDCs then be understood as thick concepts? Not necessarily. Some PDCs, such as flourishing, have a descriptive content that theorists of thick concepts would recognize.

Others, such as well-being or happiness, are usually treated in metaethics as thin, or at best as being in the thin end of a continuum. What makes a concept philosophically dense is therefore not its position on the thin-thick scale. It is the fact that the concept is attached to elements of several philosophical traditions, sometimes incompatible, each involving different considerations about norms and values (for example, eudaimonia carries a long and plural theoretical history, whatever its degree of thickness).

Mixed claims, for their part, concern the statements researchers make, whereas PDCs concern the concepts they import before making any claim. The two notions are therefore complementary, since, as I argue, PDCs are one important source of mixed claims.

What does it add to the "measurement schmeasurement" problem? Third, the proliferation of measures is not specific to well-being research. Elson et al. (2023) show that most psychological measures are used only once or twice, as if measures were toothbrushes that no one wants to share, and Flake and Fried (2020) describe how poorly specified constructs and measurement decisions constitute questionable measurement practices (the "measurement schmeasurement" attitude plague).

These works account for the proliferation of measures in psychology in general, but they leave a question open: why do some definitions and conceptualization inside certain traditions proliferate more than others? The notion of PDC offers a candidate answer. Philosophical density cause proliferation because the breadth of a PDC offers an almost inexhaustible reservoir of aspects that researchers can borrow to build their definitions and measurement tools. This is why I propose to test the hypothesis at both the conceptual and the measurement levels (Section 4.4).

2. A paradigmatic case of uncritical adoption of PDCs in the science of well-being.

>A paradigmatic case of uncritical adoption of PDCs in the science of well-being.

2.1. A brief overview of the field of hedonic and eudaimonic well-being.

Besides the "subjective well-being" tradition , which clearly leads in terms of influence, one of the most hotly debated perspectives is eudaimonic well-being, whose proponents often position themselves in opposition to subjective well-being, which they often label as "hedonic"

well-being, and there is currently no real consensus on their content or scientific relevance within the psychology of well-being (Ryan & Deci, 2001; Kashdan et al., 2008; Martela & Sheldon, 2019; King & Hicks, 2021).

Subjective well-being, also sometimes referred to as hedonic well-being, holds that, in order to accurately assess an individual's well-being, it is sufficient to measure their overall life satisfaction as well as their frequency of positive and negative emotions (Diener et al., 1985; Disabato et al., 2016). It has undeniably become one of the most common approaches to measuring well-being in contemporary psychology. The origin of this approach can be traced to the development of a scale designed to assess mood and emotional states (Diener & Emmons, 1984), although earliest measure of well-being can be found in history (Angner, 2011). Shortly after, a more cognitive measure was introduced, the satisfaction with life scale (Diener et al., 1985), which measures an individual's overall level of satisfaction with her life, and is still one of the most widely used scales to capture well-being today.

Although the former approach, initially labeled as subjective well-being, includes a nonhedonic component (i.e., emotions, and life satisfaction), it has often been grouped into the same category as the purely "hedonic" approach, a label partly coined within the tradition itself (Kahneman et al., 1999), but mostly used by proponents of eudaimonic well-being (see Section 3.2.2). For its proponents, the "daimon" individuals are really seeking for is "an ideal in the sense of an excellence, perfection toward which one strives, and it gives meaning and direction to one's life" (Ryff, 1989 (Ryff, , p. 1070)). The main idea is that the well-being of a life cannot be reduced to hedonic states of pleasure and satisfaction, and that it is necessary to take into account dimensions such as autonomy or meaning. Taking inspiration from Aristotle and humanistic psychologists like Abraham Maslow and Carl Rogers (who themselves took inspiration from philosophy), proponents of these approaches argue that there are certain goods that individuals must pursue and acquire.

This new wave of interest in eudaimonic approaches to well-being began around 1990 (Ryff, 1989), although the label "eudaimonic well-being" itself only spread after Ryan and Deci's (2001) review (which had been cited more than 23,200 times as of November 2026!).

Since then, the number of articles using this phrase in their title or abstract has risen from 16 in 2010 to 150 in 2024. This growth exceeds that of scientific output as a whole: relative to the total number of indexed articles, their share has increased more than sixfold over the same period (see Figure 1, OpenAlex data, accessed November 2026). This growth is also steeper than that of "subjective well-being", which rose from 755 to 2,535 publications over the same period, while "hedonic well-being" remains a rare label (Table 2). The distinction has sometimes been framed as one between two kinds of happiness (e.g., Waterman, 1993), and its empirical basis has been debated, through correlational and factoranalyses evidence (Disabato et al., 2016). Today, however, it is more commonly, and more defensibly, understood as a distinction between two research traditions, which emphasize different elements of well-being (Biswas- Diener et al., 2009). Understood in this way, the distinction is not problematic in itself: different traditions can legitimately shed light on different aspects of what makes a life go well. The problem lies elsewhere. It is not between the two traditions, but within them: under the same label (and the case of "eudaimonic well-being" is particularly interesting), definitions and measures have proliferated to a point where it is no longer clear what the label refers to.

2.2. The state of confusion.

The adoption of the PDC of eudaimonism went hand in hand with the development, by proponents of eudaimonic well-being, of many different definitions, theoretical frameworks, and measures, all sharing the same "eudaimonic" label. Martela and Sheldon (2019) list no less than 45 different ways to measure eudaimonic well-being, comprising 63 distinct constructs.

The broad variety of these definitions or their lack of specificity can mislead theoretical and empirical works.

This heterogeneity has also been documented by Huta and Waterman (2014) who showed that definitions of eudaimonia (and hedonia) fall into different categories of analysis, namely orientations, behaviors, experiences, and functioning, and that the terms are sometimes defined vaguely, or applied to mere correlates of well-being. Consider, for instance, the conclusion of a theoretical article on what the use of MDMA could bring to research on the feeling of connection (Lyubomirsky, 2022, p.40): "They offer pleasure, a pleasant state infused with positive emotion, the delight of viewing beauty, and the pleasure of touch.

Yet their defining characteristic is eudaimonic, leading users to feel loving, connected, empathetic, grateful, and in flow"

In Huta and Waterman's terms, this is a case of category mixing: experiences, a capacity, and a state are grouped under the same labels. Here, it is psychological states and experiences of an individual that are characterized as hedonic (positive emotions and sensory experiences as well as a general positive state), while what is characterized as eudaimonic are a cognitive competence (empathy), positive emotional experiences that are not just characterized by their valence (the feeling of gratitude, connection and love), and a specific psychological experience (flow). In other papers, what is eudaimonic pertains to life orientations or to specific experiences (Waterman, 1993, p. 199), or to types of goals and basic psychological needs, as in self-determination theory (Deci & Ryan, 2012).

Although Huta and Waterman's classification brings much-needed clarity to the eudaimonic tradition, it does not explain why definitions and measures became so diverse in the first place.

Why did so many researchers, with the best intentions, attach such different things to the same concept? In the next section, using a conceptual genealogy approach, I show how the adoption of specific philosophically dense concepts, when uncritical, can contribute to a state of confusion.

3. Genealogy as a diagnostic of the case of eudaimonic and hedonic well-being.

As a reminder, what I call philosophically dense concepts are concepts with a long history of philosophical debate, whose term is borrowed from a philosophical tradition, and that carry embedded assumptions about facts, values, and norms (see Section 1.2). And as I will show, the use of a PDC lead to confusion only when uncritically adopted. The core issue is twofold: first, these concepts were adopted from philosophy into empirical science with not enough care, second, their breadth and depth of meaning is so vast that researchers have selectively appropriated different aspects when building theoretical frameworks and developing measurement scales. This selective appropriation, I suggest, helps explain the proliferation of divergent and often incompatible conceptualizations and measures we observe today in certain tradition.

3.1. The problematic transition of eudaimonism from philosophy to science.

>The problematic transition of eudaimonism from philosophy to science.

3.1.1. The birth of eudaimonism.

Let us start with the term's folk usage. The adjective eudaimōn, from which eudaimonia and "eudaimonism" derive, was widely used in Ancient Greece. Literally, it means having a good spirit (daimōn) watching over oneself, a mark of divine favour. Being eudaimōn could be close in meaning to being makarios (blessed), olbios (prosperous, favoured) or eutychēs (fortunate), or to idioms such as eu zēn (living well), all of which belong to the semantic field of happiness, luck and the good life (Gåvertsson, 2013).

According to Gåvertsson (2013), the first occurrence of the word in extant Greek literature is found at the very end of Hesiod's Works and Days (around 700 BC, Hesiod, 2018).

Hesiod urges his brother Perses to live through honest labour and justice rather than strife, setting out a farmer's calendar, practical and religious precepts, and finally a list of lucky and unlucky days of the month, before closing with a line that contains one of the earliest attestations of eudaimon in Greek literature: "That man is happy/fortunate (eudaimon) and lucky/prosperous (olbios) in them who knows all these things and does his work without offending the deathless gods, who discerns the omens of the birds and avoids transgression".

Although eudaimōn is often translated as "happy", it has little to do with the psychological notion of happiness in use today. In everyday usage, the word "happiness" is largely understood as a state of mind nowadays, as experimental philosophy studies have shown (Kneer & Haybron, 2025). In archaic Greek thought, being eudaimōn is a matter of how a person's life is going as a whole, favored by the gods and well conducted (working hard, respecting the divine), rather than of how that person feels.

When philosophers such as Plato and Aristotle started to use the term, its religious connotations were largely (but not entirely) stripped away, and it became primarily an ethical concept, although Aristotle, unlike Plato, still acknowledges the role of fortune and external goods in a good life (Aristotle, 2009, I.8-10).

In the Nicomachean Ethics (EN), Aristotle holds that eudaimonia is the highest good for a human being: the ultimate end (telos) of all of our action, the only good chosen always for its own sake and never for the sake of anything else. To determine what it consists in, Aristotle appeals to human nature through the so-called function argument (ergon): just as a good fluteplayer is one who performs his characteristic activity well (playing the flute), the ultimate human good must lie in performing well the activity proper to human beings, something that Aristotle deems to be rationality (something highly debatable from our modern scientific perspective). In Book X, he adds that "if eudaimonia is activity in accordance with virtue (aretē), it is reasonable that it should be activity in accordance with the highest virtue" (X 7, 1177a12-13, my translation).

More concretely, what would such a life look like? In Book I, Aristotle introduces three kinds of life commonly deemed attractive: a life devoted to pleasure, a political life, and a life devoted to knowledge and understanding. He returns to them in Book X, the last book of the treatise: The life of pleasure cannot be our ultimate end, since what counts as genuinely pleasant must be judged by another standard, that of the virtuous person, pleasure and amusement still belong to a good life, but they do not direct it.

The good life is the life of theōria, i.e., of contemplation: the activity of someone who already possesses theoretical wisdom (sophia) and devotes his life to exercising it, using fully its rationality function to the fullest. Perhaps we would think that it is close to the modern academic life? But far from it: since theōria is not research but the exercise of knowledge already acquired. Those who know, Aristotle says, spend their time more pleasantly than those who are still inquiring, and the object of such knowledge is not the whole of what can be known about the world, but its first principles and causes (what we call today metaphysics, theology and cosmology). The life of practical wisdom and ethical virtue, exemplified by the political life, is eudaimōn only "in a secondary way" (book X of the treatise).

This does not mean that the other virtues are unimportant, but how exactly they relate to contemplation has been debated since antiquity (for an overview see Nagel, 1972). On the intellectualist (or "dominant") reading, they matter mainly as conditions for, or in service of, the contemplative life, on the inclusive (or "comprehensive") reading, they are themselves constituents of eudaimonia.

More broadly, the fourth and third centuries BCE saw a remarkable variety of accounts of what it is to be eudaimōn. Hedonistic accounts place pleasure at the centre of the good life.

Although contemporary psychology often contrasts "hedonic" and "eudaimonic" well-being, in ancient philosophy hedonism was one possible answer to the question of what it is to be eudaimōn, not an alternative to eudaimonism. Epicurus advocated a simple life among friends, aimed at freedom from bodily pain (aponia) and mental disturbance (ataraxia). To achieve it, one should satisfy natural and necessary desires (food, drink, shelter), limit natural but unnecessary ones (luxurious food, sex), and reject empty ones altogether (fame, power, unlimited wealth). The Cyrenaics defended a more radical hedonism, on which the end of life is present bodily pleasure (a good meal, wine, sex).

Other schools refused to make pleasure the end of life. For Aristotle, as we have seen, the good life is a life of virtuous activity, crowned by contemplation. The Stoics considered that virtue, understood as living in agreement with nature and reason, is the only true good, while health, wealth or reputation are "indifferents". The Cynics, who preceded and inspired the Stoics, embraced a form of "punk" asceticism: rejecting material attachments and social conventions alike, they were known for walking barefoot, sleeping in public places, and haranguing the rich and powerful to abandon their possessions and vain desires. That the nature of eudaimonia has remained disputed for so long is itself a good illustration of philosophical density.

3.1.2. A stable concept in contemporary philosophy.

In contemporary philosophy, the concept of eudaimonism is still in use and fairly well circumscribed, although in two related senses. In ethics (how should I live?), eudaimonism designates the view that one's own eudaimonia is the ultimate end of rational action, the framework shared by most ancient ethical theories (Annas, 1993), most of which also hold that virtue is central to eudaimonia. In the philosophy of well-being (what is good for me?), it designates the view that well-being consists in the fulfilment of one's nature: what is ultimately good for a human being is fixed by what kind of being she is, and consists in the realization of the capacities characteristic of that nature, rather than in pleasure or desire-satisfaction (Haybron, 2016). In both senses, it remains consistent with the original meaning of the concept.

The central debates then mainly revolve around whether virtue is a necessary component of well-being, and whether the fulfilment of one's nature is primarily a matter of achieving specific goals (i.e., succeeding in intrinsically valuable pursuits) or of developing one's capacities (i.e., actualizing one's nature)

3.1.3. A problematic transition from philosophy to empirical science.

To understand what is lost or appropriated in the contemporary use of the PDC of eudaimonism in psychology, it is useful to recall, as we showed, that ancient eudaimonism is not a single theory, but a shared framework within which different schools took different positions. All agree that eudaimonia is the ultimate end of human life, i.e., what is chosen for its own sake and for the sake of which everything else is chosen. But they disagree on several dimensions, which can be summarized as follows.

(1) Content: what does eudaimonia consist in? In virtuous activity, crowned by contemplation (Aristotle), in virtue alone (the Stoics), or in pleasure, understood as the absence of pain and disturbance (Epicurus)? Something else?

(2) Role of virtue/character strenght: is virtue sufficient for eudaimonia (the Stoics), necessary but not sufficient (Aristotle), or only the best means to a pleasant life (Epicurus)?

(3) Human nature: the good life is always grounded in a conception of nature, whether it is the human function of rationality (Aristotle), living in agreement with nature and reason (the Stoics), or the natural pursuit of pleasure that can already be observed in newborns (Epicurus).

(4) Activity or state: eudaimonia can be conceived as an activity, a way of living (Aristotle), or as a stable condition of the soul, such as the Epicurean or Stoics ataraxia (although they also involve a way of living).

(5) Psychological experience: pleasure can be the end itself (Epicurus), what accompanies and completes a good activity (Aristotle), or something to be distinguished from the passions, which should be eliminated and replaced by "good feelings" such as rational joy (the Stoics).

(6) External goods: they are necessary to some degree (Aristotle), indifferent (the Stoics), minimized (Epicurus), or rejected (the Cynics).

(7) Social and political dimension: the good life is lived in the city and as part of it, since man is a political animal (Aristotle), as a citizen of the cosmos i.e., the whole world (the Stoics, the Cynics), or among friends, away from political life (Epicurus).

Despite these disagreements, one feature is common to all: the good life is defined by an objective standard, grounded in nature and reason, and not by what an individual happens to feel or desire.

If we look at the main eudaimonic approaches in well-being science, it appears that each of them selects some elements of this framework, while reinterpreting or leaving aside the others. Ryff's model of psychological well-being (1989) retains a conception of human nature as potential to be realized (personal growth) and includes positive relations with others, but the telos becomes "purpose in life", i.e., the goals that the individual sets for himself, and virtue is absent. Waterman (1993) keeps the notion of daimon, but reinterprets it as one's "true self", so that eudaimonia becomes the feeling of personal expressiveness, i.e., mainly a psychological experience. Self-determination theory (Ryan et al., 2008) with their use of eudaimonism, is probably the closest to the ancient framework, since it understands eudaimonia as a way of living, grounded in basic human needs, but the other element are set aside. Huta and Ryan (2010) focus on eudaimonic motives, i.e., seeking to develop and use the best in oneself, independently of whether one actually lives well. In contrast, positive psychology's classification of character strengths (Park et al., 2004) reintroduces virtue, but as a list of traits that can be cultivated separately, without the practical wisdom or the ethical discussion that is fundamental to them. But in all these approaches, nothing is told about what and why some elements are selected, and why the other element of eudaimonism is rejected.

The problem is that the PDC of eudaimonism is too broad and too rich to be conceptualized and operationalized in empirical science without loss, a little like trying to represent a four-dimensional object in a two-dimensional space. This has in turn led to a wide variety of definitions and measures sharing the same label of "eudaimonic well-being".

3.2. The less-but-still problematic transition of hedonism from philosophy to science.

>The less-but-still problematic transition of hedonism from philosophy to science.

3.2.1. The birth of hedonism and its use in contemporary philosophy.

What about the concept of hedonism, which is often opposed to eudaimonism in the science of well-being? The term derives from the Greek hēdonē (pleasure), although "hedonism" itself is a modern coinage. As we have seen (Section 3.1.1), in ancient philosophy hedonism is not an alternative to eudaimonism, but one particular answer to the question of what the good life consists in. For Epicurus, pleasure is the only intrinsic good, but it is best achieved by limiting one's desires rather than by satisfying all of them, and the highest pleasures are the absence of bodily pain (aponia) and of mental disturbance (ataraxia). Virtue is not left aside, quite the contrary, but it is valued as the best means to a pleasant life, not as an end.

The association of hedonism and "epicureanism" with an unrestrained pursuit of ever more sophisticated pleasures is therefore a caricature, but an old one. Already in antiquity, Epictetus described Epicureanism as a philosophy of laziness (Discourses, Epictetus 2.20), Throughout the history of philosophy, the concept of hedonism has remained well delineated. Classical utilitarianism illustrates this stability, although their account differs from those of ancient hedonism. Bentham (1996) combined both positions in his famous claim that "nature has placed mankind under the governance of two sovereign masters, pain and pleasure", and proposed to measure pleasures by quantitative dimensions such as their intensity and duration. Mill (2010) kept the hedonist core but distinguished "higher" from "lower" pleasures, judging that it is "better to be Socrates dissatisfied than a fool satisfied".

In contemporary philosophy, hedonism refers to two distinct positions within philosophical debates (Moore, 2019): motivational hedonism, whose proponents claim that only pleasure or pain motivates us, and ethical (or evaluative) hedonism, which holds that pleasure is the only thing that is intrinsically good, so that a person's well-being consists solely in the balance of pleasure over pain. In other words, the good life is a pleasurable life, and pleasure is the ultimate good one should pursue (Feldman, 2004).

3.2.2. The transition from philosophy to empirical science.

In the science of well-being, "hedonic well-being" is usually equated with subjective wellbeing, which comprises three components: the frequency of positive emotions, the frequency of negative emotions, and life satisfaction. Unlike eudaimonic well-being, it has been operationalized relatively consistently. But is it hedonic?

The first two components seem, at first sight, to fit a hedonic perspective. Yet, as Haybron (2016) points out, the term "hedonic" strictly pertains to pleasure and pain, i.e., to the pleasantness or unpleasantness of experiences, whereas emotions such as joy, sadness or anxiety have a much richer content than their mere valence. Furthermore, a more serious problem concerns the third component. Life satisfaction is not an experience of pleasure, but a global evaluation of one's life, as shown by items such as "In most ways my life is close to my ideal"

or "So far I have gotten the important things I want in life" (Diener et al., 1985). One can judge one's life satisfying while experiencing little pleasure, and the reverse. Kahneman and Deaton (2010) have shown that these two dimensions can even diverge empirically: income, for instance, is strongly associated with life evaluation, but much less with emotional well-being beyond a certain level.

Where does the "hedonic" label come from, then? It is not entirely external to the tradition. In the late 1990s, Kahneman and colleagues explicitly proposed a "hedonic psychology" (Kahneman et al., 1999), defined as the study of what makes experiences and life pleasant or unpleasant , and Kahneman's notion of "experienced utility" was explicitly inspired by Bentham's hedonism (Kahneman et al., 1997). However, the label was sometimes extended from this specific research program to subjective well-being as a whole, including life satisfaction, and emotions, which are not hedonic per se. And the phrase "hedonic well-being" seems to be used mostly in contrast with its eudaimonic counterpart: 67% of the works using it also use the term "eudaimonic", against about 1% of those using "subjective well-being" (see Table 2 in section 2). It thus functions mainly as a contrast term, rather than as a self-description of the subjective well-being tradition.

To conclude, the hedonic label should be used more carefully, for three reasons. First, applying it to subjective well-being as a whole is conceptually problematic, since its three components are not purely hedonic. Second, as shown above, the opposition between hedonism and eudaimonism is itself misleading, since hedonism is one possible account of what eudaimonia, i.e., the good life, consists in. Third, since hedonism is itself a PDC, any attempt to theorize hedonic well-being from it should specify which account of hedonism is retained (e.g., Epicurus' tranquil pleasures, the Cyrenaics' present bodily pleasures, or Bentham's calculus of pleasure and pain), since these accounts are far from equivalent.

One might object that hedonism is a counterexample to the PDC hypothesis: according to the criteria proposed in Section 1.2, hedonism is clearly a philosophically dense concept, and yet hedonic well-being led to less confusion than the eudaimonic tradition. This is not a counterexample, however, for two reasons. First, the main measures of subjective well-being (positive and negative affect, satisfaction with life) were not developed from the philosophical concept of hedonism, the label was attached to them afterwards. Second, when hedonism was actually used as a source of theorization, as in Kahneman's work, it was done explicitly and narrowly: Kahneman did not borrow "hedonism" in general, but one specific element of one specific author, Bentham's conception of utility as the balance of pleasure and pain over time, and he stated it openly (Kahneman et al., 1997). The resulting measures of experienced wellbeing, such as the Day Reconstruction Method, have remained relatively consistent. This is precisely what the present paper recommends (Section 4.3): the problem is not the use of a PDC as such, but its implicit and selective use. The case of hedonism therefore does not weaken the PDC hypothesis, but illustrates its condition: a PDC leads to a proliferation of divergent conceptualizations when it is used as a broad source of inspiration, not when one element of it is explicitly selected and stated.

4. General conclusion: the root of the problem, recommendations, and a research agenda.

So far, I have proposed an account of what is a PDC: a concept with a long history of philosophical debate, whose term is borrowed from a philosophical tradition, and whose definition embeds a normative or evaluative standard of what a good life is or of how one ought to live. I showed, through the genealogy of eudaimonism and hedonism, how the uncritical adoption of such concepts (and the word uncritical is important here) can lead to selective appropriation, and to a proliferation of confusing accounts and measures sharing the same label, and I suggest that existential well-being, or flourishing traditions faces the same risk. This problem is detrimental both for the development of science, and for its application in our society, in areas such as counseling, therapy, and public policy.

In this last section, I will develop what I consider being the structural root of this problem, recommendations to alleviate the epistemic crisis, and a research agenda to test the PDC as a source of confusion hypothesis.

4.1 The structural roots of confusion.

Understanding why PDCs have been adopted so widely, and often so uncritically, requires a metascientific perspective. Below, I suggest three possible leads.

The functioning of publishing and grant systems: Funding agencies and high-impact journals tend to reward novelty, complex modeling, and theoretical innovation more than efforts toward conceptual integration, clarification, or statistical replication. In a publish-or-perish environment, the rational response for individual researchers is often to develop new scales, subdimensions, and frameworks rather than to invest time in refining and consolidating what already exists.

PDCs are (too) cognitively attractive: What makes PDCs particularly vulnerable to this dynamic is their internal richness. Because they carry centuries of philosophical theorization and encompass multiple strands of thought, they offer an almost inexhaustible reservoir of aspects to selectively appropriate and reinterpret. This is what distinguishes the PDC problem from mere conceptual vagueness: vague concepts can, in principle, be sharpened through definitional work, but PDCs resist such sharpening precisely because they are intrinsically very rich and complex.

The disciplinary fragmentation: Academia is organized into disciplines, and while interdisciplinarity is often praised in theory, the reality is that most fields in the human and social sciences still operate in relative isolation, what Klein (2021) refers to as disciplinary silos.

Despite a genuine intention to draw inspiration from philosophy in order to highlight an overlooked and important aspect of well-being this has often been done without sufficient sustained dialogue with philosophy and philosophers. Bibliometric data illustrate this imbalance: only 0.9% of the works mentioning "eudaimonic well-being" in their title or abstract are classified in philosophy, against 0.2% for "subjective well-being", while the share classified in psychology is almost identical for both labels (54.5% and 54.2%, Table 2).

4.2. Recommendations to mitigate the epistemic crisis.

The recommendations below are addressed at two levels: the structural level, which concerns the collective practices of the scientific community, including journals and funding institutions, and the individual level, which concerns researchers themselves. Several of these recommendations echo existing guidance on construct definition and measures (Elson et al., 2023; Flake & Fried, 2020), but I focus here on what is specific to PDCs, although it can be used for conceptual clarification in general.

Recommendation at the structural level: adopting a theoretical framework report

The scientific community would benefit from collective efforts to reform incentive systems in ways that reward conceptual clarity, replicability, and interdisciplinary engagement, rather than privileging theoretical and empirical novelty for its own sake. Concretely, for researchers wanting to use PDCs, this could take the form of theoretical frameworks statements in which researchers explicitly commit to a theoretical framework, justify their choice of philosophical sources, and clarify how PDCs are being operationalized before proceeding to scale or theory development.

Recommendation 1: Recognize philosophically dense concepts for what they are.

The first step toward avoiding confusion is to recognize when one is dealing with a PDC, using the three diagnostic questions proposed in Section 1.2 (see Table 1). The denser the concept, the more in-depth the conceptual examination should be before proceeding to operationalization, and in all cases, a theoretical report such as the one presented above (Table 3) is advised.

Recommendation 2: Be careful when adopting a PDC in one's theoretical framework, and clarify conceptualizations before operationalizing.

In practical terms, this means that when a researcher adopts a concept such as eudaimonism, they should explicitly engage with its philosophical genealogy, not necessarily in exhaustive detail, but sufficiently to identify which aspects of the tradition are being retained, which are being set aside, and why. The selective appropriation documented in Section 3, whereby Aristotle's rich ethical framework was reduced to the notion of self-actualization, is a direct consequence of skipping this step. Researchers should also be explicit about which philosophical tradition they are drawing on and why: privileging Aristotle over the Stoics, for instance, is not a neutral choice.

Concretely, before developing or using a measure involving PDCs, researchers should also state how their conceptualizations and operationalization differs from existing ones that use the same or similar labels (see the template proposed in Table 3).

Recommendation 3: Foster genuine interdisciplinary collaboration when needed.

When a research project involves PDCs, genuine collaboration with experts in those traditions can constitute a methodological protection. Just as the replication crisis prompted a broader involvement of statisticians and methodologists in psychological research, a parallel movement toward philosophical collaboration could help address the confusion documented in this article.

This is not about deferring to philosophers as ultimate authorities on conceptualizations, but about benefiting from their training in conceptual analysis, their familiarity with the history of ideas, and their sensitivity to the normative and evaluative dimensions that empirical researchers may overlook when dealing with PDCs. Encouraging precedents already exist in the literature: Margolis et al. (2022) offer an example of work that systematically compares measurement scales across different philosophical traditions of well-being.

Recommendation 4: Resist the temptation to create new scales.

The academic incentive structure rewards novelty, and developing a new measurement instrument is often a more publishable contribution than carefully validating or refining an existing one. The scales are too often used as "toothbrush" (Flake & Fried, 2020).

4.3. A research agenda: testing the PDC hypothesis.

The argument developed in this paper relies on observations and theoretical considerations, and its central claim, i.e., that philosophical density can contribute to conceptual and operational divergence under the same label, over and above general vagueness and academic incentives, remains a hypothesis. This hypothesis can be tested without collecting new data. For example, Fried (2017) coded the items of seven common depression scales into distinct symptoms, computed the Jaccard index (the proportion of content shared by two scales) for each pair of scales, and found a low mean overlap (0.36). The same method could be, with some adjustments, applied here, at two levels: the measurement level and the conceptual level.

Concretely, the coding procedure I propose is inductive and as fine-grained as possible.

• First, all items of the selected scales are read, and each item is assigned to a category describing the specific content it assesses (e.g., general meaning, purpose, death anxiety, relationship, etc), regardless of the label of the subscale it belongs to. A new category is created whenever an item addresses a content that is not covered by the existing ones, and each category is defined as precisely as the item allows: for example, an item about personal significance (whether one's life matters to oneself or to others) is not coded in the same category as an item about cosmic significance, even though both would usually fall under the broad label of "meaning".

• Each scale can then be represented by the set of categories it covers, and the overlap between scales computed with the Jaccard index, as in Fried (2017). Since the level of precision of the categories directly affects the overlap obtained, the same coding rules should be applied to scales built from a PDC and to scales built from a low-density concept, ideally by at least two independent coders unaware of the hypothesis.

The same procedure can, and should, be applied at the conceptual level, since this is where, according to the PDC hypothesis, the problem originates. Instead of items, the coded material would be the definitions and theoretical components of frameworks sharing the same label, for example the definitions the eudaimonic well-being existential well-being or the flourishing tradition. Each framework would then be represented by the set of conceptual components it includes, and the overlap between frameworks computed in the same way. I expect that low conceptual overlap should be observed among most frameworks using PDC.

If the PDC hypothesis is correct, conceptualizations and scales using PDC should on average show lower content overlap than those built from a low-density concept. If they do not, the hypothesis should be weakened: philosophical density would then be, at most, one contributor among others to a problem that affects psychology as a whole. Either result would be informative, and would help the field to know where conceptual efforts are most needed.

4.4. A non-definitive conclusion.

So, what should a young social psychologist starting their PhD do if they are seeking to conduct a well-being survey to assess the quality of life of their citizens and guide policy, when faced with the overwhelming number of different theoretical frameworks and measurement tools?

Besides the commonsense advice to look at existing recommendations (VanderWeele et al., 2020), the general conclusion would be to be aware of PDCs, and to handle carefully framework operating under the umbrella of such PDCs, such as eudaimonic well-being and flourishing, and to follow the four recommendations listed above: • Recognize philosophically dense concepts for what they are.

• Be careful when adopting a PDC in one's theoretical framework, and clarify conceptualizations before operationalizing (by using the template proposed in the section 4.2 for example).

• Foster genuine interdisciplinary collaboration when needed.

• Resist the temptation to create new scales.
