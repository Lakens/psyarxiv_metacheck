How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard), also Internal Review Board - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term); not IEC followed by a number, which is a technical standard - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee/Commission, in the singular or plural - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee, Bioethics Committee/Commission, Ethikkommission - Ethical Review Authority/Unit/Office/Agency - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Human Research Protection Office/Program, Office for Human Subjects Protection - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee(s) on/for (Medical and Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. When a statement is expected

The module sorts each paper by where its data came from (the internal .data_source(); the signs and how the rule was tested are described in R/data-source.R): - own: the authors collected data from people or animals, for this paper, in an earlier study of their own, or (a protocol) will collect them; - existing_records: records about individual people that the authors obtained for this study, such as hospital records or a cohort; - existing_unclear: existing data about people from a source the module could not identify; - open_resource: an open resource any researcher can use, such as UK Biobank or NHANES; - none: no data about people or animals (a review, simulation, commentary, theory, tutorial, coding of published papers, or figures about groups); - uncertain: the signs were missing or pointed both ways; - no_text: the paper has no text.

A statement is expected for own, existing_records and existing_unclear. A paper without a statement in these classes, or in uncertain, is flagged. The module reads the text, and the results of power, stat_p_exact, stat_check, repo_check, data_check and prereg_check when they ran before it (run it after them); without stat_p_exact, it finds the p-values in the text.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

Read more in the metacheck manual.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Abstract

The Big Five personality traits, particularly Negative Emotionality, are well-established correlates of internalizing symptoms. However, less is known about whether Disintegration, a proposed basic personality trait reflecting proneness to psychotic-like experiences and beliefs, accounts for additional individual differences in internalizing experiences in everyday life. The present study examined the incremental contribution of Disintegration beyond the Big Five to daily depression, anxiety, and rumination. A sample of 156 psychology students completed measures of Disintegration and the Big Five, followed by seven days of daily diary assessments (916 observations). Multilevel models were used to examine associations between personality traits and daily internalizing experiences. When all six personality traits were considered simultaneously, Disintegration was uniquely associated with higher daily depression (β = .25), anxiety (β = .24), and rumination (β = .17), alongside Negative Emotionality. Adding Disintegration to the Big Five increased explained variance by 10.25% for depression, 7.79% for anxiety, and 2.69% for rumination. Sensitivity analyses excluding potentially overlapping symptom-related content supported the robustness of the associations with depression and anxiety. These findings suggest that Disintegration contributes to explaining between-person differences in everyday internalizing experiences beyond established personality dimensions, extending evidence for its incremental validity to repeated daily assessments.

Beyond the Big Five: Disintegration as a Predictor of Daily Depression, Anxiety, and Rumination Introduction

Contemporary personality research has established robust associations between the Big Five, Neuroticism in particular, and internalizing outcomes such as anxiety, depression, and maladaptive cognitive processes, including rumination (Kotov et al., 2010; Lahey, 2009; Muris et al., 2005). Meta-analytical evidence showed that Neuroticism has consistently emerged as the personality dimension most strongly associated with internalizing psychopathology, whereas lower Extraversion and lower Conscientiousness have also shown consistent associations with a range of internalizing disorders (Kotov et al., 2010). Nevertheless, there is growing recognition, supported by meta-analytical studies, that the Five-Factor Model may not fully capture individual differences in psychologically relevant phenomena, particularly those involving unusual perceptual experiences, distorted beliefs, cognitive dysregulation, and fluctuations in affect (Knežević et al., 2016).

Disintegration has been proposed as a basic personality trait that complements the Big Five by capturing stable individual differences in biased reality testing and proneness to psychotic-like experiences and beliefs (PLEBs, Knežević et al., 2017 , 2022). Conceptually, Disintegration encompasses a broad range of tendencies, including magical thinking, perceptual distortions, paranoia, enhanced awareness, cognitive dysorganization, somatoform dysregulation, mania, apathy/depression, and flattened affect (Knežević et al., 2017). Meta-analytical and crosscultural evidence indicates that Disintegration-like phenomena are largely independent of the Big Five (Knežević et al., 2019), HEXACO (Knežević, Lazarević, et al., 2022; Scherhag et al., 2026), Cloninger's psychobiological model (Lazarević et al., 2016), and Eysenck's PEN traits (Knežević et al., 2019).

Disintegration also differs conceptually from PID-5 Psychoticism, another personalitybased conceptualization of psychosis proneness. Whereas Psychoticism is defined within the PID-5 as a domain of maladaptive personality functioning, Disintegration is conceptualized as a basic personality trait spanning both normal and maladaptive variation. The two constructs also differ in breadth: PID-5 Psychoticism primarily encompasses unusual beliefs and experiences, eccentricity, and perceptual dysregulation, whereas Disintegration represents a broader, ninefaceted disposition encompassing positive, negative, disorganized, affective, and somatoform manifestations of proneness to PLEBs (Knežević et al., 2017; 2022; Knežević, Lazarević & Zorić, 2022). Despite these conceptual differences, both constructs have been shown to predict momentary psychotic-like experiences in everyday life, supporting their shared relevance to psychosis proneness (Knežević, Lazarević, & Zorić, 2022).

Beyond its structural distinctiveness, evidence supports the incremental predictive validity of Disintegration beyond established personality dimensions across a range of psychological and behavioral outcomes (for a systematic review and meta-analytic evidence gap maps, see Scherhag & Bošnjak, 2026), including psychopathological symptoms (Ristić et al., 2024), irrational and conspiracy beliefs (Knežević et al., 2023; Stanković et al., 2022), questionable health behaviours (Knežević, Petrović, et al., 2024; Lazarević et al., 2023), depression and post-traumatic stress disorder (Knežević, Savić, Vermetten, & Vidaković, 2022), and mental and physical health outcomes (Lazarević et al., 2026). However, this evidence has been based predominantly on conventional between-person assessments, leaving considerably less known about whether Disintegration is associated with internalizing experiences as they occur in everyday life. This is an important limitation because internalizing experiences are not static characteristics but dynamic states that show considerable within-person variability over time (Bolger & Laurenceau, 2013; Moberly & Watkins, 2008). Diary and experience-sampling studies have demonstrated that stable personality characteristics are systematically associated with everyday emotional experiences. Research conceptualizing traits as density distributions of states suggests that stable traits are expressed through characteristic patterns of momentary behavior, cognition, and affect (Fleeson, 2001; Fleeson & Gallagher, 2009). Neuroticism, for example, is associated with more frequent experiences of negative affect and distress in daily life, whereas Extraversion is associated with greater positive affect and well-being (e.g., Komulainen et al., 2014; Kritzler et al., 2020; Wilt et al., 2012). Diary and experience-sampling methods are particularly well suited to studying such phenomena because they capture experiences close to their occurrence, reduce reliance on retrospective reports, and allow stable individual differences to be examined in relation to experiences occurring in everyday contexts (Bolger et al., 2003; Bolger & Laurenceau, 2013). Importantly, repeated assessments also allow researchers to estimate individuals' characteristic levels of everyday emotional experiences across multiple occasions, rather than relying on a single retrospective evaluation. This approach is particularly relevant when examining whether stable personality dispositions are associated with differences in the frequency or intensity of internalizing experiences in daily life. However, research on personality predictors of daily emotional experiences has largely focused on the Big Five, leaving open the question of whether Disintegration contributes explanatory power beyond established personality dimensions.

A complementary line of evidence comes from experience-sampling and diary research on psychotic experiences and schizotypy. Psychotic experiences and paranoia in daily life have been associated with negative affect (Peters et al., 2012; Riehle et al., 2025), lower momentary self-esteem (Daemen et al., 2022), and social anxiety (Chau et al., 2023). More broadly, experience-sampling research supports close dynamic associations among negative emotional states, stress sensitivity, self-esteem, emotion regulation, and psychotic experiences in everyday life. Most recently, Berglund et al. (2026) found that both positive and disorganized schizotypy were associated with greater use of rumination as an emotion-regulation strategy in daily life.

Together, these findings suggest that psychotic-like dispositions and experiences are relevant to everyday emotional functioning. However, previous studies have focused primarily on specific psychotic experiences or schizotypal dimensions rather than Disintegration as a broader personality disposition, have generally not examined its contribution alongside the Big Five, and have not simultaneously considered daily depression, anxiety, and rumination.

The present study addresses these gaps by examining whether Disintegration is associated with average levels of daily depression, anxiety, and rumination beyond the Big Five using a multilevel diary design. Specifically, we examine whether stable individual differences in proneness to PLEBs account for unique between-person differences in everyday internalizing experiences after accounting for established personality dimensions. From a theoretical perspective, the association between Disintegration and everyday internalizing experiences may reflect several features of this disposition. Tendencies towards unusual interpretations of experiences, suspiciousness, cognitive dysregulation, and affective instability may be relevant to how individuals experience and process everyday events. These characteristics could contribute to elevated levels of distress and repetitive negative thinking, even in the absence of clinically significant psychotic symptoms. Importantly, such associations need not be reducible to Negative Emotionality, as Disintegration encompasses cognitive and perceptual tendencies that are not central to traditional models of negative affectivity.

Method Sample and Procedure

We calculated that a sample of at least N = 130 would enable the detection of correlations 0.25, with desired power of .90, if the alpha level is set at .05. This ≥ calculation provided an estimate of statistical power to detect between-person relationships, which were of our primary interest. This number of entities at level two is far larger than the number found to lead to biased estimates of the secondlevel standard errors in multi-level random coefficient modeling (meaning a sample of 50 or less, Maas & Hox, 2005). Our sample size is also in line with recommendations from Gabriel et al. (2019), who, as a general rule of thumb, recommend at least 83 participants (Level 2) and a total of 835 data points (Level 1) for experience sampling analyses. Gabriel et al. (2019) further emphasize the importance of the Level 2 sample size, and the sample size indicated by our power calculation was well above their recommended minimum.

The sample consisted of psychology students who were invited to participate in person during regular classes and afterwards, via group email, received information about the research and a Google Docs link where they could leave their email address to receive further instructions, and participated for course credit. Initially, we recruited 209 participants for the retrospective assessment phase, and 195 of them at least started to fill out questionnaires for this phase, and 190 of them completed questionnaires on personality traits. Retrospective measures were administered first via the Lime Survey online app. Inclusion criteria were having a smartphone with access to the Internet and being aged between 18 and 25 years, leading to the exclusion of 8 participants from further analyses due to older age. To increase anonymity, participants did not provide their emails during this phase, and all initially recruited participants received an invitation for the daily diary part of the research. Data from retrospective assessments and daily diaries were merged using a unique code that participants created using predefined categories of letters and numbers. The final sample, consisting of those who completed both phases, consisted of N = 156 participants, with an average age of 20.7 years (SD = 1.21), and 92.3 % female. The compliance rate was 84 % (M = 5.9 out of 7 daily questionnaires completed, SD = 1.39, with 40% of participants who completed all 7 daily assessments), similar to what is reported in previous research (Wrzus & Neubauer, 2023).

Next, we collected daily assessments of rumination, depression, and anxiety using the SEMA3 smartphone app (O'Brien et al., 2024). Participants received a standard push notification once per day, at 9 PM, as a reminder, and could respond to the survey between 9 PM and 11.59 PM. They filled in the survey every day for 7 consecutive days. In total, we collected 916 daily diary recordings (data points). We did not impute missing data, and all available data were used for all analyses.

Variables and instruments

Criterion variables -daily assessments

Daily Depression. PHQ-2 (Löwe et al., 2005) was used as a measure of daily depression, with instructions and the rating scale adjusted for daily assessments. It consists of two items (little interest or pleasure in doing things; feeling down, depressed, or hopeless) from the PHQ-9 (Kroenke, Spitzer, & Williams, 2001). Participants were asked to rate the intensity of each symptom during the day using a scale ranging from 0 (not at all) to 3 (whole day). The internal consistency coefficient at the between-person level (where each item rating was aggregated within each participant before computation) was .76, and it was roughly in line with previously reported (de Barbaro et al., 2023).

Daily Anxiety.

Daily anxiety was measured with GAD-2 (Kroenke et al., 2007), and instructions and the rating scale were adjusted for daily assessments. It consists of two items (feeling nervous, anxious, or on edge; not being able to stop or control worrying) from the GAD-7 (Spitzer et al., 2007). Participants were asked to rate the intensity of each symptom during the day using a scale ranging from 0 (not at all) to 3 (whole day). Cronbach's alpha coefficient of aggregated item ratings was .88, and it was in line with previously reported (de Barbaro et al., 2023).

Daily Rumination. Momentary Ruminative Self-Focus Inventory -abbreviated (MRSI-

A; Connolly & Alloy, 2017) was used as a measure of rumination. It consists of three items, and the response scale ranges from 1 (completely agree) to 7 (disagree completely). The internal consistency coefficient of aggregated item ratings was .95, and it was in line with previously reported (Connolly & Alloy, 2017).

Personality traits -retrospective assessment

Big Five Traits. Big Five Inventory-2 (Soto & John, 2017) is a 60-item instrument that includes both domain and facet scales of Big Five personality dimensions, with each facet measured with four items, and each domain measured with 12 items. The response scale ranges from 1 (strongly disagree) to 5 (strongly agree). Cronbach's alpha reliabilities obtained in the study ranged between .80 for Agreeableness and .90 for the Negative Emotionality trait.

Disintegration. Delta-9 (Knežević et al., 2017) was used as a measure of disintegration.

It consists of 20 items, and the response scale ranges from 1 (completely inaccurate) to 5 (completely accurate). Internal consistency obtained was .90, and it was in line with metaanalytically estimated reliability (Tholking et al., 2024).

Analytical Strategy

We report descriptive statistics and reliability coefficients of all measures. To estimate the strength of the relationship between retrospectively measured variables and retrospective measures and aggregated diary measures, we used Pearson correlations. To explore the relations between daily and retrospective measures, we employed a multilevel approach, as the most appropriate method for hierarchically nested data (Nezlek, 2001). Multilevel random coefficient modeling (MRCM) is the most appropriate method for the dataset, as days of assessment (level 1) are nested within individuals (level 2). The main advantage of MRCM is the possibility of modeling random effects. That is, it enables calculating more accurate parameter estimates and tests of significance compared to Ordinary Least Squares (OLS), and enables decomposition of the overall variance into variance from stable factors (individual differences) and variance from unstable factors (intra-individual differences).

We tested several multilevel random coefficient models, with rumination, depression, and anxiety measured at level 1. To determine the proportion of variance attributable to stable individual differences versus within-person fluctuations, unconditional multilevel models were estimated for daily depression, anxiety, and rumination, and intraclass correlation coefficients (ICCs) were computed (Model 1).

To explore the best predictors of rumination, depression, and anxiety, we tested three separate hierarchical models (Models 2-4, respectively). Personality traits (i.e., DOCEAN traits) were entered as level-2 predictors, allowing us to examine whether stable individual differences predicted average levels of daily internalizing experiences.

All six DOCEAN personality dimensions were entered simultaneously to evaluate the incremental predictive validity of Disintegration beyond the Big Five personality traits. For completeness, additional models including each personality trait as the sole level-2 predictor were also estimated. To further quantify the incremental contribution of Disintegration, we compared the proportion of variance explained by models including only the Big Five personality traits (OCEAN) with that explained by models additionally including Disintegration (DOCEAN).

Sensitivity analyses were conducted to address the possibility of criterion contamination resulting from conceptual overlap between the personality measures and the outcome variables.

In the model predicting daily depression, depression-related facets were removed from both the Disintegration and Negative Emotionality composite scores. In the model predicting daily anxiety, anxiety-related facets were removed from the Negative Emotionality score. The modified personality scores were then entered into the same multilevel models to examine whether the observed effects persisted after reducing potential content overlap. The stability of the findings across these analyses was interpreted as evidence for the robustness of the incremental predictive validity of Disintegration.

All data manipulations and analyses were conducted using R (Version 4.6.0; R Core Team, 2026) in the RStudio environment (Version 2026.8.1.195; Posit Team, 2026). The following packages were used: tidyverse (Wickham et al., 2019), psych (Revelle, 2026), nlme (Pinheiro, Bates, & R Core Team, 2026), effectsize (Ben-Shachar, M. S., Lüdecke, D., & Makowski, D., 2020), and r2mlm (Shaw et al., 2023).

Discussion

The present study examined whether Disintegration contributes to the prediction of everyday internalizing experiences beyond the Big Five personality traits. Three findings are particularly noteworthy. First, there was considerable within-person variability in depression, anxiety, and rumination, confirming that these experiences fluctuate meaningfully across daily life. Second, Disintegration was consistently associated with all three outcomes and remained a unique predictor when considered simultaneously with the Big Five. Third, these associations were comparable in magnitude to those of Negative Emotionality, the personality trait most consistently implicated in internalizing psychopathology. Together, these findings extend previous evidence for the incremental validity of Disintegration across a range of psychological and health-related outcomes (see Scherhag & Bošnjak, 2026) to internalizing experiences measured repeatedly in everyday life.

The unique contribution of Disintegration is particularly noteworthy given the established role of Negative Emotionality/Neuroticism in internalizing psychopathology. Meta-analytic evidence identifies Neuroticism as the broad personality dimension most strongly associated with depressive and anxiety disorders (Kotov et al., 2010), and daily-life studies similarly show that individuals higher in Neuroticism experience higher average levels of negative affect in everyday life (Hisler et al., 2020). In the present study, Negative Emotionality was indeed associated with higher average daily depression, anxiety, and rumination. However, Disintegration showed associations of comparable magnitude and retained unique associations with all three outcomes when the personality dimensions were considered simultaneously. Given the moderate correlation between Disintegration and Negative Emotionality (r = .53), this pattern suggests that the two dimensions share some vulnerability-related variance but are not redundant in their associations with everyday internalizing experiences. This interpretation was further supported by comparisons of explained variance, which showed that including Disintegration alongside the Big Five increased the variance explained in all three daily outcomes, particularly depression and anxiety.

The pattern of results with the Big Five also provides further support for the conceptual distinctiveness of Disintegration. Although Disintegration was moderately associated with Negative Emotionality, its associations with the remaining Big Five dimensions were small to moderate, consistent with previous meta-analytic and cross-cultural evidence that Disintegration occupies personality space not adequately captured by the Big Five (Knežević et al., 2016 (Knežević et al., , 2017)).

This distinction is theoretically important because Disintegration captures a broader set of cognitive, perceptual, affective, and behavioral tendencies-including unusual beliefs and perceptions, suspiciousness, cognitive dysregulation, and disturbances in affective functioningthat are not central to the Big Five (Knežević et al., 2017). Such characteristics may represent an additional dispositional vulnerability to psychological distress. The present findings, therefore, suggest that everyday internalizing experiences are related not only to a general propensity toward negative affect but also to individual differences in how reality is perceived, interpreted, and cognitively processed. This extends the relevance of Disintegration beyond psychotic-like phenomena and highlights its potential contribution to understanding individual differences in everyday psychological distress.

The results also converge with an emerging literature linking psychosis-related characteristics to emotional functioning in everyday life. Experience-sampling studies have shown that psychotic experiences are associated with negative affect (Peters et al., 2012) and lower momentary self-esteem (Daemen et al., 2022), while paranoia and social anxiety appear to be dynamically related in daily life (Chau et al., 2023). Our findings extend this literature in two respects. First, rather than focusing on specific psychotic experiences or individual dimensions of schizotypy, we examined Disintegration as a broad personality disposition reflecting proneness to PLEBs. Second, we examined its contribution alongside the Big Five, demonstrating that its associations with everyday internalizing experiences cannot be reduced to established personality dimensions such as Negative Emotionality.

Rumination is particularly interesting in this respect. Unlike depression and anxiety, which represent affective symptom domains, rumination reflects a repetitive cognitive process implicated in the development and maintenance of a range of internalizing problems (Muris et al., 2005). The finding that Disintegration uniquely predicted average daily rumination, therefore, suggests that its relevance to internalizing experiences extends to repetitive negative thinking, rather than being confined to depressive and anxious affect. This interpretation converges with Berglund et al. (2026), who found that positive and disorganized schizotypy were associated with greater use of rumination in everyday life. One possibility is that cognitive dysregulation or difficulties disengaging from internally generated material contribute to the association between Disintegration and repetitive negative thinking. However, these mechanisms were not assessed in the present study and remain to be tested directly in future intensive longitudinal research.

The sensitivity analyses further strengthen the interpretation of the main findings.

Because both Disintegration and Negative Emotionality contain content conceptually related to internalizing symptoms, an important alternative explanation is that their associations with daily depression and anxiety simply reflect predictor-criterion content overlap. Removing depressionrelated content from Disintegration and Negative Emotionality had virtually no effect on the association between Disintegration and daily depression (β = .25 in the primary analysis vs. β = .24 in the sensitivity analysis). Similarly, its association with daily anxiety remained essentially unchanged in the sensitivity analysis. By contrast, the association between Negative Emotionality and depression was attenuated but remained statistically significant after depression-related content was removed. Although these analyses cannot completely eliminate concerns about conceptual overlap among personality and psychopathology constructs, they indicate that the unique association between Disintegration and daily internalizing experiences is unlikely to be explained solely by symptom-like content in the Disintegration measure.

More broadly, the findings have implications for the interface between personality and psychopathology research. Much of the literature linking broad personality traits to internalizing outcomes has understandably focused on Neuroticism/Negative Emotionality (Kotov et al., 2010; Lahey, 2009). The present findings do not challenge the importance of this dimension; rather, they suggest that it may not provide a complete account of dispositional vulnerability to everyday psychological distress. Extending traditional personality models with Disintegration may capture additional individual differences relevant to internalizing functioning, particularly those involving atypical cognition, perception, and reality testing. This interpretation is consistent with previous evidence that Disintegration contributes incremental information beyond established personality dimensions across a range of psychological and behavioral outcomes (see Scherhag & Bosnjak, 2026).

At the same time, the multilevel nature of the present findings should be interpreted carefully. Although depression, anxiety, and rumination showed considerable within-person variability, Disintegration and the Big Five were assessed as stable Level-2 characteristics. The present analyses therefore demonstrate that individuals higher in Disintegration reported higher average levels of depression, anxiety, and rumination across the diary period; they do not demonstrate that Disintegration predicts day-to-day changes in these experiences within individuals.

An important next step would be to examine whether Disintegration also moderates within-person processes-for example, whether individuals higher in Disintegration show stronger emotional responses to daily stressors, greater persistence of negative affect or rumination across successive assessments, or stronger coupling between rumination and subsequent anxiety or depressive affect. Such analyses would move beyond demonstrating dispositional differences in average daily functioning toward identifying the dynamic processes through which Disintegration may confer vulnerability.

Limitations

Several limitations of the present study should be acknowledged. First, all variables were assessed using self-report measures, raising the possibility of common-method variance and response biases (Podsakoff et al., 2003). Although diary methodology reduces retrospective recall bias by assessing experiences close to their occurrence, it does not eliminate biases associated with self-report. Future studies could complement diary reports with behavioral, physiological, or clinician-rated indicators of psychological functioning.

Second, the sample consisted predominantly of young female psychology students, which may limit the generalizability of the findings to other age groups, educational backgrounds, and gender distributions. At the same time, examining Disintegration dimensionally in a non-clinical sample is consistent with its conceptualization as a basic personality disposition rather than a marker restricted to psychopathology. Replication in clinical and more heterogeneous community samples would nevertheless be important for establishing the generalizability of the observed associations.

Third, the relatively short diary period may not fully capture longer-term patterns in emotional functioning. Although repeated daily assessments provide advantages over single retrospective measurements, a longer assessment period or multiple measurement bursts would provide a stronger basis for examining the stability of the observed associations across time.

Repeated assessment may itself also increase participants' awareness of their emotions and cognitions and thereby introduce measurement reactivity (Bolger et al., 2003).

Fourth, the observational design precludes causal conclusions. Personality traits were assessed prior to the diary period, but this temporal ordering does not establish that Disintegration causes subsequent depression, anxiety, or rumination. Moreover, the current models address between-person differences in average daily experiences rather than temporal relations among daily processes. Designs incorporating multiple assessments per day and lagged analyses could help clarify how psychosis-proneness, stress, rumination, and emotional symptoms unfold and influence one another over time.

Conclusion

The present study demonstrates that Disintegration is uniquely associated with everyday depression, anxiety, and rumination beyond the variance accounted for by the Big Five personality traits. Its associations were comparable in magnitude to those of Negative Emotionality and remained robust for depression and anxiety after reducing potential predictorcriterion content overlap. These findings extend previous evidence for the incremental validity of Disintegration from conventional between-person assessments of psychopathology to internalizing experiences repeatedly assessed in everyday life. More broadly, they suggest that dispositional vulnerability to psychological distress may encompass not only proneness to negative affect but also individual differences in atypical cognition and biased reality testing.

Incorporating Disintegration alongside established personality dimensions may therefore contribute to a more comprehensive account of the personality foundations of everyday emotional functioning.
