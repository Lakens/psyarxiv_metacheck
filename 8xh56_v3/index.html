How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Read more in the metacheck manual.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. A TRO uses them for each trov:TransparentResearchPerformance. * run_results$exit_status: the exit status of the process that ran the script (0 = ended normally); NA when the process did not report one (a time limit, or Docker failing to start the container). * artifacts: one row per file, with stage (deposit: as downloaded from the repository, hashed from the downloaded file; before_run: the run folder just before the first script ran, after metacheck’s own changes, such as rewritten paths and unpacked archives; after_run: only the files the scripts made (status new), changed (changed) or removed (removed), as an unchanged file is the one listed before the run), path (relative to the run folder, as valid UTF-8, a byte of another encoding written as <xx>), size (bytes), sha256, mime_type (by extension, NA when unknown), source (the file’s address in the repository, for deposit). The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). Files metacheck itself copied or made between passes (see modifications, output_copied, file_placed) show in after_run too. A TRO uses the rows as trov:ResearchArtifacts and each stage as a trov:ArtifactArrangement. * modifications$sha256_before, modifications$sha256_after: the hash of the changed script as downloaded and as it ran; NA when the script is not at the same path in both (a script made from an R Markdown file). * environment: the system that ran the code (trov:TransparentResearchSystem in a TRO): metacheck_version, started_at, ended_at, host (R version, platform, operating system), sandbox, network_isolated (TRUE only when every Docker run had --network none; the process sandbox does not stop the network), internet, timeout_per_script, total_timeout, python_local (the Python run outside Docker and its version), and docker: runs (for each kind of run, r_run, r_install, python_run, python_install, the image and the docker run arguments, such as --network none, --read-only, --cap-drop ALL, no-new-privileges, the process limit and the memory and processor limits) and images (for each image, its ID and digests from docker image inspect, its creation time and the R and Python versions its environment declares).

run_results$checked says what the run of each script shows about the code, in five groups, so that a script that does not reproduce is kept apart from one the check could not test: ran (it ran to the end); code_error (it did not reproduce: it stopped with an error of the code or the deposit, could not be read, or needs a file the deposit does not hold); dependency (a package or program was not available: a package that could not be installed or was not loaded, a program outside R and Python, a system library, or R crashing in a package’s compiled code); time (it did not finish within the time limit for one script, or was not started because the time for all scripts had run out); and not_checkable (it could not be checked automatically: it needs a person, the internet, a key or another setting of the authors’ computer, one platform such as Google Colab, Windows or OpenSAFELY, more memory than the run allows, a file that was not downloaded, a screen, or a program that runs it, or it is a file of a package of the code that Python runs only as a module of that package). It is NA for files run inside another script, files that are not an analysis, files not read, and a Docker failure. summary_table counts each group (repro_ran_n, repro_code_error_n, repro_dependency_n, repro_time_n, repro_not_checkable_n), and the report’s first sentence gives them. The traffic light reflects the static readiness signals by default; a script in code_error that stopped with an error makes it red, as a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure, and a script in one of the other three groups keeps it from being green. Calls that need a screen, which the run does not have (dev.print(), dev.copy2pdf(), dev.copy2eps(), savePlot(), windows(), win.graph(), x11(), quartz(), View()), are replaced before the run by calls that need none, which modifications records as screen_call_replaced. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

The RetractionWatch Check module compares the reference list against studies in the RetractionWatch database based on the DOI. If a study in the database is found, a reminder is provided that the study was retracted, has an expression of concern, or a correction.

The module requires that the reference has a DOI: the DOI in the paper’s reference list, or one looked up with add_bib_match() before the module runs (see ref_table()). References without a DOI are not checked.

It is possible the authors are already aware that a study was retracted, but the module can’t evaluate this.

The database can be manually updated with the rw_update function. For more information, see https://gitlab.com/crossref/retraction-watch-data.

How Replication Check works

This module checks references and warns for citations of original studies for which replication or reproduction studies exist in the FLoRA database.

The Replication Check module compares the reference list against studies in the FLoRA (FORRT Library of Replication Attempts) database based on the DOI. If a study in the database is found, a reminder is provided that a replication or reproduction of the original study exists, and should be cited. No reminder is given when the paper already cites that replication or reproduction (matched by its DOI).

The module requires that the reference has a DOI: the DOI in the paper’s reference list, or one looked up with add_bib_match() before the module runs (see ref_table()). References without a DOI are not checked.

It is possible the original study was cited for other reasons than the empirical claim tested, or that the replication/reproduction in the FLoRA database is for only one of the studies in the paper, and not the study the authors discuss.

The database can be manually updated with the FLoRA_update() function. For more information, see https://forrt.org/FLoRA/.

Abstract

Background: Neuroticism is known for being linked to detrimental outcomes, but it may also serve beneficial functions in adolescence, where avoiding and protecting oneself from risks becomes crucial.

We examined potential risks and benefits of neuroticism based on longitudinal data of two samples of Dutch adolescents, one population (n = 2229, 51% female) and one clinicreferred (n = 543, 34% female) sample. Neuroticism was measured with parent-ratings of the temperament facets fear and frustration and used to predict a diverse set of outcomes in the domains lifestyle choices (e.g., substance abuse) and social resources (e.g., social skills) between ages 11 to 19.

Results: High neuroticism conveyed heightened risk but three key observations refine this picture: (1) fear related to fewer risks than frustration and was additionally linked to benefits regarding better social skills and more prosocial behavior among adolescents in the clinicreferred sample; (2) both fear and frustration were consistently associated with parent-rated social behavior problems across measurement points, while other risk-related associations were less consistent; (3) most links between fear and detrimental outcomes became nonsignificant after controlling for childhood adversity (e.g., low socioeconomic status, parental divorce).

Although our results indicate several risk-related effects, we also provide initial evidence that the fear facet of neuroticism can be beneficial in adolescence, especially among youth with a clinical background and in the social domain. We call for more nuanced research studying the potential protective role of neuroticism within high-risk populations.

Risks and Benefits of Neuroticism in Adolescence: Longitudinal Evidence from Population and Clinic-Referred Samples

Previous research on neuroticism, the tendency to experience frequent and intense negative emotions (Barlow et al., 2014), and related constructs has focused almost exclusively on their negative outcomes. Across the lifespan, empirical findings consistently show that higher neuroticism is associated with poorer physical and mental health (e.g., Jeronimus et al., 2014; Laceulle et al., 2014), increased mortality in adulthood (Mroczek et al., 2009) as well as fewer and less satisfying social relationships (e.g., Mund & Neyer, 2014; Wagner et al., 2014; Winefield et al., 2015). This predominantly negative portrayal may overlook potentially adaptive benefits of high neuroticism, however, often summarized as a "better safe than sorry" strategy (Van den Bergh et al., 2021). This protective function of neuroticism could be particularly relevant during adolescence, an age period where neuroticism levels tend to increase (e.g., Borghuis et al., 2020) and individuals face accumulating developmental challenges and health risks (Casey et al., 2011; Steinberg, 2007). To date, however, potential benefits of neuroticism in adolescence remain largely unexplored.

The current study addresses this research gap using longitudinal data from two adolescent samples: a population sample and a clinic-referred sample from the TRacking Adolescents' Individual Lives Survey (TRAILS). Using parent-ratings at age 11, we investigated how fear and frustration, two key facets of neuroticism, relate to outcomes in two domains that pose a challenge in modern adolescent life: lifestyle choices (e.g., substance use) and social resources (e.g., social skills). We tested cross-sectional associations and prospective effects on outcomes measured at different ages between early and late adolescence (ages 11 to 19). Guided by an evolutionary-developmental perspective (Ellis et al., 2023), we further investigated group differences with regard to childhood adversity (e.g. parental divorce, low socioeconomic status), gender, and sample type (population versus clinic-referred samples), to refine when and for whom neuroticism may pose a risk, have a neutral effect, or confer a benefit.

Understanding Neuroticism: From its Conceptual Origins to Evolutionary Accounts

First introduced by Eysenck (1947), "neuroticism" is derived from the clinical term "neurosis", which historically referred to a range of disorders associated with anxiety and depression (Barlow et al., 2014). Over time and across research traditions, different conceptually related constructs have emerged. These include broad personality traits such as (low) emotional stability (Ashton & Lee, 2001), affective styles such as trait anxiety (Lee et al., 2006) and emotional reactivity (Kuppens et al., 2007), as well as child temperament facets of negative emotionality, including fear and frustration (Rothbart, 2007; Rothbart & Bates, 2006). Whereas these constructs differ in scope and emphasis, they all capture relatively stable individual differences in the experience and regulation of negative emotions and thus reflect core features of neuroticism (Barlow et al., 2024; BLINDED). With regard to temperament and personality, research further highlights temporal continuity (Soto & Tackett, 2015). For these reasons, we draw on literature referring to all these constructs when discussing the potential risks and benefits of neuroticism.

Consistent with its historical roots in psychopathology, neuroticism has typically been viewed as a risk factor for maladjustment. Research has linked neuroticism with different undesirable outcomes, leading scholars to argue that neuroticism is the most consequential personality trait for public health (Lahey, 2009). Despite this evidence, the persistence of substantial individual differences in neuroticism raises an important question: Why would a trait associated with so many risks remain so common? Evolutionary accounts provide a compelling answer: individual differences in feelings, thoughts, and behaviors (i.e., personality traits) can be conceptualized as adaptive responses to recurring problems in humans' environments (Buss & Penke, 2015; Nettle, 2006).

From this functional perspective, neuroticism may reflect an evolved strategy to detect and react to potential risks, thereby facilitating survival in dangerous ecological contexts (Denissen & Penke, 2008a , 2008a; Nesse, 1991; Watson & Casillas, 2003). Importantly, this perspective does not suggest that neuroticism is beneficial in a general sense. Rather, neuroticism may be better understood as a strategy that prioritizes the avoidance or attenuation of costly negative outcomes over the maximization of gains. For example, individuals higher in neuroticism may be less likely to underestimate dangerous situations that could cause severe injuries or death, even if that heightened vigilance comes at the cost of elevated anxiety and stress. Consequently, although negative feelings related to higher neuroticism can be costly, a "better safe than sorry" strategy may be crucial in certain situations, environments, or developmental phases.

Adolescence as an Important Developmental Context for Risks and Benefits of Neuroticism

Whereas neuroticism generally declines from childhood to adulthood (Roberts et al., 2006), the disruption hypothesis proposes temporary increases during adolescence (Soto et al., 2011). Although researchers continue to debate the precise timing and duration of this increase, a growing body of longitudinal research provides support for such temporary neuroticism surges (Borghuis et al., 2017; Gillespie et al., 2024; van den Akker et al., 2014).

From an evolutionary perspective, adolescence may be a period in which potential benefits of neuroticism are particularly salient. Historically, adolescence has been one of the most challenging stages of human development, where individuals must learn to navigate life with increasing autonomy despite lacking the experience, resources, and skills of adults (De Waal, 2022; Steinberg, 2005). Although many dangers, such as violence and starvation, have been mitigated in industrialized societies, adolescence remains associated with disproportionately high rates of injuries, illness, and mortality relative to other developmental periods (Casey et al., 2011). For some adolescents, elevated levels of neuroticism and the associated vigilance toward potential risks may act as an attenuative or even protective factor. Drawing on this perspective, we focus on two life domains in which neuroticism may be associated with beneficial outcomes during adolescence.

The first domain concerns lifestyle choices. Specifically, adolescence is a time where many behaviors are established that contribute to non-communicable diseases in adulthood (Ellis et al., 2012; Sawyer et al., 2012; Steinberg, 2005). This includes healthy behaviors related to adolescents' diet and sport routine, but also unhealthy behaviors related to drug consumption or self-harm. Additionally, experimentation with sexuality and romantic relationships bears the risk of transmissible diseases, unwanted pregnancy, and emotional distress. Theoretically, tendencies toward worry and anticipation of potential problems (Buss & Penke, 2015) may help adolescents with higher neuroticism to reduce the likelihood of harmful outcomes.

The second domain concerns social resources. Adolescents strive to fit in socially and prepare for adult roles, while disconnecting from important others represents an elementary risk (Blakemore, 2012; Smetana et al., 2006; Steinberg, 2005). Adolescents must acquire interpersonal skills and behaviors that help to become socially accepted by family, teachers, and peer groups (Blakemore, 2012). The desire to belong to a social group is perhaps most evident within peer relationships, where adolescents establish their status as friend, outsider, or bully (Smetana et al., 2006). Theoretically, social emotions like shame, guilt, and jealousy could help adolescents with higher neuroticism to detect and avoid cues of social exclusion and engage in behaviors aimed at preserving at least a minimum of social resources (Baumeister & Tice, 1990; Denissen & Penke, 2008b).

Empirical studies on potential benefits of neuroticism in adolescence remain limited.

Regarding adolescents' lifestyle choices, initial evidence comes from a large (N = 4070) longitudinal birth-cohort study in the UK, which found that adolescents higher in anxiety were less likely to experience fatal accidents before the age of 25 (Lee et al., 2006).

Moreover, studies focusing on self-reported substance abuse in early and mid-adolescence have shown that higher emotionality-nervousness was related to lower levels of smoking and drinking on the cross-sectional level (Engels et al., 2006) and that higher harm-avoidance predicted later onsets of drug use (Masse & Tremblay, 1997). Regarding adolescents' social resources, a cross-sectional study among Dutch adolescents found that individuals with higher emotionality also reported more prosocial behavior (Pronk et al., 2021).

In sum, some previous research provides initial evidence for beneficial associations of adolescent neuroticism. However, this work has examined a limited set of outcomes and relies largely on cross-sectional self-reports, leaving the prospective associations and developmental timing of potential benefits unclear (cf. Lee et al., 2006; also see BLINDED). Most previous studies have also typically measured adolescent neuroticism either very broadly (e.g., Big Five neuroticism) or through a single characteristic (e.g., trait anxiety), potentially obscuring differences among its facets. For example, genetic research in adults found that higher overall scores on neuroticism were linked to shorter life expectancy, but if researchers zoomed into the anxiety/tension and worry/vulnerability facets specifically, these were associated with better health and longevity (Hill et al., 2020). Such findings suggest that understanding the role of neuroticism in adolescent development, including its potential benefits, may require to examine whether its lower-order facets have distinct, or even divergent, associations with central life outcomes. To address this issue, we distinguished between the temperament facets fear and frustration, which are the two most central and evolutionary primary facets of negative affectivity, the temperament equivalent of neuroticism (Rothbart, 2007; Rothbart & Bates, 2006). Fear captures the propensity to experience negative affect when anticipating stressful events, whereas frustration describes the tendency to experience negative affect in reaction to being interrupted in an ongoing task or blocked in one's goals. We explored their respective prospective associations with a broad range of cross-sectional and prospective outcomes in the domains of lifestyle choices and social resources.

Moderating Ecological Conditions: Childhood Adversity and Gender

Evolutionary accounts suggest that the protective role and related benefits of high neuroticism are most salient when adolescents grow up in dangerous and unpredictable environments (Buss & Penke, 2015; Nettle, 2006). Complementing this functional perspective, developmental frameworks such as Relational Developmental Systems (Lerner & Overton, 2008) and Dynamic Systems Theory (van Geert, 2011) emphasize that individual traits such as neuroticism may emerge through ongoing person-environment transactions and, under some conditions, contribute to reducing exposure to negative experiences (e.g., encountering fewer conflicts or detecting problems earlier). Accordingly, neuroticism may function as what Ellis et al. (2023) describe as a "hidden talent" in high-stakes contexts, helping individuals to face and handle risk, threat, stress, and loss. Together, these evolutionary and developmental approaches suggest that the attenuative or beneficial role of neuroticism should not be uniform across adolescents but instead depend on moderating ecological conditions (Bronfenbrenner, 1986; Lerner & Overton, 2008; also see BLINDED).

In this study, we considered two potential moderators: childhood adversity and gender.

First, higher neuroticism is likely to be more beneficial for adolescents who already accumulated adverse experiences in their childhood and who currently live under adverse conditions (Boyce & Ellis, 2005). Individuals with such backgrounds tend to encounter more challenges in life, such as greater likelihood of risk behaviors and social difficulties (Layne et al., 2014). Meanwhile, such adolescents often have fewer resources to cope with these challenges, such as social support. Consequently, the suit of feelings, thoughts and behaviors associated with heightened carefulness and stronger avoidance of potential risks may prove especially beneficial in such contexts. Therefore, potential benefits of neuroticism may be more pronounced among adolescents who have experienced childhood adversities such as parental divorce or prolonged separation from family due to an illness, as well as among those currently living under adverse or stressful circumstances, such as very low family socioeconomic status (SES), or child abuse and neglect.

Second, research on personality development suggests that the age-normative increases in neuroticism may be stronger for adolescent girls than for boys (Borghuis et al., 2017). This divergence in trait development may correspond with gender differences in adaptive benefits of neuroticism. Evolutionary theories suggest that, because females have historically faced greater reproductive costs and greater vulnerability to certain forms of risk (e.g., sexual violence), characteristics related to caution and threat sensitivity may have provided greater benefits for women than for men (Buss & Penke, 2015). For men, by contrast, aggressive and risk-taking strategies may at times yield higher returns in terms of social status attainment, potentially translating into stronger fitness benefits (De Waal, 2022; Ellis et al., 2012). Hence, potential risk attenuation or benefits of high neuroticism could be larger among adolescent girls than among adolescent boys. To our knowledge, moderating effects on the association between neuroticism and potential risks and benefits in adolescence have never been studied.

Present Study

The potential benefits of neuroticism remain an understudied aspect of adolescent development. We addressed this gap in the literature with four research questions. Our primary aim and first research question (RQ1) was to investigate whether higher fear and frustration in adolescence relate to benefits in lifestyle choices and social resources. Given the lack of previous research in this area, the rapid changes in adolescent development (Borghuis et al., 2017; Steinberg, 2005), and the possibility that some associations may manifest with a delay (Nesse, 2005), we did not specify hypotheses regarding where (i.e., specific outcomes) or when (i.e., age) benefits might occur. Instead, we examined cross-sectional and prospective associations with several outcomes in the domains of lifestyle choices (e.g., substance use) and social resources (e.g., friend nominations) assessed from ages 11 to 19. As second research question (RQ2), we further examined whether associations of fear and frustration with these outcomes were moderated by adolescents' degree of childhood adversity (e.g., experience of parental divorce, family with low socioeconomic status). Because theoretical accounts propose that the protective role of neuroticism is most likely to emerge in situations of actual risks and costs (Boyce & Ellis, 2005; Buss & Penke, 2015; Ellis et al., 2012 Ellis et al., , 2023;; Nettle, 2006), we hypothesized more pronounced benefits among individuals with higher childhood adversity. Our third research question (RQ3) concerned gender differences. We hypothesized that the benefits associated with higher neuroticism would be more pronounced among girls than boys. Finally, as a fourth research question (RQ4), we first examined RQ1-RQ3 among adolescents from the general population and then repeated these analyses among adolescents with a clinical background. This follow-up allowed us to explore whether benefits or attenuations of risks related to neuroticism were more pronounced among adolescents who were particularly vulnerable with regard to mental health, as suggested by theories on the adaptive value of threat sensitivity in high-risk contexts (Ellis et al., 2023).

To address these questions, we leveraged data of one population and one clinicreferred sample from TRAILS, a longitudinal study conducted in the Netherlands (Huisman et al., 2008; Oldehinkel et al., 2015). We examined parent-rated fear and frustration at age 11 in relation to predict outcomes assessed from ages 11 and 19 and captured by adolescent, parent, teacher, and peer ratings as well as experimental tasks. Materials and Methods

This study used data from Waves 1-4 of TRAILS, an ongoing prospective cohort study with biennial assessments and seven assessment waves so far. Full descriptions of participants, procedures, and data accessibility can be obtained from the cohort profile reports of TRAILS (Huisman et al., 2008; Oldehinkel et al., 2015). We report on how we determined the sample size and all study measures. Since we use existing data, the present study is considered a secondary analysis of existing data. All hypotheses and analyses of the current article were pre-registered; the preregistration; analysis code and supplemental materials are available at the Open Science Framework (OSF): https://osf.io/eq8um/overview?view_only=9520c35ea9a044e6bc83963db6c2a518.

Procedure

Ethical approval for data collection in TRAILS was granted by the Dutch Central Committee on Research Involving Human Subjects. The participants were treated in compliance with the ethical standards outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki by the World Medical Association (WMA), and all measurements were carried out with their adequate understanding and written consent. At each measurement of TRAILS, a combination of questionnaires completed by adolescents, their parents, teachers, or peers were used. These assessments were supplemented by interviews, physical measurements, and, in some waves, neuropsychological testing or peer ratings in the classroom.

Participants

TRAILS started in 2000 with N = 2230 adolescents from the Netherlands (population sample; response rate 76%, MAge 11.1, SD = 0.6, 51% girls; Oldehinkel et al., 2015). The response rates of the second, third, and fourth waves in this cohort were 96% (N = 2149, MAge = 13.6 years, SD = 0.5, 51% girls), 81% (N = 1816, MAge = 16.3 years, SD = 0.7, 52% girls), and 83% (N = 1881, MAge = 19.1 years, SD = 0.6, 52% women), respectively. Based on the variable selection in this study, we used data from all except one participant, resulting in N = 2229 participants in the population sample at Wave 1. From 2004 on, TRAILS additionally collected information from N = 543 adolescents who have been referred to a child psychiatric outpatient clinic in the Northern Netherlands before the age of 11 (clinic-referred sample; (response rate 43%, MAge = 11.1, SD = 0.5, 34% girls; Oldehinkel et al., 2015).

The clinicreferred sample comprises individuals who have been referred to a child psychiatric outpatient clinic in the Northern Netherlands before the age of 11. Boys were overrepresented since they made up the largest portion in the most prevalent diagnostic groups in the clinic (i.e., attention deficit/hyperactivity disorder, disruptive behavior, and autism spectrum disorders). All participants of the clinic-referred sample participating at Wave 1 were included in the current study. Response rates were a bit lower than in the population sample, with 85% (N = 462, MAge = 12.8 years, SD = 0.6, 34% girls), 77% (N = 419, MAge = 15.9 years, SD = 0.7, 34% girls), and 78% (N = 422, MAge = 19.1 years, SD = 0.7, 34% women) at the second, third, and fourth wave, respectively.

Corresponding to the mean ages at each measurement point, we denote the first wave as "age 11", the second wave as "age 13", the third wave as "age 16", and the fourth wave as "age 19" in the remainder of this article. Additional information on longitudinal selectivity can be found in the supplementary materials (Appendix A).

Measures

Most variables were measured in both samples (see Figure 1). If there were any differences in the variables or the way of their assessment, we explicitly describe this in the following.

Neuroticism Facets

The neuroticism facets at age 11 were measured with parent reports of fear and frustration using the Dutch version of the short form of the Early Adolescent Temperament Questionnaire-Revised (EATQ-R; Hartman, 2000; Putnam et al., 2001). Parent reports are a valid and the most frequently used method for assessing temperament and personality in childhood and early adolescence (Caspi & Shiner, 2008; Rothbart & Bates, 2006). The fear and frustration scales consisted of five items each and included ratings on adolescents' tendency to feeling scared when entering a darkened room at night (fear; Cronbach's  = .63) or getting irritated when being criticized (frustration;  = .74). Each item was rated on a fivepoint scale ranging from 1 (almost never untrue) to 5 (almost always true). We used the parent version because its factor structure is superior to that of the child version in TRAILS (Oldehinkel et al., 2004).

At age 16, parents repeated the assessment of their children's personality, using the same measure of fear ( = .66) and frustration ( = .75) as at age 11. In addition, adolescents provided self-reports of their neuroticism using the angry hostility, impulsivity, and vulnerability subscales of the revised NEO Personality Inventory (NEO-PI-R; Costa & McCrae, 1992). Answers were given on a five-point scale ranging from 1 (almost never untrue) to 5 (almost always true). For this study, we summarized the 24 items of the subscales to one overall neuroticism score ( = .81).

Lifestyle Choices

Over the course of the TRAILS study, adolescents and their parents provided information on six variables that reflect several adolescent lifestyle choices. If not specified otherwise, the same variables were measured in the population and clinic-referred sample.

Breakfast Frequency

At ages 16 and 19, adolescents provided information on their diet, including the frequency of having breakfast per week. In adolescence, irregular breakfasts are considered a risk for eating disorders and obesity, whereas regular breakfasts are considered as an indicator of healthy lifestyle (Hallström et al., 2013; Keski-Rahkonen et al., 2003). At age 16, answers were provided on a 5-point scale (never, less than once a week, 1 to 3 days per week, 4 to 6 days per week, every day). At age 19, a dichotomous measure was used (less than once a week vs. once a week or more often).

Sport Frequency

At ages 16 and 19, adolescents in the population sample reported on their sport frequency. The information assessed included the total time spent on sports in minutes and the number of times doing sport per week. We z-standardized both variables and then combined them in a composite score.

Substance Use

At ages 13, 16, and 19, adolescents reported on their substance use by indicating whether they consumed a certain substance "not once" or "once or more often". Within this survey, adolescents provided information on their alcohol, tobacco and cannabis use. Other drugs (e.g., amphetamines, cocaine, XTC) were left out of the analyses because previous research using TRAILS data indicated that only very few participants (4%) have used these drugs (van Hemel-Ruiter et al., 2013). Alcohol consumption was measured based on yearly and monthly consumption, both in terms of overall consumption and in terms of getting drunk. Tobacco use was measured based on monthly and daily consumption, whereas cannabis use was measured based on yearly and monthly consumption. We uniformly coded the prevalence data for each indicator across different time scales, with values ranging from no (0) to high (2) substance use. Depending on the original scale, the latter referred to daily versus monthly or monthly versus yearly consumption, with the shorter time frames implying more frequent substance use. Next, we z-standardized values for general alcohol consumption and drunkenness, calculated the average of the two values, and thus obtained a single indictor of alcohol consumption. In a final step, we z-standardized the three indicators of alcohol, tobacco, and cannabis consumption and combined them into a composite index of general substance use.

Self-Harming Behavior

At ages 11, 13, 16, and 19, adolescents reported on their frequency of self-harming behavior with one item from the Youth/Adult Self-Report (Achenbach, 1991; "I deliberately try to hurt or kill myself"). Answers were given on a 3-point scale (not at all, a little or sometimes, clearly or often).

Sexual Experience

At ages 13, 16, and 19, adolescents reported on their sexual experience. At ages 13 and 16, we used a dichotomous variable indicating no experience with sexual intercourse (0) or previous experience with intercourse (1). At age 19, we computed a continuous variable based on adolescents' reports of their age at first sexual intercourse. Specifically, we subtracted the age of first sexual intercourse from adolescents' current age plus one (i.e., 19 +1 = 20), resulting in an index reflecting adolescents' sexual experience in years (0 years if they have not yet had intercourse; 1 year if they had their first sexual intercourse at age 19; 2 years if they had their first sexual intercourse at age 18, and so on).

Partner Number

Furthermore, at age 19, adolescents reported on their lifetime partner number. Specifically, they were asked to indicate how many people they had been in a romantic relationship with so far.

Social Resources

To obtain information on variables related to adolescents' social resources from multiple perspectives and social contexts, reports provided by adolescents themselves, their parents, teachers, and classmates were used. As for lifestyle choices, we investigated six variables. If not specified otherwise, the same variables were measured in the population and clinic-referred sample.

Social Behavior Problems

At ages 11, 13, 16, and 19, parents rated adolescents' social behavior problems with the children's social behavior questionnaire (Hartman et al., 2006; Luteijn et al., 2000). The questionnaire consists of 49 items, which cover behaviors and emotions that are not optimally tuned to the social situation, reduced contact and social interest, difficulties in understanding social information, fear of and resistance to changes, stereotyped behavior, and orientation problems in time, place, or activity. All items were rated by adolescent's parents with regard to the frequency of their occurrence on a 3-point scale (not at all, a little or sometimes, clearly or often) and summarized as a composite score (age 11:  =. 92; age 13:  = .94; age 16:  =.93; age 19:  = .95).

Prosocial Behavior

At ages 11, 13, and 16, teachers rated adolescents' prosocial behavior with the prosocial behavior questionnaire (Tremblay et al., 1992). The questionnaire included 11 items on adolescents' prosocial behaviors towards their peers, such as helping them or trying to stop arguments. All items were rated by adolescent's teachers with regard to the frequency of their occurrence on a 5-point scale (never, almost never, sometimes, almost always, always) and summarized as a composite score (age 11:  = .93; age 13:  = .92; age 16:  = .93).

Social Skills

At age 11, teachers additionally rated adolescents' social skills using the social skill rating system (Gresham & Elliot, 1990). The rating system included 30 items capturing cooperation with the teacher, assertion, and self-control. All items were rated by adolescents' teachers with regard to the frequency of their occurrence on a 3-point scale (never, sometimes, always) and summarized as a composite score (= .95).

Face Processing

At age 11, adolescents took part in the Amsterdam Neuropsychological Tasks (ANT; De Sonneville, 1999), which tests for an individual's ability to process facial information (i.e., face recognition and identification of facial emotions). In the face recognition task, a picture of a face with neutral expression was presented for 2.5 seconds, followed by four pictures of faces. The task was to press the yes button if the first picture was among them (target trial), and the no button if it was not (non-target trial). There were 20 target and 20 non-target trials.

The identification of facial emotions task consisted of six parts, measuring adolescents' ability to identify the following emotions: 'happy', 'sad', 'angry', 'fearful', 'surprised' and 'disgusted'. The task was to press the yes button if an emotion was shown (target trial), and the no button if it was not (non-target trial). There were 20 target and 20 non-target trials for each emotion. In both tasks, the output variables were the average number of errors and the average response time per response type (i.e., hits, correct rejections, false alarms, and misses). For this study, we computed a composite index based on the z-standardized percentage of errors (i.e., false alarms and misses) and the z-standardized response times for correct answers (i.e., hits and correct rejections) in each task, whereby the values of the different emotions from the identification of facial emotions task were averaged beforehand.

To facilitate interpretation, the index was coded such that higher values indicate higher ability to process facial information.

Friend and Bully Nominations

At ages 11 and 13, adolescents in the population sample and their classmates provided peer nominations within their classes. Since peer nominations were only assessed in classrooms with at least ten TRAILS participants, this type of information was only available for a subsample of adolescents who attended regular elementary schools and did not repeat grades (N = 1065 at age 11). In contrast, children in special education (5.6 percent of the sample), small schools (6.4 percent) and children who repeated a grade (16.9 percent) or skipped a grade (2.2 percent) were excluded from this type of data collection (see Lorijn et al., 2022). Based on the peer nominations, we computed two indices, indicating the frequencies of being nominated as a friend ("Which of your classmates are your best friends?") or as a bully ("Which of your classmates are bullying you?").

Moderating Variables

Childhood Adversity

To capture adolescents' childhood adversity, we calculated an index based on past experiences and their current living conditions at age 11. The index was based on a range of variables that we extracted from adolescents' demographic questionnaire or from the developmental history interview that their parents completed. In the population sample, 9 specific variables were coded regarding whether they apply or not (0 = No,1 = Yes) and then summed up to a childhood adversity index ranging from 0 to 9, with higher values indicating higher degrees of adversity: low SES (i.e., among the lowest 30%; the family of origin's SES in TRAILS is a composite of five z-scored continuous variables, including professional occupation and educational attainment of both parents/guardians and household income.), migration background (first or second generation), as well as a number of experiences in adolescents' past between ages 0-11, namely parental illness (either mother or father), parental divorce, accidents, diagnosis of common disorders (including physical and mental illness of the adolescents), death of a dear one (family or other close others), and long stays away from home. In addition, based on ratings of adolescents' home environments provided by the interviewers of TRAILS, we will code whether adolescents grew up in an adverse environment (i.e., among the lowest 30%; the home environment ratings were summarized to a composite of four z-scored continuous variables, including atmosphere, dirt, luxury, and space).

In the clinic-referred sample, only few participants had sufficient information regarding accidents (n = 260 participants with complete data), diagnosis of common disorders (n = 203), and growing up in an adverse environment (n = 31). Therefore, a slight variation of the childhood adversity index was used in the clinic-referred sample, which was based on six out of the nine specific variables (low SES, migration background, parental illness, parental divorce, death of a dear one, and stays from home) and ranged from 0 to 6.

Gender

Adolescent gender was obtained as part of the demographic assessment. In this study, it was coded as 0 (male) and 1 (female).

Analysis

All analyses were performed with R Version 4.5.3, using RStudio (Posit team, 2026).

Before running the models, we z-standardized all variables. To answer our research questions, we used a set of manifest path models predicting outcomes regarding lifestyle choices and social resources from adolescents' fear and frustration. All models were specified in parallel for the two facets. Within each model set, separate models were estimated for each measurement point, resulting in cross-sectional models for outcomes at age 11, and longitudinal models for outcomes at ages 13, 16, and 19. This approach yielded nuanced information about how associations differed depending on the specific predictor and age at outcome assessment (Beck & Jackson, 2022). All model sets were first tested in the population sample and then ran again in the clinic-referred sample.

To address the first research question, we set up four basic path models using adolescents' fear/frustration scores at age 11 to predict individual differences in lifestyle choices and social resources between ages 11 and 19 (see Figure 1), considering all outcomes of one measurement point within the same model (Model Set 1). For the second research question, we extended the first model to account for the moderating role of childhood adversity. To this end, childhood adversity and the interaction between fear/frustration and childhood adversity were entered as predictors in two steps. First, the main effect of childhood adversity was included and then the moderation was tested. Finally, addressing our third research question regarding gender effects, we extended the first model set to multigroup models and estimated separate effects for female and male adolescents. In the population sample, all models at each measurement point had seven outcomes. In the clinic-referred sample, the models at ages 11 and 13 had five outcomes, while those at ages 16 and 19 had six outcomes (see Figure 1).

Power and Effect Sizes

To determine the power in our study, we conducted an a priori power analysis in G*Power (Faul et al., 2007). Based on our most complex path model in the population sample, we estimated the power for a multiple regression model with three predictors (fear or frustration, childhood adversity, and interaction between fear or frustration and childhood adversity) and with seven outcomes, thus setting the alpha error probability to .05/7 = .007 (Dunn, 1961). Results of the power analysis suggested that a sample size of N = 828 is required to detect small effects (f² = 0.02) with 80% power. The population sample exceeds this number of participants at all measurement points and should therefore provide sufficient statistical power to run our models. The clinic-referred sample, which was smaller than the population sample, was used for follow-up analyses that tested the research questions in a more specific, theoretically relevant group of adolescents.

Based on common effect sizes in psychological research (Funder & Ozer, 2019), we classified correlations (r) and betas (β) as very small if they were between .05 and .09, small between .10 and .19, moderate between .20 and .29, and large from .30. We report effects with all effect sizes, as even very small effects may be consequential (Funder & Ozer, 2019).

Robustness Checks

No data was excluded. As we conducted separate models per measurement point, only data of those adolescents participating in the corresponding wave were included. Due to the study set-up, participants could have complete data in one survey mode (e.g., self-reports), but missing values in another (e.g., peer nominations; see Table 1). These missing values were handled by FIML. To control for multiple testing, we only interpreted effects that were still statistically significant after adjusting p-values using a false discovery rate (Benjamini & Hochberg, 1995), accounting for all effects across measurement waves estimated per model set (e.g., 7 outcomes × 4 waves = 28 effects in Model Set 1 in the population sample). Exact estimates and p-values are provided in the supplementary materials.

Testing for Gender Differences

At age 11, adolescent girls in both samples scored higher on fear on average (ds: 0.19-0.21; p < .05), and boys scored higher on frustration, but only in the population sample (d = 0.15; p < .05). However, multi-group models provided no evidence that fear or frustration related to adolescents' lifestyle choices or social resources differently by gender. Across neuroticism facets and measurement points, models with gender-specific estimates showed no improved fit over those with identical estimates in both samples (see supplementary Tables E1 and E2). Since we preregistered these analyses, we report gender-specific estimates (see supplementary Tables E3 and E4), but refrain from further interpretation.

Exploratory Follow-Up Analyses

As a first exploratory robustness check, we repeated all longitudinal analyses addressing RQ1 (i.e., prospective models predicting outcomes at ages 13, 16, and 19), while controlling the earliest available measurement of each outcome where available. A detailed description of the analyses and results is provided in supplementary Tables F1-F7. After adjustment, the prospective associations of fear at age 11 with lower substance use at age 19 in the population sample and higher prosocial behavior at age 16 in the clinic-referred sample remained robust. In contrast, the proportion of prospective associations pointing to neuroticism as a risk-factor was substantially reduced across facets and samples. For fear, this proportion was reduced across outcome domains from 29% to 5% in the population sample and from 18% to 0% in the clinic-referred sample. For frustration, it decreased from 76% to 24% in the population sample and from 24% to 6% in the clinic-referred sample. This first set of exploratory analyses support the robustness of beneficial associations related to fear at age 11, whereas fewer risk-related associations of both facets were evident in the adjusted models.

In a second exploratory analysis, we examined associations with outcomes assessed from age 16 onward, replacing the parent ratings of fear and frustration at age 11 with either parent ratings of the same facets at age 16 or adolescents' self-reports of Big Five neuroticism at age 16 1 . Full results are reported in supplementary Tables G2 to G15. Both parent-and selfreports at age 16 showed more risk-related associations than fear or frustration at age 11.

Across parent-and self-rated measures, the proportion of risk-related associations with outcomes between ages 16 and 19 increased from 43% to 69% in the population sample, and from 17% to 39% in the clinic-referred sample. The beneficial associations observed for fear at age 11 were not significant in the age-16 analyses. Otherwise, the results largely resembled those using the age-11 predictors, with fewer risk-related patterns in the clinic-referred than in the population sample, fear being linked to fewer risks than frustration (and Big Five neuroticism), and the most consistent risk-related association concerning social behavior problems. As before, controlling for childhood adversity substantially reduced the proportion of risk-related associations, with little evidence for moderating by childhood adversity or gender.

Discussion

To what extent is neuroticism associated with both risks and benefits during adolescence? To address this question, the current study examined how fear and frustration, as two key facets of neuroticism, were associated with a broad range of adolescent lifestyle choices and social resources. Using multi-method data from a population sample and from a clinic-referred sample followed from age 11 to 19, we found that higher levels of fear and frustration were associated with several risks in adolescent development, consistent with previous research (Aldinger et al., 2014; Wagner et al., 2014; Wieczorek et al., 2021; Zara & Farrington, 2010). Nevertheless, several findings challenged the uniformly maladaptive perspective on neuroticism, pointing to a more nuanced picture. The Facets Fear and Frustration Show Distinct Associations with Risks and Benefits

The present findings show that the associations between neuroticism and outcomes in adolescence differed substantially across its facets. Whereas higher parent-rated frustration at age 11 was fairly consistently associated with risk-related outcomes, fear showed a more beneficial pattern of associations. Specifically, our results provide initial evidence that the fear facet of neuroticism in early adolescence may be linked to selected beneficial outcomes.

In the general population sample, higher parent-rated fear at age 11 was associated with reduced substance use at age 19. In the clinic-referred sample, higher fear at age 11 predicted better teacher-rated social skills and prosocial behaviors across measurement points. In line with evolutionary considerations on the adaptive function of personality traits (Buss & Penke, 2015; Nettle, 2006), more fearful adolescents may be more inclined towards actively avoiding social exclusion (Denissen & Penke, 2008a , 2008b) and maintaining positive relationships with peers and teachers at school.

The prospective benefits appeared specific to fear assessed in early adolescence. In the exploratory robustness checks, associations between higher fear at age 11 and beneficial outcomes remained robust after accounting for prior outcome levels, whereas comparable beneficial effects were no longer observed when fear was measured at age 16. As neuroticism increases during adolescence as a normative trend (Aldinger et al., 2014), being more fearful than the average may add no further value, but is not a disadvantage either with regard to most of the studied outcomes. At the same time, the analysis on parent-rated fear and frustration at age 16 also indicated that the difference between both facets persisted, such that fear remained less of a risk than frustration. Perhaps the classification of neuroticism as a risk factor is largely driven by the tendency to react tense or annoyed (i.e., scoring high on frustration) while being scared or worrying a lot (i.e., scoring high on fear) is less of a risk for lifestyle choices and social resources in adolescence.

Overall, our results highlight that fear and frustration distinctly relate to concurrent and prospective outcomes in adolescent lifestyle choices and social resources. We therefore emphasize the need for research to view neuroticism as a multidimensional construct or family of traits (Brandes & Tackett, 2019; Hill et al., 2020), and broad overall scores may obscure important differences between facets (Irwing et al., 2024). Practically, these findings can be used to develop resource-oriented interventions (Priebe et al., 2014). Rather than focusing exclusively on reducing neuroticism in the general population (Wright et al., 2026), interventions might also seek to identify and leverage the social competencies associated with fear. Such an approach may be especially valuable for adolescents with high fear and mental problems, a combination that is quite common (Jeronimus et al., 2016).

The Role of Informants

The most consistent risk-related links of both fear and frustration concerned parentrated social behavior problems. This association is partly inconsistent with the link between fear and the related construct of teacher-rated prosocial behavior. This illustrates that the risks and benefits of neuroticism may differ not only between facets but also across social contexts and perspectives (Laursen & Bukowski, 1997). One possible explanation is that adolescents may show more problematic behaviors at home than at school because the different social contexts trigger certain behaviors, providing different information to adolescents' parents and teachers (Brandt et al., 2021). Given the relatively high stability of family relationships (Laursen & Bukowski, 1997), adolescents with high fear may feel safe to act in a socially less desirable manner when at home. In contrast, not adhering to norms may have more negative consequences at school, where fitting in is key during that age (Blakemore, 2012; Smetana et al., 2006). Furthermore, considering the heritability of neuroticism (Kandler, 2012), it seems likely that parents of adolescents with high neuroticism score high on neuroticism themselves, which has been linked to problematic parenting behaviors (Prinzie et al., 2009). This, in turn, could provoke problematic behaviors in their adolescent children that are not triggered in the same way at school. Another explanation refers to common method bias (Podsakoff et al., 2003), as both adolescents' fear/frustration and their social behavior problems were rated by adolescents' parents. Potentially, parents who generally perceive their child as fearful or quickly frustrated tend to evaluate their behavior as more problematic due to preconceived judgments or may even trigger problematic behavior in the sense of a self-fulfilling prophecy (Darley & Fazio, 1980).

Further informant-related differences appeared in our exploratory analyses.

Adolescents' self-ratings of Big Five neuroticism at age 16 showed similar outcomeassociations as the corresponding parent-rated frustration facet, supporting the robustness of our results across raters. At the same time, parent-ratings of temperament and adolescents' self-ratings of neuroticism at age 16 were only moderately correlated, highlighting both similarities and differences between measures and raters.

Future research is needed to better understand how and why self-and parent perspectives differ and to determine their unique predictive power for outcomes in adolescent development (Brandt et al., 2021).

The Role of the Developmental Context

Most associations between fear and risk-related outcomes disappeared when controlling for childhood adversity (e.g., parental divorce or financial scarcity). Although this result pattern was not part of our hypotheses and should be considered exploratory, it suggests that some of the detrimental associations of fear may reflect adverse developmental conditions rather than fear itself. Whereas the environment in which people grow up is often neglected in personality research, higher levels of neuroticism in adolescence may also be regarded as a result of stressful life events or unpredictable environments in childhood (Jeronimus et al., 2014; Kandler, 2012) and not only as a cause. This interpretation aligns with clinical models that link neuroticism and psychopathology through common developmental pressures: detrimental outcomes that reflect unhealthy lifestyle choices and social difficulties have been recognized as clinical symptoms and may partly stem from the same adverse childhood experiences that contribute to higher neuroticism (Durbin & Hicks, 2014).

At the same time, beneficial associations of adolescents' neuroticism facets were not reinforced by childhood adversity or more pronounced among female adolescents, providing no support for our hypotheses on moderating ecological conditions (Buss & Penke, 2015; Ellis et al., 2012). However, adolescents from the clinic-referred sample showed more beneficial and fewer risk-related associations than adolescents from the population sample, which is consistent with the notion of a "hidden talent" that manifests under challenging conditions (Ellis et al., 2023). One possible explanation is that the risks captured by the childhood adversity index and those related to female gender differ from the risks experienced by the adolescents in the clinic-referred sample, such that fear may be protective only in the latter context. Alternatively, the lower rate of risk-related associations in the clinic-referred sample may also reflect ceiling effects, given that this group scored higher on fear and frustration compared to the population sample on average. Taken together, these findings suggest that the developmental context matters, but that the specific conditions under which higher neuroticism may be beneficial remain insufficiently understood.

More broadly, our results raise the possibility that beneficial effects of neuroticism are difficult to detect because they are rarely examined in contexts where they are most likely to emerge. Whereas the few beneficial associations that we observed almost exclusively emerged in the clinic-referred sample, most evidence on risk-related effects of neuroticism and its facets is derived from relatively low-risk populations living in comparatively safe and predictable environments (BLINDED). Even when focusing on sensitive developmental periods or indicators of adversity, most adolescents in our Dutch samples grew up within a developmental context characterized by prolonged education, extensive parental support, and comparatively low exposure to severe risks (Arnett, 2000). Under such conditions, the potential protective role of neuroticism may remain largely invisible because serious dangers are rare. At the same time, neuroticism may be associated almost exclusively with risks, whereas beneficial effects are rare. Distinguishing between these possibilities requires future research to study neuroticism in more diverse populations and developmental contexts, including clinic-referred youth, child maltreatment survivors, refugees, or adolescents living in regions affected by poverty or conflict.

Limitations and Future Directions

Despite important strengths of the current study, including the wide range of outcomes measured between ages 11 and 19, the comparison of a population and clinic-referred sample, and the multi-methodological approach, we note at least four limitations. First, most outcomes were not measured at every measurement point, leaving it open to what degree findings are age-specific. Some outcomes, such as teacher-rated social skills and face processing ability, were only measured at the same age as neuroticism (age 11). Further investigating the critical time frames in which effects of neuroticism and its facets manifest, how lasting they are, and how they predict changes in a given outcome is an essential task for future research to better understand the role of neuroticism as either a risk factor or a resource (Hopwood et al., 2022).

Second, although our research questions and hypotheses were informed by evolutionary and developmental perspectives (e.g., Buss & Penke, 2015b; Lerner & Overton, 2008) suggesting that neuroticism may function as a protective strategy through processes such as heightened threat sensitivity, these processes were not directly measured. As a result, our findings do not allow us to draw conclusions about the mechanisms underlying these links. Future studies are needed to measure the theoretically proposed processes in naturalistic settings or manipulate them in the laboratory to test more directly how neuroticism may contribute to both risks and benefits.

Third, in the main analysis, neuroticism was measured using parent-rated temperamental facets of fear and frustration. This may not capture the full breadth of the trait, as existing definitions often include additional facets such as sadness and discomfort (Rothbart, 2007). At the same time, it remains unclear to what extent the current measure generalizes to content typically assessed by adult personality questionnaires. For example, in the NEO-PI-R, fear aligns closely with the anxiety facet and frustration with the angry hostility facet; however, other facets, such as depression or self-conscientiousness, remain partly uncovered (Costa & McCrae, 1992). Given the nuanced facet-specific findings, future studies on risks and benefits of adolescent neuroticism should expand on the current study by exploring additional facets featured in temperament and personality models.

Finally, although both the general population and the clinic-referred sample were large, the general population sample was much larger, increasing the likelihood of finding even small significant effects (Faber & Fonseca, 2014). Consequently, the higher number of risk-related neuroticism effects in the population sample, some of which had very small effect sizes, may be due to the larger sample size. Nevertheless, sample-size differences alone are unlikely to explain the pattern of beneficial associations observed in the clinic-referred sample. Moreover, some variables, such as the peer nominations as friend or bully, were only measured in the population sample, limiting direct comparisons across contexts.

Besides these limitations, our findings open the opportunity for several routes of future research. As people with high neuroticism levels tend to see themselves and the world in a more negative light (Ehring & Watkins, 2008), relying solely on self-reports of potential risks and benefits may lead to an overestimation of risks. In other words, including other-reports, such as those from parents, peers, or teachers, as we did in this study, is crucial to obtaining a balanced view of the role of neuroticism in adolescence. However, some potential benefits of neuroticism may only reveal themselves at the group level, by contributing to more diverse perspectives within a social group (Pringle & Robinson, 2024). For example, a group of adolescents may be better prepared for a camping trip if at least one member in the group scores high on neuroticism and worries about dangerous animals. Thus, future research should not only look at individual-level outcomes but look at how adolescents with higher neuroticism may contribute to better group functioning.

Another promising direction is to examine how adolescent neuroticism interacts with other personality traits. While some studies in adulthood suggest that higher neuroticism paired with higher conscientiousness relates to beneficial health outcomes (e.g., Turiano et al., 2013), investigating how adolescent neuroticism interacts with traits such as sensation seeking and openness to new experiences seems compelling, given their relevance to adolescent risktaking (Ellis et al., 2012; Steinberg, 2005).

Finally, the findings on potential benefits of neuroticism add to the broader discussions on normative interpretations in personality research: rather than considering traits as universally "good" or "bad", researchers should refrain from value-laden labels but aim to understand what functions certain traits fulfill in a certain developmental context (Klimstra & McLean, 2024). Thus, although neuroticism may hold mostly disadvantages for most individuals and in most contexts, there are notable exceptions to this general pattern that warrant further attention. Future research could build on the current findings by examining potential benefits of other personality traits that are commonly considered as undesirable, such as introversion (low extraversion) or antagonism (low agreeableness).

Conclusion

In conclusion, we conducted a multi-method study, which compared longitudinal data from two large adolescent samples, one from the general population and one clinic-referred.

To our knowledge, this is the most comprehensive investigation of potential risks and benefits of neuroticism to date. Although higher neuroticism generally posed a risk to adolescent lifestyle choices and social resources, these associations were more pronounced for the frustration facet and varied by context. I contrast, the tendency to experience fear in anticipation of stress was linked to selected benefits, namely less substance use in the population sample, and better social skills and more prosocial behaviors in the clinic-referred sample. Furthermore, many risk-related associations of fear were no longer statistically significant after accounting for adolescents' general degree of childhood adversity. These results raise the question of whether the almost exclusively negative portrayal of neuroticism in the existing literature is justified. Offering counterpoint and nuance, we suggest some beneficial role of the fear facet of neuroticism, especially among youth with a clinical history.

Critical questions remain about the specific timing, context, and mechanisms underlying these associations.
