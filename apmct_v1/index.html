To fix Exact P-Values

What we found

We found 2 imprecise p values out of 35 detected p values.

Show details

Reporting p values imprecisely (e.g., p < .05) reduces transparency, reproducibility, and re-use (e.g., in p value meta-analyses). Best practice is to report exact p-values with three decimal places (e.g., p = .032) unless p values are smaller than 0.001, in which case you can use p < .001.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (e.g., “decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

To check Non-Significant P Value Check

What we found

We found 19 non-significant p values that should be checked for appropriate interpretation.

Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

Ethics Approval Check

Based on the following text, we would expect an ethics approval statement, and it was present:

Across all experiments, participants were given definitions for agency (“ability to plan and act”) and experience (“ability to feel and perceive”) and rated 48 entities belonging to 8 categories (humans, pets, voice assistants, robots, media, tools, appliances, transport) from 0 (no agency/experience) to 100 (full agency/experience).

Participants provided an initial rating of all the entities identical to those obtained during the previous two experiments.

Participants were then randomly assigned to receive a Google Home device (Experimental; n = 23) or not (Control; n = 24).

After 1 and after 2 weeks of living with the device, participants were asked to provide a second and third rating of the entities.

The experiments were administered through Gorilla Experiment Builder (69) to four different samples of participants that were recruited through the recruitment system of the University of Utrecht (SONA; Experiment 1, 3, and 4), convenience sampling via snowballing (Experiment 4), and through Prolific Academic (Experiment 2).

Participants provided informed consent (electronically) before participation.

Participants completed two separate counterbalanced blocks, one for agency (Experiment 1-3: “the ability to plan and act”, Experiment 4: “the ability to plan actions and exercise self-control”) and one for experience (Experiment 1-3: “the ability to sense and feel”, Experiment 4: “the ability to feel fear and pain”), in which they rated the entities one-by-one in randomized order on a slider scale from 0 (no agency/experience) to 100 (full agency/experience).

Participants were randomly assigned to either a mechanic condition or intention condition.

In Experiment 3 we recruited participants who did not previously own a voice assistant.

Participants provided a first baseline rating of all entities and were then randomly allocated to the control or experimental condition.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine, Daniel Lakens and Cristian Mesquida

Overall, among all instances flagged as power statements, 90.6% were correct (positive predictive value).

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). Statcheck might still miss one-sided tests that are described in another sentence, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 685 tests with 34 instances of inconsistent reporting, Statcheck correctly detected 34 of them, and incorrectly identified 26. Therefore 0% of true instances were missed, and 43% of detections were false positives. See https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/ for more details of the validation.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on 226 Psychological Science papers with 5,019 hand-coded p-values, 438 of them imprecise (scienceverse/validation, branch stat_p_exact). Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). Of the misses, 13 are starred table notes (“*p < .05”) that the module leaves out on purpose and 11 are p-values it did not find; of the wrong reports, 20 are p-values the ground truth does not contain (such as “ps < .05”) and 6 use ≤ or ≥. These figures measure agreement with the hand-coding, which has known errors in both directions (scienceverse/validation#48).

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

Abstract

Understanding how people decide whether someone or something possesses a mind is central to a myriad of psychological, biological, and philosophical questions. It shapes fundamental processes at the individual (e.g., social interaction) and societal level (e.g., morality and ethics).

While mind perception has been studied, it remains unclear which internal model people use when ascribing mental capacities to entities. Across four experiments (total N = 256), we explored what model people hold and its flexibility when ascribing agency and experience to entities encountered in everyday life and which differ in animacy and socialness (e.g., humans, pets, robots, voice assistants, tools). Using representational similarity analysis combined with labbased experiments, a six-week long social interaction intervention, and longitudinal real-life exposure, we tested which model people use to ascribe agency and experience by comparing observed rating data to theoretical models. We demonstrated that an animacy distinction underlies the representational structure of experience attributions, while internal models for agency attributions are more idiosyncratic and flexible. When receiving explicit information on one entity, the internal model of agency changes. People update their model by incorporating this information, generalizing across the entire category of entities. We found no evidence that extended exposure to or ownership of inanimate technology, such as robots or voice assistants, or pets leads to restructuring of the models for experience or agency. Overall, these results provide insight into the internal models people hold when perceiving minds, the flexibility thereof, and offer new ways to understand the roots and consequences of mind perception.

When do people perceive someone or something to possess a mind? Although research has shown that agency, the ability to act, and experience, the ability to feel, are the two underlying dimensions of mind perception, it remains unclear which criteria or mental model people use to attribute those capacities. We applied a method commonly used in neuroscience to compare internally held models to competing theoretical models that may underlie mind attribution. Across four experiments, we demonstrate that animacy is the underlying distinction for experience attributions, with more idiosyncratic internal models observed for agency attributions. This distinction is stable across time, ownership of pets and AI systems, but temporarily flexible in response to new information on an entity.

Main Text Introduction

When do we see someone or something as animatus: "instilled with life" or as animus: "possessing a mind"? How people perceive the minds of other agents and objects has been investigated across domains and centuries of scientific inquiry. Early records of scholars debating perception of minds in non-human animals date back to philosophers in the 18 th century. In 1785, Kant asserted that "[…] when we are talking about moral worth, it does not depend on the actions, which one sees, but on the inner principles, which one does not see (1, p. 19-20)". The question of who or what has a mind has since influenced moral considerations such as the extension of human rights to certain non-human animals (2), the right of abortion (3), environmental protection (4), and considerations for robots (5, 6). Here, we ask the question how minds are represented in the mind of the observer. What model do people use when perceiving minds of others? How stable is this model? It is not simply a matter of saying yes or no, or more or less, if an entity has a mind. First shown by Gray and colleagues (7), people distinguish minds across two dimensions: agency, the ability to plan and act, and experience, the ability to sense and feel. A large body of research supports the findings of this landmark study (8) (9) (10) (11). Other scholars similarly identified two dimensions, labelled as warmth, the ability to sympathize, and competence, being skilled (12), converging with the dimensions of experience and agency respectively. Research on dehumanization shows that people dehumanize entities by ascribing either less human nature, emotionality and interpersonal warmth, or less human uniqueness, higher order cognition and moral sensibility, to them (13, 14), again converging with the dimensions of experience and agency respectively. This two-dimensional structure of mind perception has not only been found in psychological research but is also reflected in the literature itself. From a historic perspective, the distinction between agency and experience ratings is not a modern phenomenon. In archives of written text over the past 200 years, perceived agency and experience of different entities have reflected the social status of women and the moral value attributed to domestic animals (15). For instance, agency and experience ascriptions of women track societal developments and historic events affecting women's status in society (e.g., suffragettes).

The perception of mind in other entities has perceptual and behavioral consequences.

Believing that an entity can act or feel affects everyday perception across domains, from objectification of men and women (16, 17), gender stereotypes (12, 18), to dehumanization (13).

It not only affects their social standing, but also if they are deemed moral agents or patients with associated rights and responsibilities (19, 20). People are more willing to punish wrongdoing when perceiving a mind in the actor (7), and perceiving outgroup members as more sentient increases people's willingness to help them following a natural disaster (21). Not perceiving minds in animals contributes to more readily accepting violence against them (22, 23). Mind perception can affect lifestyle decisions such as whether people withdraw life support from sick patients (24), their decision to eat meat (25), as well as perception of AI technologies, such as service robots (26).

While existing literature robustly outlines that perceived agency and experience affect an entity's social, moral, and ethical status, it remains unclear how people represent the wide variety of agents encountered in everyday life. What model do people hold when ascribing agency or experience? Do people view the world through an animate-inanimate lens, or do they take the social nature of the entity into account? Is the model formed by how the entity looks or by the beliefs of the perceiver? People frequently perceive minds in inanimate objects (27, 28), and the presence of a face can promote this (29), and people can also see mindless humans and mindful robots (30). Indeed, both stimulus cues (e.g., human-likeness of the entity) and knowledge cues (e.g., beliefs, lifestyle of the perceiver) influence mind perception (31, 32). Objective measures of mind perception (32), such as self-recognition (33) or cognitive performance (34), do not always align with people's subjective mind perception (35). Paradoxically, people even attribute more mental capacities in terms of agency to dead people than to people in a persistent vegetative state (36), suggesting that a model distinguishing between animacy and inanimacy might not fully explain people's judgements during mind perception. The model people use to ascribe minds remains unknown and may not merely be based on objective, stable properties of an entity.

Instead, the model might take the perceived socialness of an entity into account and might be open to change in response to new information, experiences, or updated beliefs regarding an entity.

Across four experiments (total N = 256), we explored what model or models people hold while judging minds. We asked people to rate minds on two dimensions, agency, the ability to plan and act, and experience, the ability to sense and feel. We surveyed a large number of entities, humans, pets, voice assistants, robots, (social) media, tools, appliances, and transport, that are part of our everyday life. These entities are encountered frequently in either real life or in the online domain and differ across a wide variety of dimensions, including animacy and socialness. Going beyond just descriptive statements, we used representational similarity Through what model do people view the minds of others and how rigid is this?

In Experiment 1, we tested which model people initially hold during mind perception, testing both theory-driven (e.g. animacy, face selective, or social orientation) and latent models, and how they differ across the two dimensions of mind perception. In Experiment 2, we investigated the flexibility of the models people hold when judging the minds of other entities.

Specifically, we tested the susceptibility of dominant and non-dominant models of mind perception to new information. In Experiment 3, we investigated the short-term effects of extended contact to a non-human entity (i.e. voice assistant) using a six-week intervention study including a four-week wash-out period. In Experiment 4, we further looked at the long-term effects of extended contact (> 1 year) to other non-human entities (pets, robots, voice assistants). Across these experiments, we found evidence that people hold a model that distinguishes between animacy and inanimacy when ascribing experience. This model described experience attributions better than agency attributions, as the internal model for attributions of agency is more idiosyncratic. In the short term, the representational model was flexible in response to explicit new information about a non-human entity but stable during extended contact with an entity.

An animacy model is used when perceiving experience, but less so for agencyPrevious research has repeatedly shown that, during perception, people innately distinguish between animate and inanimate entities (37). The distinction of animacy manifests during infanthood (38), shapes our interpretation of the world (39), and is a semantic category in most, if not all, languages (40). First, we tested whether this animacy distinction reflects both dimensions of mind perception and to what degree. Across all experiments, participants were given definitions for agency ("ability to plan and act") and experience ("ability to feel and perceive") and rated 48 entities belonging to 8 categories (humans, pets, voice assistants, robots, media, tools, appliances, transport) from 0 (no agency/experience) to 100 (full agency/experience). To compare representational organizations underlying the obtained ratings, we used representational similarity analysis (RSA). Rather than comparing absolute ratings of different entities, RSA can be used to compare patterns of dissimilarity across stimuli. For this purpose, the observed rating data were then converted into dissimilarity matrices using Euclidean distance (i.e., absolute difference between all possible combinations of entities), indicating pairwise dissimilarity between all tested entities (e.g., young boy versus Google Home or bunny versus hammer). This was done individually for all participants and separately for the two dimensions of agency and experience. These values then reveal which entities a participant rated more dissimilar in terms of experience or agency with higher values indicating higher dissimilarity (absolute difference in ascribed agency or experience) between two entities (Figure 1C). To investigate which theoretical distinction underlies the observed patterns of dissimilarity, the individual dissimilarity matrices were then correlated with theoretical matrices.

Theoretical matrices were based on possible distinctions that people may make when attributing a mind (e.g., animacy or affordance of social interaction; Figure 1E). We created an 'animacy model' distinguishing between animate and inanimate agents, a 'face model' distinguishing between agents with and without a face, an 'interaction model' that differentiates between agents that afford a responsive interaction and those who do not, and a 'social model' that differentiates agents based on social affordances, as well as a 'control model' based on image luminance values. The dissimilarity matrix of each participant's ratings was correlated with all theoretical dissimilarity matrices using Mantel test (n = 9999 permutations). This resulted in one Mantel correlation coefficient for each participant and each theoretical model and each dimension of mind perception (Figure 1D). A higher Mantel correlation value indicates higher correspondence between a theoretical and observed matrix. In other words, the model fit signifies to what extent the observed pattern of dissimilarity of the ratings for a dimension follows the theoretically assumed pattern of dissimilarity. Comparing these resulting model fits allowed us to assess whether representations of agency and experience were best characterized by a distinction in animacy, presence of a face or alternative distinctions. The results of Experiment 1 (N = 40) show that people use an animacy-based model when ascribing experience to entities but have a less clearly defined model when ascribing agency to the same entities (Figure 2A-2B). The animacy model produced the highest correlation of all tested theoretical models with the rating-based representational structure (experience: r = .66 95 Confidence Interval [.60, .73], agency: r = .38 [.31, .44]). When scaled by the noise ceiling, the animacy model achieved 89% of the performance of a perfect model, considering the heterogeneity between participants, for experience ratings, but only 73% for agency ratings. The animacy model was the best performing model for 82.5% of participants for experience ratings, but only for 42.5% of participants for the agency ratings. For both dimensions of mind perception, the fit of the animacy model is followed by the face model (experience: r = .47 [.40, .54], agency: r = .34 [.27, .41]), the interaction model (experience: r = .32 [.25, .38], agency: r = .28 [.21, .35]), and the social model (experience: r = .15 [.09, .22], agency: r = .22 [.15, .29]). The control model for image luminance produced the lowest correlation (experience: r = -.05 [-.06, -.04], agency: r = -.04 [-.05, -.02]).

Mixed effects models revealed a significant interaction between the dimensions, experience and agency, and the tested models (F(3, 273) = 14.00, p < .001, ηₚ² = .13). The animacy model (p < .001) and the face model (p = .001) fitted the representational structure of the experience ratings significantly better than agency ratings (Figure 2B). The animacy model performed significantly better than all other theoretical models for experience ratings (all ps < .001). For agency ratings, the animacy model only performed better than the social model (Δr = .16 [0.05, 0.26], t(273) = 3.94, p < .001) but not better than the face (Δr = .04 [-0.07, 0.14], t(273) = 0.94, p = .389) or interaction model (Δr = .10 [-0.01, 0.21], t(273) = 2.46, p = .059). These findings are in line with the visual interpretation of the dissimilarity matrices suggesting that while experience is strongly structured by animacy, the animacy model is not an ideal description of the representational structure of the agency data (Figure 2B). These findings were replicated in the baseline measurements of Experiment 2 (N = 80) and 3 (N = 47; Figure 2A-2B), as well as Experiment 4 (n = 15; no ownership group). Again, a significant interaction between the dimensions and theoretical models was found for Experiment 2: F(3, 553) = 21.70, p < .001, ηₚ² = .11; Experiment 3: F(3, 322) = 17.51, p < .001, ηₚ² = .14; Experiment 4: F(3, 98) = 9.37, p < .001, ηₚ² = .22. Model performance of the animacy model was significantly higher for experience ratings than for agency ratings (ps < .001) and higher compared to all other models for experience ratings (ps < .001), but not for agency ratings. No significant difference in representational model fit was found for agency ratings between the animacy model and the face model across all experiments (ps > .08). The replication of these findings in Experiment 4 while using different definitions (experience; "to feel fear and pain", and agency: "to plan actions and exercise self-control"), suggests that definition-specific effects do not explain the difference in model fit between experience and agency attributions.

What is the latent model for agency?

To further investigate the difference in representational model fit between the experience and agency dimensions, we determined a latent model for the agency ratings using a data-driven approach. We used partition around medoids (PAM) clustering on the average dissimilarity matrix for agency of Experiment 1. We looked at solutions ranging from two to eight clusters (number of entity categories). A two-cluster solution produced the highest silhouette width (Figure S1).

Based on this data-driven two-cluster solution, we built a binary model similar to the other theoretical models described and tested this in independent samples of Experiment 2 and 3. This matrix was similar to the animacy model with the difference being that one robot was grouped as more similar to humans and animals rather than inanimate entities. The animacy model performed significantly better for the baseline measures of Experiment 2 (Δr = .02 [.00, .04], t(79) = 2.80, p = .025) and also remained the dominant model for the baseline measures of Experiment 3, although the difference in fit between models was not significant (Δr = .02 [-.01, .05], t(46) = 1.28, p = 0.206; Table S6).

Together, these results suggest that people distinguish between animate and inanimate entities when judging the ability to experience in agents and objects. People perceive pets and humans to be similar to each other when ascribing the ability to experience and dissimilar to inanimate entities. While this animacy distinction was also visible in the representational structure of agency ratings, model fit was lower with higher levels of variability between participants.

Individual differences in model fit, especially in agency ratings, pose the question of how personal experience with different entities may influence the representational model people hold in the short-or long-term.

New information on an agent changes the dominant model for agency, but not experience

In Experiment 2 (N = 80) we investigated how stable model-based mind perception is in response to new information. In everyday life, the model people use to ascribe a mind to other entities may not always solely distinguish between animacy and inanimacy. Instead, people even perceive other humans to vary in terms of agency and experience based on stereotypes and previous experiences (12). Research has shown that information about an individual can alter to what extent people ascribe a mind to them (19, 41). In Experiment 2, we tested whether new information on a robot can lead to a change in the representational model people use to ascribe minds to other entities.

Participants rated the same entities as in Experiment 1 on experience and agency twice (N = 80). In between the two rating blocks, participants received new information on one of the entities. During this intervention, one group of participants saw a video in which the behavior of a social robot called Cozmo was described as mechanical (e.g., "its behavior is constrained by its strict programming"), while the other group saw a video in which the same Cozmo robot was described as having intentions (e.g., "it can behave flexibly", 42). Linear mixed effects models revealed that the intervention did not differently change fit across models for experience ratings (Table S7; Table S8) and we did not observe a change in dominant model (Figure 3). The animacy model fit the data best before and after the intention video (Δr = .01 [-.05, .07]) and the mechanic video (Δr = .04 [-.02, .10]; Figure 3B). New information on an entity did not change the representational structure when ascribing experience. For agency, however, watching the intention video led to an overall change in representational model fit across all theoretical models (Table S9), as well as a change in dominant model (Figure 3). Following the intervention, the representational model people used to ascribe agency was reflected better by the face model which groups robots with animate entities than one that groups robots with inanimate entities. The face model (r = .67 [.62, .72], Δr = .17 [.10, .24]), and not the animacy model (r = .64 [.58, .71], Δr = .13 [.07, .19]), became the dominant model for agency ratings, performing similar to the animacy model (Δr = -.03, [-0.09, 0.04], t(40) = -0.82, p = .418).

To investigate whether the intervention led to a change in model fit that is limited to the robot shown in the video or extends to all robots shown during the experiment, we conducted an exploratory analysis. We created a new theoretical dissimilarity matrix that groups humans, pets, and Cozmo together, distinguishing them from the remaining entities, and which contrasts with the face model that groups the entire robot category with humans and pets (Figure S3). After watching the intentional video, the Cozmo model performed significantly better than the animacy model (Δr = .01 [0.00, 0.03], t(40) = 2.32, p = .026), but not significantly better than the face model (Δr = -.01 [-0.07, 0.04], t(40) = -0.44, p = .660).

The results of Experiment 2 show the flexibility of the model people use to ascribe minds in response to new information. Receiving new information on a robot's ability to act freely led to a change in the dominant model for agency ratings. After this intervention, people perceived the robot they saw to be more similar to humans and pets in terms of agency rather than to inanimate entities. Receiving information on the same robot's inability to behave flexibly had the opposite effect and consolidated the use of the animacy model. The Cozmo model did not perform significantly better than the face model which groups all shown robots to be more similar to humans and animals than to inanimate entities. This suggests that information about one robot can temporarily influence participants' perceptions of the entire robot category and can lead to a change in model fit. For experience ratings, we did not observe a significant change in the dominant model when providing information on the behavioral flexibility of a robot.

Extended contact to an agent does not change the dominant model for mind perception

The flexible nature of model-based mind perception in response to new information raises the question of whether long-term exposure to specific entities can similarly influence the representational structure of experience and agency perception. In Experiment 3, we investigated whether two-week contact with a specific inanimate entity, namely a voice assistant (Google Home device), affects the perception of the device and the representational model people hold during mind perception. Living with an entity may increase the need to understand and predict the behavior of that entity, leading people to ascribe more human-like cognition to them (43, 44).

Importantly, to probe cross-platform generalizability (45), we used a voice assistant which is focused on human-like language and response generation rather than animal-like movement of the embodied robot used in Experiment 2.

During Experiment 3, we tested 47 participants who did not own a voice assistant before the experiment. Participants provided an initial rating of all the entities identical to those obtained during the previous two experiments. Participants were then randomly assigned to receive a Google Home device (Experimental; n = 23) or not (Control; n = 24). After 1 and after 2 weeks of living with the device, participants were asked to provide a second and third rating of the entities.

Average interaction frequency with the device was 12 times during week 1 and 9 times in week 2 (Table S10). After those two weeks of living with the Google Home, participants returned the device to the researchers. A final rating of all entities was requested 4 weeks after returning the Google Home, to investigate whether the potential changes in the ratings lasted in the absence of the device or whether they washed out. The control group did not receive a Google Home but provided similar ratings across time.

We found no evidence that living with a voice assistant changed the dominant model compared to baseline for experience or agency ratings. Results of linear mixed effects showed no interaction between condition, timepoint, and model for experience (F(9,675) = 0.13, p = .999, ηₚ² = .002) or agency (F(9, 675) = 0.23, p = .990, ηₚ² = .003), suggesting that the intervention did not differently alter the representational structure of experience nor agency attributions (Figure 4A-4D). Directly testing each theoretical model's fit across time did not show any changes in model fit in response to two-week long exposure to a voice assistant (Table S13). Exploring the impact of exposure further, we found no evidence that frequency of use was associated with the fit of the animacy model in the first (Experience: Spearman's ρ = -.21 [-.59, .24], p = .326; Agency: ρ = -.35 [-.67, .05], p = .103) or second week (Experience: ρ = -.23 [-.55, .17], p = .302; Agency: ρ = -.01 [-.40, .40], p = .946) of cohabitation. To investigate whether the intervention led to a change in model fit that is limited to the Google Home device or extends to all voice assistants shown during the experiment, we created two explorative theoretical models. The Google Home model groups together humans, pets, and the Google Home device that people interacted with, distinguishing them from the remaining entities. Similarly, the voice assistant model groups humans, pets, and all voice assistants together (Figure S4). Exploratory analyses revealed that the animacy model fit the representational structure of both experience and agency significantly better than the two exploratory models at the two-week mark of living with the Google Home device (all ps < .001).

The results of experiment 3 hint at the stable nature of the representational model underlying mind perception. Over time, the animacy model remained dominant across groups, independent of everyday exposure to a new non-human entity. While Experiment 2 showed that explicit information on a non-human entity possessing human-like properties can lead to a shift in relative representational model alignment, more realistic, everyday experiences were not associated with such a shift. While there is research hinting at prolonged human-robot interaction influencing socialization with robots in naïve users such as children (46), previous research has demonstrated that one-week engagement with a socializing robot did not impact ratings of observed pain and pleasure in the robot (47), corresponding to the current study's measure of experience. Animacy perception therefore seems to be a rather robust, fundamental predictor thereof that does not get overwritten through short-term interaction with a non-human entity such as a voice assistant.

No effect across diverse entities of long-term cohabitation on model-based mind perception

To further test model stability, Experiment 4 investigated whether long-term cohabitation for 1 year or longer with non-human entities is associated with differences in the representational model people hold during mind perception. We tested a sample of 89 participants who owned either a pet (n = 37, average ownership of 4.8 years), a robot (n = 22, 2.2 years), a voice assistant (n = 15, 1.9 years) or neither (n = 15). They were asked to rate the different agents and objects, including a new category of more physically complex robots.

We replicated the finding that the animacy model is the dominant model used to ascribe experience to entities across all 4 groups with a weighted average correlation of r = .75 (pet: r = .76 [.68, .85]; robot: r = .75 [.67, .84]; voice assistant: r = .67 [.54, .81]; none: r = .77 [.65, .89]).

For agency, more variability was observed between participants, with a weighted average correlation of r = .51 (pet: r = .50 [.39, .62]; robot: r = .55 [.44, .67]; voice assistant: r = .56 [.40, .72]; none: r = .44 [.25, .63]; Figure 5A-5B). There was no evidence that relative model fit differed across ownership conditions for both dimensions of mind perception (experience: F(9, 255) = 0.58, p = .816, ηₚ² = .020; agency: F(9, 255) = 0.31, p = .973, ηₚ² = .011). Directly comparing the fit of the animacy model between ownership groups showed no significant difference for both experience and agency ratings (all ps = 1.00), and the same was the case for the face model (all ps > .230). Average dissimilarity matrices for experience ratings of the four groups were highly correlated with the lowest correlation being between pet owners and people who owned none of the entities (r = .62) and the highest correlation between robot and voice assistant owners (r = .85).

Similarly, for agency ratings, the lowest correlation was found between pet owners and people owning none of the entities (r = .55) and the highest correlation between voice assistant and robot owners (r = .95). Across ownership types, we found no evidence that duration of ownership was associated with animacy model fit for either experience (Spearman's ρ = .19 [-.04, .39], p = .102) or agency (ρ = .19 [-.04, .41], p = .106). Together, this suggests that ownership of one of the tested entities was not associated with differences in the representational structure of mind perception ratings.

These results converge with the findings of Experiment 3 by suggesting long-term exposure to a specific agent is not associated with owners holding a different dominant model of mind perception. The results provide further evidence for cross-platform generalizability (45) as findings generalize across types of technology (embodied non-verbal robot and disembodied voice assistant) and agents (technology and pets).

Discussion

Across four experiments, we explored the representational model underlying mind perception as well as its stability in response to explicit information and long-term exposure.

Rather than asking to what degree individual entities are perceived to possess a mind, we examined the representational organization of mind perception by testing which theoretical model best explains how humans perceive other entities. Do people view minds through an animacy or social lens? We found that, across samples, people initially hold an animacy model when attributing experience, while the representational organization when attributing agency showed more interindividual variation. We further investigated the stability of this representational organization in response to explicit information and long-term exposure. In Experiment 2, a shift in the dominant model occurred after watching a video that provided explicit information on a robot having intention rather than being merely mechanic. This manipulation led participants to shift towards a representational model for agency in which the entire category of robots, not only the manipulated robot, was more similar to animate than other inanimate entities. These findings suggest that the model people hold is, at least temporarily, flexible in response to new information on an entity. Experiment 3, however, hinted at a more stable distinction underlying the representational structure of mind perception. The representational organization was robust to a 2-week intervention of contact to a non-human entity. Experiment 4 further confirmed that extended exposure of more than 1 year to a non-human entity such as a pet, robot or voice assistant is not associated with differences in the model people hold during mind perception.

Together these results suggest that people intuitively hold a view of the social world that distinguishes between animacy and inanimacy when ascribing experience while the representational model underlying agency attribution is more idiosyncratic. This view further seems to be flexible enough to temporarily incorporate new information about an entity, but with limited restructuring in the long-term.

Our findings suggest that recognizing animacy is relevant to perception of experience in entities. This finding is in line with previous research showing that animacy distinction is one of the earliest developing perceptual systems (38). This may give rise to a robust cognitive system that allows for quick processing and interpretation of potentially social interaction partners. Maintaining a stable representational organization in the long term may be adaptive and facilitate efficient cognitive processing and social engagement with the environment (48). Previous research shows that social perception innately distinguishes between animate and inanimate entities (37, 38) and that animate compared to inanimate stimuli produce distinct brain activity (49) even after controlling for visual similarity between animate and inanimate entities (49, 50). At the same time, our results show that the role of animacy in mind perception differs across the two dimensions. Agency attribution is less well explained by an animacy distinction with higher individual differences in model fit and flexibility.

In contrast to the stability of the animacy distinction found in Experiment 3 and 4, Experiment 2 showed that the representational structure of mind perception can change in response to explicit information. Watching a video that described a robot as able to learn and act with intention led participants to subsequently view the category of robots as more similar to animate rather than inanimate entities when ascribing agency. Previous research has shown that knowledge cues to animacy may influence social perception (51) (52) (53) (54) as well as behavioral responses in social contexts (52). In line with this, our results suggest that, while extended contact alone does not affect mind perception, explicit information on an entity can temporarily change the representational position of an inanimate entity within people's organization of the social world. While questions remain on the temporal dimension of this manipulation, findings from social psychology show that receiving explicit new information about individual outgroup members can produce lasting changes in outgroup perception (55). Prompting people to perceive an individual as more or less agentic or able to experience may further affect mind perception and moral judgement on an entity (19). While we did not observe a shift in the dominant model during short-and long-term exposure to an inanimate entity in Experiment 3 and 4, the type and quality of the interaction, as well as the type of entity (e.g., AI model), may be relevant. For example, the requirement to obtain a common goal (56) or involvement of touch (57) have been shown to affect the perception of such entities following exposure to them. Incorporating these manipulations into longitudinal investigations over months and years paired with advanced data analyses, would allow us to test the flexibility and stability of models more precisely, and answer open questions on exposure and restructuring of internal models.

The intervention in Experiment 2 only led to a shift in the representational structure in agency ratings while experience ratings remained structured by animacy. Across the four experiments, the animacy model consistently showed a better representational fit for experience than for agency ratings. This suggests that the mental representation of experience is more stable in response to individual differences in prior social experiences. The experience dimension has been shown to account for more variance in mind perception than the agency dimension (7).

Experience is further more essentialized than agency (58), making it more stable in response to changes in the perceived entity (59). While people may be likely to ascribe agency to inanimate entities such as a robot, they may be reluctant to ascribe subjective experiences and feelings to an inanimate object. Sentience and the ability to feel emotions are closely linked to human uniqueness (13, 30, 60) which people may be motivated to preserve for humans or, at the minimum, animate entities.

Across our experiment, we compared the data-driven representational structures to a pre-defined set of theoretical models using RSA. This approach goes beyond mere quantitative descriptions of mind perception, as it allowed us to compare competing hypotheses about the mental representation of a variety of entities in terms of mind perception. As everyday interaction involves increasingly diverse entities, this approach from systems neuroscience (61), can provide new insights into outstanding questions by directly testing competing theories and assumptions.

While previous research commonly applied RSA at the group-level, we applied individual-level RSA to investigate individual differences in mind perception, further allowing us to test the generalizability of latent models. Future research should further disentangle how dispositional (62, 63) together with situational factors drive model-based mind perception. Combining this multi-level approach with new developments in RSA, such as looking at representational "neighborhoods" rather than absolute pairwise distances (64) will provide detailed insights into the internal models people use to ascribe minds to a wide variety of entities.

Recent literature further challenges the two-dimensional nature of mind perception and argues for a three-dimensional structure in which physiological sensations, basic and social emotions, and perceptual cognitive abilities (65) or sensory, cognitive, and affective experience are distinguished (66). Directly comparing both frameworks, a two-dimensional framework has been shown to describe entity-specific differences in mind perception, while a three-dimensional framework rather captures observer-specific differences such as spiritual beliefs (63). Participants from western countries have, however, been shown to apply a two-dimensional rather than threedimensional structure during mind perception (67) distinguishing between cognitive abilities and the experience of emotions (68). Future research could further investigate the underlying models of mind perception across diverse samples in a cross-cultural study.

Conclusion

Across four experiments, we mapped the representational nature of mind perception. We showed that when ascribing experience, a stable distinction between animate and inanimate entities is visible. This distinction remains unchanged in response to new information on an entity, as well as long-term exposure to an entity. For agency, a more idiosyncratic representation was found, with an animacy distinction still capturing attributions best at the group level. For ascribing agency, the representational organization temporarily adjusts in response to explicit information.

Across both dimensions, the representational organization of mind perception seems to be stable in response to extended real-life exposure to inanimate technology such as robots or voice assistants. Overall, the attribution of experience appears to be intuitively rooted in early developing perceptual systems that distinguish what is alive from what is not alive with the internal model for attributions of agency being more flexible to new information. As social interactions increasingly extend beyond humans to include pets, robots and other AI systems, our results highlight the promise of RSA to enhance understanding of mind perception.

Materials and Methods

Participants

The experiments were administered through Gorilla Experiment Builder (69) to four different samples of participants that were recruited through the recruitment system of the University of Utrecht (SONA; Experiment 1, 3, and 4), convenience sampling via snowballing (Experiment 4), and through Prolific Academic (Experiment 2). An overview of the sample characteristics can be seen in Table S1. Participants provided informed consent (electronically) before participation. The study was approved by the Ethics Review Board of the Faculty of Social and Behavioral Sciences of Utrecht University (24-0402; 21-0106).

The Rating Task

Participants rated a total of 48 different entities, divided over eight categories: humans, pets, robots, voice assistants, media, appliances, tools, and transport (Table S2). Before the ratings task, participants saw an overview of all eight categories with all exemplars. Participants completed two separate counterbalanced blocks, one for agency (Experiment 1-3: "the ability to plan and act", Experiment 4: "the ability to plan actions and exercise self-control") and one for experience (Experiment 1-3: "the ability to sense and feel", Experiment 4: "the ability to feel fear and pain"), in which they rated the entities one-by-one in randomized order on a slider scale from 0 (no agency/experience) to 100 (full agency/experience). The variation in definition was used to ensure that definition-specific effects do not explain the difference in model fit. In Experiment 4, six additional robots were added, and the total of 12 robots were subdivided into two categories: simple and advanced based on physical and social features. In Experiment 4, participants also rated themselves and the entity they own on the two dimensions of mind perception.

Experimental Manipulation

Experiment 2

In Experiment 2 we manipulated information on one entity by using a video intervention. After providing a first baseline rating of all the entities, participants watched a 25s video introducing a small consumer-grade Cozmo robot (Anki / Digital Dream Labs, USA). Participants were randomly assigned to either a mechanic condition or intention condition. Both videos were identical in introducing the robot moving in front of a green screen ("This is the Cozmo robot. In this short videoclip we will describe the functionality of this robot. Cozmo is a small robot and has 50 gears and 4 motors that", Movie S1 & S2), with further description informed by Marchesi and colleagues (42), and describing the robot as either mechanical ("[…] dictate it to navigate and move its arms and head. The robot is rigid with a fixed behavioral repertoire. Its behaviors are scripted and stable over time. These behaviors are based on programmed scripts") or intentional ("[…] allow it to navigate and move its arms and head. The robot is flexible with an expanding behavioral repertoire. Its behaviors are learned and evolve over time. The behaviors are based on artificial intelligence", Figure S2). Validation testing showed that videos were successful (N = 60, between-subjects, MANOVA p < .001), differing in ascribed intentional stance ("behaviors can be described as result of design or mind", p = .009), experience (p = .06), and agency (p < .001).

Experiment 3

In Experiment 3 we recruited participants who did not previously own a voice assistant.

Participants provided a first baseline rating of all entities and were then randomly allocated to the control or experimental condition. Participants in the experimental condition were provided with a Google Home device (Google Home Nest Mini, second Generation) which they were asked to incorporate in their life for 2 weeks after giving their baseline rating. They were asked to provide ratings after one and two weeks of living with the device. They were also asked to provide a rating 4 weeks after returning the Google Home device. Participants in the control condition were asked to provide a total of 4 ratings following the same intervals. Retention was high, with 47 out of the initial 55 recruited participants completing the study. Of the 8 recruited participants who did not complete the study, five did not complete the first (baseline) rating of all entities. Two of the dropouts occurred after the first rating was obtained and only one occurred after the second rating was obtained.

Experiment 4

The ownership groups were determined based on self-report. Participants could indicate that they owned multiple types of entities. To create independent groups, we applied a hierarchy in the classification of participants based on estimated frequency of ownership. Participants who owned a robot were assigned to the robot group regardless of if they owned a pet or voice assistant. Participants who owned a voice assistant were assigned to the voice assistant group regardless of if they owned a pet. Participants who did not own a voice assistant or robot but did own a pet were in the pet group. Average ownership duration was 2.2 years for robot owners, 1.9 years for voice assistant owners, and 4.8 years for pet owners. Participants who owned none of the entities were assigned to the no-owner group. Instructions were given in either English, Dutch, or Turkish based on the preference of the participants.

Dissimilarity Matrices

For both dimensions separately and for each participant, the observed rating data was converted into dissimilarity matrices, indicating pairwise dissimilarity between all tested entities in terms of agency or experience. Each cell of the resulting symmetrical 48´48 (Experiment 1-3) or 54´54 (Experiment 4) matrix represented the Euclidean distance in ratings between two entities. Smaller values indicated that two entities received less dissimilar ratings whereas higher values indicate that two entities received more dissimilar ratings. Group-level visualizations were obtained by averaging participants' standardized dissimilarity matrices.

Theoretical Matrices

The four theoretical matrices were created based on competing binary distinctions underlying the representational organization of mind perception (e.g., animacy or affordance of social interaction; Figure 1E). We conducted our main analyses on the animacy, face, social, and interaction model. A control model based on image luminance was created to ensure that luminance does not explain differences in model fit. We further created three exploratory matrices (Cozmo, Google Home, and Voice Assistant model) that were exploratorily compared to the main (dominant) models in terms of model fit in Experiment 2-3 using direct comparison (t tests).

Mantel Correlations

For agency and experience ratings separately, individual-level dissimilarity matrices were correlated to theoretical matrices using a Spearman Mantel test (vegan in R, 9999 permutations, 70). This produced one Mantel correlation coefficient per participant, theoretical matrix, and dimension of mind perception. A higher Mantel correlation indicates a higher overlap between two dissimilarity matrices. Individual-level Mantel correlations with the four main models were used as dependent variables in the linear mixed-effects analysis using lme4 and lmerTest in R (71). Similar results were found when rerunning the analysis with Fisher z-transformed values. We calculated the noise ceiling for each sample separately for experience and agency. For each participant, the vectorized representational dissimilarity matrix was correlated with the average dissimilarity vector of all remaining participants. The resulting correlations were then averaged to obtain a noise-ceiling estimate. We further investigated the effect of exposure on model fit by correlating (Spearman) the obtained mantel r with either interaction frequency (Experiment 3) or ownership duration (Experiment 4). To compare ownership groups in Experiment 4, we computed pairwise correlations (Spearman) between the group-average dissimilarity matrices of the ownership groups.

Latent models

We explored possible latent representational models underlying the attribution of agency using a data-driven approach. We created an average dissimilarity matrix across participants of Experiment 1 for agency ratings. We then used Partitioning Around Medoids clustering, considering solutions ranging from two to eight clusters. We used average silhouette width to evaluate cluster solutions. Based on the data-driven cluster solution, we built a binary model that distinguishes the two clusters. In this matrix, entities belonging to the same cluster were assigned a 0 and entities belonging to different clusters were assigned a 1. Generalizability of this datadriven model was then tested using the described RSA procedure on two independent samples stemming from the baseline ratings of Experiment 2 and 3.
