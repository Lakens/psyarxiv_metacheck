About the personal-information flags. These are pattern matches, not confirmed disclosures, and they are tuned to over-report rather than miss something. Expect false positives:

  • Column names are matched against identifying words in English and other European languages (email, telefoonnummer, geboortedatum, …). A column called phone_number flags whether or not it holds one, and an empty or already-anonymised column flags on its name alone.
  • Values are matched against the shape of an identifier. An IP address pattern also matches a version string or a dotted numeric code; a card-number pattern requires both a real issuer prefix and a checksum, but a long numeric ID can still coincide.
  • Free text is flagged when a column holds long, mostly-distinct prose — the shape open-ended answers take. Most such columns contain no names at all.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard), also Internal Review Board - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term); not IEC followed by a number, which is a technical standard - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee/Commission, in the singular or plural - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee, Bioethics Committee/Commission, Ethikkommission - Ethical Review Authority/Unit/Office/Agency - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Human Research Protection Office/Program, Office for Human Subjects Protection - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee(s) on/for (Medical and Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt - Ethics review that was not needed: Ethics review was not required, did not require further ethical review, exempt from formal human research ethics review, no new institutional ethics review

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Read more in the metacheck manual.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. A TRO uses them for each trov:TransparentResearchPerformance. * run_results$exit_status: the exit status of the process that ran the script (0 = ended normally); NA when the process did not report one (a time limit, or Docker failing to start the container). * artifacts: one row per file, with stage (deposit: as downloaded from the repository, hashed from the downloaded file; before_run: the run folder just before the first script ran, after metacheck’s own changes, such as rewritten paths and unpacked archives; after_run: only the files the scripts made (status new), changed (changed) or removed (removed), as an unchanged file is the one listed before the run), path (relative to the run folder, as valid UTF-8, a byte of another encoding written as <xx>), size (bytes), sha256, mime_type (by extension, NA when unknown), source (the file’s address in the repository, for deposit). The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). Files metacheck itself copied or made between passes (see modifications, output_copied, file_placed) show in after_run too. A TRO uses the rows as trov:ResearchArtifacts and each stage as a trov:ArtifactArrangement. * modifications$sha256_before, modifications$sha256_after: the hash of the changed script as downloaded and as it ran; NA when the script is not at the same path in both (a script made from an R Markdown file). * environment: the system that ran the code (trov:TransparentResearchSystem in a TRO): metacheck_version, started_at, ended_at, host (R version, platform, operating system), sandbox, network_isolated (TRUE only when every Docker run had --network none; the process sandbox does not stop the network), internet, timeout_per_script, total_timeout, python_local (the Python run outside Docker and its version), and docker: runs (for each kind of run, r_run, r_install, python_run, python_install, the image and the docker run arguments, such as --network none, --read-only, --cap-drop ALL, no-new-privileges, the process limit and the memory and processor limits) and images (for each image, its ID and digests from docker image inspect, its creation time and the R and Python versions its environment declares).

run_results$checked says what the run of each script shows about the code, in five groups, so that a script that does not reproduce is kept apart from one the check could not test: ran (it ran to the end); code_error (it did not reproduce: it stopped with an error of the code or the deposit, could not be read, or needs a file the deposit does not hold); dependency (a package or program was not available: a package that could not be installed or was not loaded, a program outside R and Python, a system library, or R crashing in a package’s compiled code); time (it did not finish within the time limit for one script, or was not started because the time for all scripts had run out); and not_checkable (it could not be checked automatically: it needs a person, the internet, a key or another setting of the authors’ computer, one platform such as Google Colab, Windows or OpenSAFELY, more memory than the run allows, a file that was not downloaded, a screen, or a program that runs it, or it is a file of a package of the code that Python runs only as a module of that package). It is NA for files run inside another script, files that are not an analysis, files not read, and a Docker failure. summary_table counts each group (repro_ran_n, repro_code_error_n, repro_dependency_n, repro_time_n, repro_not_checkable_n), and the report’s first sentence gives them. The traffic light reflects the static readiness signals by default; a script in code_error that stopped with an error makes it red, as a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure, and a script in one of the other three groups keeps it from being green. Calls that need a screen, which the run does not have (dev.print(), dev.copy2pdf(), dev.copy2eps(), savePlot(), windows(), win.graph(), x11(), quartz(), View()), are replaced before the run by calls that need none, which modifications records as screen_call_replaced. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

Large language models (LLMs) have been shown to reproduce central aspects of human personality. This correspondence creates opportunities to automate research, but its theoretical value will depend on what LLMs recover and what accounts for their discrepancies with human data. We propose a heuristic distinction between model-derived variance-the regularities in human personality data recoverable from LLM predictions-and residual variance-the structure that remains unexplained by those predictions. LLM-human correspondence may reflect several factors, such as general semantic structure, culturally shared beliefs, scientific findings in LLM training corpora, or psychometric finetuning. Residual variance, in turn, may reflect model limitations, population-specific variation, or psychological information not captured in language. Using this distinction, we examine three applications in personality psychology with substantial potential for automation: enhancing assessment, improving conceptual clarity, and simulating human respondents. Current evidence suggests that linguistic representations account for a substantial share of LLM-human correspondence, with psychometric fine-tuning providing additional gains; however, the relative contribution of other factors remains unresolved. We argue that automation can contribute to conceptual progress to the extent that the sources and limits of LLM-human correspondence are identified. Doing so can establish what similarities and divergences reveal about personality itself and where automation is justified.

1 Introduction

Large Language Models (LLMs) are artificial intelligence systems trained to interpret and generate human-like text. Their rapid adoption in psychological research, including personality psychology (Abdurahman, 2024; Demszky et al., 2023; Hussain, Binz, et al., 2024), suggests a deep alignment between these models and the field itself. Personality psychology remains fundamentally a language-based science (Furr, 2009; Rauthmann, 2020). Language is both its primary measurement tool-through items, inventories, and behavioral descriptions-and a core object of theory. The lexical hypothesis, in particular, assumes that personality structure is encoded in natural language and can be recovered from it (Galton, 1884). If this is the case, it is natural to expect that systems trained on large-scale linguistic data can at least approximate established empirical methods to uncover personality structure. Emerging evidence supports this idea, showing that LLMs can reproduce key empirical regularities in personality research (e.g., Guenole et al., 2024; Hommel & Arslan, 2025; Schoedel et al., 2026; Wulff & Mata, 2025a).

In this article, we argue that LLMs are well positioned to automate core elements of personality psychology. By automation, we mean the delegation of research operations to computational systems, reducing time and cost, and reliance on newly collected human data, while potentially increasing accuracy. The case for such automation rests on the correspondence between LLM-derived predictions and patterns in human personality data. Yet this correspondence also raises fundamental questions: What do LLMs actually recover, what do human data uniquely contribute, and under what conditions is LLM-based automation scientifically justified?

We address these questions in three parts. First, we introduce the training processes and capabilities that support LLM applications in personality psychology. We then introduce the distinction between model-derived and residual variance that we use to evaluate three domains with substantial automation potential: enhancing assessment, improving conceptual clarity, and simulating human respondents. Finally, we discuss the conditions necessary for automation to advance not only efficiency but also conceptual progress in personality psychology.

2 A primer on large language models for personality psychology

Large language models are systems trained to predict language by learning from massive text corpora. In doing so, they acquire rich representations of meaning and world knowledge that can be repurposed for tasks far beyond their original training objective (for introductions, see Abdurahman, 2024; Hussain, Binz, et al., 2024). Before describing the capabilities that make LLMs relevant to personality research, it is important to understand what these models have been trained on and how this shapes what they can and cannot do.

2.1 What LLMs are trained on

The capabilities of LLMs stem largely from their training data. Modern LLMs are pretrained on massive corpora comprising web pages, books, academic articles, social media posts, news articles, and many other digitized cultural products spanning trillions of tokens (e.g., words, subwords, punctuation, numbers) across multiple languages (Hussain, Binz, et al., 2024). These corpora are not neutral samples of human experience, but they skew toward English-language text, toward populations with high internet access and digital participation, and toward written, formal registers over spoken, colloquial, or private language. Consequently, specific demographic groups, cultural contexts, and forms of psychological experience, particularly those rarely documented in writing, may be poorly represented or absent (Atari et al., 2023; Crockett & Messeri, 2026).

Pretraining is typically followed by additional training stages that further shape model behavior. Many LLMs undergo post-training, including instruction tuning and reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF), which adjust model outputs to improve human ratings of helpfulness, safety, and user satisfaction. Alternatively or additionally, models can be fine-tuned on empirical personality data, such as observed interitem correlations or scale-level psychometric properties (Hommel & Arslan, 2025; Hommel et al., 2026; Wulff & Mata, 2025a); such fine-tuning typically starts from base or embedding models rather than from post-trained models. Different applications draw on different model types. Embeddingbased analyses typically rely on base or sentencetransformer models not subject to post-training alignment, whereas text-generation applications, such as silicon sampling or informant-style ratings, use instruction-tuned models shaped by both pretraining and alignment.

Each training stage constrains what a model's performance can show and where it can go wrong. Pretraining bounds the regularities a model can recover to what its corpora document, so estimates for underrepresented populations, culturally specific constructs, or rarely discussed psychological traits may be unreliable or systematically distorted. When an off-the-shelf model nonetheless recovers empirical personality structure, this is informative about what natural language encodes. When a model is fine-tuned on psychometric data, it instead shows that such structure is learnable and transferable across instruments and datasets (see Fig. 1B). Both results are valuable, but they answer different questions, and interpreting one as the other risks overstating what language alone provides. Post-training, in turn, can introduce biases of its own, such as a left-libertarian political lean (Hartmann et al., 2023) and socially desirable answers to personality items (Salecha et al., 2024), which post-training may introduce or amplify, and it interacts with two widely discussed failure modes. Hallucinations, fluent but confabulated content, remain possible after posttraining and can even be amplified when models are tuned on material beyond their pretraining knowledge (Gekhman et al., 2024), although rates have declined substantially as models have scaled, at least for tasks in which models work from provided source material rather than from memory (Kalai et al., 2025; Tamber et al., 2025). Sycophancy, the tendency to align responses with perceived user expectations, is exacerbated by optimizing on human ratings (Sharma et al., 2024) and is relevant for the generation-based applications discussed below, where it can skew responses toward confirming researchers' expectations.

In what follows, we discuss two capabilities of LLMs particularly relevant for automating personality research and which operate through different mechanisms and carry different implications: the extraction of embeddings (i.e., numerical representations that enable quantitative measurement of meaning), and text generation.

2.2 Quantifying Meaning Using Embeddings

Embeddings are numerical representations of text that capture semantic meaning. They are produced by mapping units of text, such as words, sentences, or longer passages, into high-dimensional vector spaces, where proximity reflects similarity in meaning. The embeddings for sociable and outgoing will be close together; sociable and meticulous will be farther apart. This geometric structure emerges from training: models learn to place texts that occur in similar linguistic contexts near one another.

Embeddings can be extracted from different types of models. Early static word embeddings (e.g., Word2Vec, GloVe) assign a fixed vector per word and cannot represent context-dependent meaning. Modern transformer-based models produce contextualized embeddings that are semantically richer and better equipped to interpret linguistic uncertainty (e.g., polysemy, neologisms). For example, the word "drive" in the item "I have a strong drive to succeed" will yield a different vector representation than the same word in a different statement, such as "I drive faster than the speed limit." Beyond token-level embeddings, models can encode the meaning of entire sentences or paragraphs, which makes them wellsuited for processing personality items, construct definitions, or behavioral descriptions.

A central operation enabled by embeddings is quantifying semantic similarity, most commonly by computing cosine similarity between two semantic vectors, though alternatives have been proposed (Steck et al., 2024). These continuous similarity measures are powerful for personality research because much of the field's empirical work rests on relationships among verbal stimuli: items that cluster, constructs that overlap or diverge, and descriptions that align with trait categories. Embedding-based similarity can predict empirical inter-item correlations, recover factor structures, and approximate divergent validity between scales (Cutler & Condon, 2023; Hommel & Arslan, 2025; Kilmen & Bulut, 2025; Wulff & Mata, 2025a). This predictive success reflects that semantics, as used here, extends beyond dictionary-like knowledge of word meanings to general knowledge about the world expressed in language (Hussain, Mata, et al., 2024; Kumar, 2021). 2.3 Text Generation

Text generation refers to the capacity of LLMs to produce coherent, contextually appropriate text in response to prompts. By learning to predict the next token in a sequence across vast corpora, LLMs acquire not only linguistic fluency but also broad world knowledge, including psychological concepts and human behavior. In practice, this capability is accessed through prompting, that is, providing natural-language instructions that specify a task, which can range from rephrasing an item to directly evaluating psychological content. This makes text generation a generalpurpose tool: rather than requiring dedicated software or trained raters for each task, researchers can address diverse problems through carefully designed prompts (Hussain, Binz, et al., 2024; Törnberg, 2024). Work in personality psychology has begun to exploit this. Text generation has been used to create and translate items (Götz et al., 2024; Hommel et al., 2022), estimate item properties such as difficulty or social desirability (Hommel, 2023; Leistner et al., 2025), predict inter-item correlations (Schoenegger et al., 2025), and estimate personality dimensions from free-text responses (Peters & Matz, 2024; Wright et al., 2026). Another prominent application involves using LLMs as simulated participants, so-called silicon samples, which will be discussed in more detail below.

3 A framework for evaluating automation

The technical capabilities described above make LLM-based automation possible, but they do not determine when such automation is justified. That judgment depends on which patterns in human data a model recovers and on the nature of what remains unexplained. It may be helpful to organize evaluation around a distinction between model-derived variance and residual variance. For a specified empirical target-such as an inter-item correlation matrix or the factor structure of a new inventory-model-derived variance denotes the structure captured by model predictions, whereas residual variance denotes the discrepancy between predicted and observed correlations. We use this distinction as an organizing heuristic, without implying that it is always feasible to perform a formal statistical decomposition: When the target is directly quantifiable, as when semantic similarities are used to predict empirical inter-scale correlations (Hommel & Arslan, 2025; Wulff & Mata, 2025a), correspondence and discrepancy can be quantified; in other applications, the distinction may remain a conceptual guide. Neither component, model-derived or residual variance, is a fixed property of LLMs or human personality: their balance depends on the model, the target population, and the criterion used to evaluate correspondence. Crucially, LLM-human correspondence can arise through several nonexclusive channels. First, training corpora contain descriptions of actual behavior and its consequences in news, biographies, social media, clinical reports, and other records. Second, fiction and other cultural products encode shared beliefs, implicit theories, and stereotypes about how traits cohere and manifest. Third, training corpora include the scientific literature itself, including published findings on personality structure and trait-outcome associations. Fourth, together these sources produce a general understanding of personality-related language that human respondents share and draw on when answering items. Model and respondent can therefore agree on how items relate simply because they interpret them alike, whether or not those interpretations track actual behavior. Finally, psychometric fine-tuning can give models direct exposure to empirical personality data. These channels have different inferential implications. Correspondence may reflect documented behavioral regularities, culturally shared assumptions, prior exposure to published findings, general semantic overlap, or structure learned through empirical optimization. The channels can also interact, as when scientific frameworks shape how personality is represented in broader culture. LLM-human correspondence does not, by itself, identify what a model has recovered.

The sources of LLM-human discrepancies can be similarly heterogeneous. They may reflect limitations or biases in the model, populations and experiences underrepresented in its training data, meaningful individual-or population-specific variation in test samples, method effects, measurement error, or sampling variability. The human data are themselves fallible, and where they are noisy or biased, the model could be the more accurate option (Wright et al., 2026). Discrepancy should therefore not automatically be interpreted as uniquely human psychological variance, just as correspondence should not automatically be interpreted as recovery of substantive psychological structure.

This framework helps clarify what evidence would justify automation. Automation is most defensible when the empirical target and intended use are clearly specified, model predictions generalize to independent human data from the relevant population, and plausible sources of LLM-human discrepancies are unlikely to affect the conclusions or decisions at stake. Importantly, this judgment depends on the relevance of the discrepancy, not only its magnitude: even a small discrepancy may matter if it reflects systematic individual-or population-specific variation. Human observation remains essential for establishing and reassessing generalization, and indispensable whenever the residual contains the phenomenon of interest. Accordingly, in what follows, we evaluate each application of LLMs to personality psychology by asking what models recover, what remains unexplained, and whether the information lost through automation matters for the intended use.

4 Opportunities for automating personality psychology

The framework raises the question of where these conditions are met. If personality-relevant information is encoded in language, and LLMs have learned to represent it, then much of the empirical pipeline in personality research, from developing scales to mapping construct relationships to estimating trait correlations, may be well captured by these models and recoverable without collecting new human data. We examine three domains that we believe show the most promise for automation and use the distinction between model-derived and residual variance to assess, for each, where this balance lies. We return to a broader assessment across applications in the Discussion (see Table 1).

4.1 Enhancing Personality Assessment

Personality assessment relies heavily on selfreport scales, and developing these scales involves a labor-intensive pipeline of item generation, psychometric evaluation, and scale compilation. LLMs can automate each stage, potentially compressing a process that spans months into hours. These applications are strong candidates for automation because they depend primarily on population-level regularities in the structure of personality-relevant language that are well represented in LLM training corpora.

4.1.1 Automating Scale Development.

Item generation, the first step in developing a scale, has long resisted automation. Earlier approaches to automated item generation succeeded only for knowledge and reasoning tests, where items have correct answers and the features that make an item easy or difficult are relatively well defined (Arendasy & Sommer, 2012; Gierl & Lai, 2013). Generating Likert-style items for personality measurement is qualitatively different because it requires producing statements that are aligned with a target construct's meaning. LLMs are well-suited to this task. Recent work has shown that LLMs can generate large pools of personality items within seconds, given only a few example items or a construct description as input (Götz et al., 2024; Hernandez & Nie, 2023; Hommel et al., 2022; P. Lee et al., 2025). The resulting items are not merely plausible on the surface: when compiled into scales and administered to human samples, LLM-generated item sets show factor structures and inter-scale correlations broadly comparable to established measures (Götz et al., 2024; Hommel et al., 2022; Russell-Lasalandra et al., 2026). Beyond item generation, LLMs can support scale compilation and shortening. For compilation, embeddings can be used to run psychometric analyses, such as network modeling or factor analysis, directly on semantic relationships between items, enabling researchers to assemble and evaluate scales before collecting any human data (Guenole et al., 2024; Hernandez & Nie, 2023; Russell-Lasalandra et al., 2026). Validation against human samples shows that scales compiled through such pipelines exhibit stable structural properties. For shortening, embedding-based similarity can identify semantically redundant items, achieving model fit in confirmatory factor analyses comparable to established optimization methods-again without requiring human responses (Jung & Seo, 2025; Kilmen & Bulut, 2025).

That LLM-based approaches match traditional methods with dramatically lower demands on data collection and researcher time suggests that model-derived variance already accounts for much of what several stages of scale development are designed to optimize. Item generation, scale compilation, and shortening are fundamentally concerned with organizing meaning, and the aggregate regularities captured in LLM representations may often be sufficient for these purposes. Human data remain necessary, however, to fully establish how scales function across populations and whether they predict meaningful real-world outcomes.

At the same time, the ease of automated item generation could encourage a proliferation of scales lacking theoretical grounding (Anvari et al., 2025; Elson et al., 2023; Wulff & Mata, 2025b), and standardized procedures for validating AI-generated items and scales have yet to be established (Fyffe et al., 2024; Götz et al., 2024; Hommel et al., 2022; Russell-Lasalandra et al., 2026). In our view, validation standards should not depend on how an item or scale was generated. Any instrument, whether humanor machine-authored, should demonstrate nonredundancy with existing measures, be normed and validated in independent samples (Elson et al., 2023), and, above all, predict meaningful real-world outcomes. We argue, moreover, that parity with current practice is an unambitious benchmark. Scale developers must balance several demands at once, such as calibrating items to cover the intended range of trait expression, observing writing guidelines that safeguard item quality (e.g., avoiding double-barreled or ambiguous phrasing), and occasionally meeting specific constraints, such as balancing perceived desirability across forced-choice alternatives.

4.1.2 Accessing Neglected Data Sources and Assessment Methods.

While the previous subsection focused on automating established practices on how we measure personality, LLMs also expand what personality-relevant data can be assessed. Beyond traditional self-report measures, personality psychologists have long recognized the value of alternative data sources, including behavioral observations, conversational speech, and unstructured text from interviews, open-ended survey responses, or social media posts (Koutsoumpis & De Vries, 2022; G. Park et al., 2015; Schliebener & Dufner, 2024; Valente et al., 2012; Youyou et al., 2015). In practice, these sources have remained underutilized because processing them (i.e., transcribing audio, classifying behavioral content, or rating responses) is highly resource-intensive and typically requires trained human coders. LLMs remove this bottleneck. In what follows, we consider three roles LLMs can play in this process, namely as external informants, as automated coders, and as conversational interviewers.

As external informants, LLMs can translate unstructured data into standardized test responses. Provided with a person's text corpus, such as transcribed verbalizations, daily diary entries, or social media posts, an LLM can be prompted to "get to know" the person and then complete established personality inventories on their behalf, generating informant ratings that correlate significantly with self-reports, in some cases more closely than ratings provided by family or friends (Peters & Matz, 2024; Wright et al., 2026). Whether such ratings are psychometrically equivalent to traditional informants remains an empirical question, but LLMs appear to offer viable alternatives.

As automated coders, LLMs can go a step further and bypass standardized instruments altogether, mapping raw behavioral traces or free text directly onto psychological dimensions. Where earlier machine-learning approaches required large labeled datasets linking digital footprints to traits (Kosinski et al., 2013), LLMs perform such scoring zero-shot: they can detect psychological constructs in text across languages (Rathje et al., 2024), infer Big Five traits from social media posts (Peters & Matz, 2024), and score politicians' public personality traits directly from parliamentary speeches (Birkenmaier & Lechner, 2026), providing a pipeline for data sources that were previously impractical to process at scale.

Finally, as conversational interviewers, LLMs can actively generate new personality-relevant data rather than merely processing existing text. Unlike rigid static surveys, an LLM interviewer can dynamically ask follow-up questions, probe ambiguous answers, and prompt participants to elaborate on specific behavioral examples in real time, combining the standardization of psychological assessment with the depth and ecological validity of qualitative interviewing. AI-led interviewing has proven feasible as a general methodology, yielding data quality comparable to that of human interviewers at scale (Geiecke & Jaravel, 2024; Wuttke et al., 2025), and the first personality-specific implementations show that trait scores derived from guided conversations converge moderately with self-reports and standard inventories (J. Lee et al., 2024; Matšenas et al., 2026; Peters et al., 2024). The resulting transcripts can then be fed directly into the informant and coding pipelines described above, closing the cycle from data generation to psychometric scoring.

However, this automated scoring of unstructured data raises fundamental questions about validation. First, the quality of any assessment is bounded by the suitability of the source text, because not all natural language is equally diagnostic of personality. Second, whereas scale development targets population-level structure, the applications in this section aim to score individuals. They thus rest on the assumption that the aggregate regularities linking linguistic markers to constructs, which constitute the modelderived variance in this domain, transfer to the individual case; how much individual-level variation escapes them is precisely the residual at stake. Third, scoring text according to established inventories applies existing taxonomies at scale but never expands them. Human coders share this limitation, but automation changes its consequences. A constraint that once operated on hundreds of hand-coded transcripts now operates on millions of documents, entrenching taxonomies precisely where new phenomena might have been noticed. Finally, there remains the question of what serves as ground truth for validating LLM-derived scores. Human gold standards are difficult to obtain at scale, and in many realworld settings where automated scoring is most attractive, such as social media feeds or public speech records, no self-reports or expert annotations exist. Where traditional references are available, namely human-coded annotations and self-reports, they are themselves fallible, so disagreement between model-derived and traditional scores cannot simply be read as model error: it may equally reflect noise or bias in the benchmark, an ambiguity that can only be resolved by the kind of decomposition we discuss below.

4.2 Improving Conceptual Clarity

Personality psychology faces persistent challenges around the organization of its core constructs, including how many dimensions are needed, where one construct ends and another begins, and whether existing labels carve up the space accurately. If the previous section described how LLMs can automate the production and scoring of personality data, this section concerns a more fundamental question: whether LLMs can help the field audit its own conceptual architecture. These applications are strong candidates for automation because they are overwhelmingly linguistic, asking whether two labels refer to the same construct or whether an item belongs under a given heading, which is precisely the kind of information richly represented in training corpora.

4.2.1 Mapping Conceptual Structure.

The dominant structural models of personality, most prominently the Big Five (Goldberg, 1990) and HEXACO (Ashton et al., 2004), were derived from factor analyses of personality-descriptive adjectives and follow the lexical hypothesis (i.e., the idea that important individual differences are encoded in natural language Galton, 1884). Despite wide acceptance, these models face recurring criticism: they often fail confirmatory tests (Hopwood & Donnellan, 2010; Marsh et al., 2010), may under-represent the breadth of personality (Altgassen et al., 2024), and depend on adjective sets selected through subjective rather than standardized procedures (Saucier, 1997). LLMs offer a way to revisit these foundations. Embeddings can compute semantic relationships between the entire set of personality descriptors and subject them to structural analyses such as cluster analysis or principal components analysis (PCA), and structures derived in this way resemble those obtained from human ratings, though not for every factor (Cutler & Condon, 2023). Because embeddings are available for any language with sufficient training data (e.g., Wang et al., 2024), these procedures also enable direct cross-linguistic comparisons, opening new avenues for cross-cultural research.

In terms of our framework, embedding-derived structures are model-derived variance in a strict sense. They reflect the aggregated semantic structure of a language as a whole and thus abstract away from the very thing personality psychology set out to study, namely differences between individual people. The residual here is not incidental but constitutive. A related, more tractable limitation concerns samples. Personality structure can differ with a sample's demographics, culture, or clinical status, whereas a structure derived from language in the aggregate reflects the mixture of populations whose language dominates the training corpus. This raises questions of measurement invariance when model-derived structure is mapped onto real-world groups. Unlike the absence of person-level information, however, this limitation is not fixed: models can in principle be conditioned or fine-tuned to yield population-specific predictions, although whether such predictions track actual group differences or merely stereotypes about them must be established against human data from the respective populations.

4.2.2 Evaluating Overlap between Scales.

A persistent problem in personality research is redundancy at the scale level. The field has accumulated many overlapping instruments, and detecting this overlap traditionally requires collecting responses to multiple scales, a costly and burdensome process. Embedding-based approaches can instead evaluate semantic similarity between items and scales before any data collection, building on earlier efforts using latent semantic analysis (Rosenbusch et al., 2020) but with substantially greater precision through modern transformer-based embeddings and fine-tuned LLMs (Hommel et al., 2026). For instance, Wulff and Mata (2025a) computed cosine similarities between sentence embeddings of 459 scales from the International Personality Item Pool and showed, in empirical data for five inventories, that these similarities predicted correlations between scales (out-of-sample r ≈ .63) (see also, Hommel & Arslan, 2025). By identifying which scales are semantically redundant, researchers can streamline assessment batteries, reducing participant burden and clarifying each instrument's unique contribution before investing in empirical validation.

Here, the decomposition into model-derived and residual variance is directly observable: the accuracy of the semantic prediction quantifies the model-derived share of inter-scale covariance, and the prediction errors constitute the residual. This residual points to variance that language alone does not capture, including potential method effects and population-specific response patterns.

4.2.3 Detecting Mislabeling across

Constructs.

Beyond scale-level redundancy, the field faces a deeper organizational problem: the proliferation of constructs and labels that fragments the literature and hinders cumulative progress (Anvari et al., 2025; Hanfstingl et al., 2024). This fragmentation manifests as jingle fallacies, in which distinct constructs share a label, and jangle fallacies, in which the same construct carries different labels (Aikins, 1902; Kelley, 1927). LLMs can help by analyzing semantic similarity between construct labels and their associated measures, enabling screening for jingle-jangle fallacies that is more systematic than manual review because it operates over the full set of instruments at once (Wulff & Mata, 2025a , 2025b). The Wulff and Mata (2025a) analysis provides concrete examples: scales with different names (e.g., Exhibitionism in the HPI-HIC and Snyder's Self-monitoring scale) were semantically near-identical, and identically named scales (e.g., Emotionality in the 16PF and in the NEO) diverged considerably in item content. These are the kinds of fallacies the field has long recognized but lacked scalable tools to detect (Hanfstingl et al., 2024).

In principle, LLMs could go beyond detection and automatically reassign items to more appropriate construct labels (Fyffe et al., 2024; Wulff & Mata, 2025a). This more ambitious strategy, however, faces critical bottlenecks. There is no exhaustive or accepted list of construct labels in psychology, leaving the target space for reassignment undefined. An LLM's representation of psychological concepts may be skewed by nonacademic language use, in which case its reassignments would inherit the distortion. And if finetuning on psychological taxonomies is required, the training data must contain correct label-item assignments, yet the pervasiveness of jingle-jangle fallacies means these cannot be trusted; training on mislabeled data risks reproducing the problematic assignments, effectively laundering human measurement error through an automated pipeline (e.g., Larsen et al., 2025).

These bottlenecks invite a more radical thought, on which the authors of this article hold different views. If reassigning items to labels is so fraught, embedding-based content search could bypass labels altogether, substituting search by content for search by name (Hommel et al., 2026). The authors of this article are divided on this prospect. Some of us favor it. Construct labels are inherently reductive, as a single word or phrase compresses heterogeneous item content into one name, and some constructs may not be adequately described by any label at all. Shoehorning scales into labels therefore carries its own risks, including construct drift (Kevin Bolinskey & Nichols, 2011), which itself constitutes one of the mechanisms that may give rise to jingle-jangle fallacies. Others among us judge these risks worth bearing, because labels are more than indexing conventions. They function as theoretical commitments that bundle expectations about antecedents, correlates, and outcomes, supporting hypothesis generation precisely where measurement is noisy, and their looseness can be productive, allowing a construct to connect otherwise separate research contexts (Hutmacher & Franz, 2025). On this view, the problem jinglejangle detection addresses is not that the field uses labels but that its labels are poorly calibrated to content. We agree, however, that computational mapping combined with expert deliberation can improve the alignment between labels and content, with the caveat that perfect alignment is unattainable for concepts whose meanings remain in flux (wulff"˙escaping"˙2025; Hutmacher & Franz, 2025).

In this domain, detecting mismatches between labels and content is a question of language, and model-derived variance accordingly accounts for most of what detection requires. Deciding how to resolve a detected mismatch is not, since semantic proximity does not guarantee theoretical identity, as when subjective stress and its physiological counterpart remain distinct despite similar language (Wulff & Mata, 2025b). Whether semantic similarity as captured by LLMs reflects psychological rather than merely lexical similarity thus remains the central open question for this application.

4.3 Simulating Human Respondents

A distinctive promise of LLMs is that they may stand in for human respondents: not just processing personality-relevant text, but generating synthetic data that approximate empirical results. Combined with the automation described above, this raises the most provocative possibility, namely that parts of personality research could be conducted without human participants at all. It is also where the limits of model-derived representations matter most.

4.3.1 Simulating Population-Level Structure.

Nomothetic personality research depends on stable correlations between constructs, which require large samples and significant investment. These costs are compounded by growing evidence that commercial online panels are increasingly at risk of degradation by LLM-generated input (Gordon et al., 2026; Rilla, Nussberger, et al., 2026; Rilla, Werner, et al., 2026). LLMs offer a way to sidestep this. One approach extracts construct relationships directly from the model by prompting it to estimate correlations or compute associations from item embeddings; such model-internal correlations can mirror human data with substantial accuracy (Hommel & Arslan, 2025; Wulff & Mata, 2025a). A second approach, silicon sampling, prompts LLMs to adopt participant personas and respond to psychometric scales, with responses aggregated to the sample level. This involves many methodological decisions, such as model selection, temperature settings, and prompt design (Cummins, 2026), and known artifacts, such as discrepant responses to reversed items (Meyer et al., 2026) and a lack of realistic variance (Bisbee et al., 2024). Even so, silicon samples can yield personality scores with convergent, discriminant, and criterion-validity patterns resembling human psychometric data (Serapio-García et al., 2025), and they can be combined with human samples in mixed-subject designs to increase statistical power without large-scale recruitment (Broska et al., 2025). The success of these approaches implies that a large share of the covariance structure in personality data is model-derived variance: recoverable from language because training corpora encode real psychological regularities rather than surfacelevel word associations. But where empirical correlations diverge from model-derived predictions, synthetic data will systematically miss the discrepancy. The residual behind such divergence is heterogeneous, spanning genuine population-level variation, measurement artifacts such as acquiescence bias or careless responding, and sampling noise; disentangling these components is a challenge we return to in the Discussion.

4.3.2 Simulating Specific Individuals and Groups.

Silicon sampling can also be used to represent specific individuals or groups. Varying sociodemographic, cultural, or personality descriptors in a prompt yields synthetic cohorts representing particular populations, and conditioning models on rich individual data extends this to single persons: generative agents built from two-hour interviews reproduced participants' survey responses and Big Five profiles with high fidelity (J. S. Park et al., 2024). For individuals, however, high correspondence with previously observed responses does not necessarily demonstrate that a model has captured a generative representation of the person. It may instead reflect accurate extraction and reconstruction of information already contained in the conditioning material. A stronger test would require predicting the individual's responses or behavior in new situations, at later time points, or on outcomes not represented in the source data. Currently, silicon samples do not appear to reliably produce response patterns matching the specified targets (Cummins, 2026), though this may improve as models develop. Worse, in this domain model-derived variance is not just incomplete but potentially misleading. LLMs inherit the distributional biases of their training data, which skew toward WEIRD populations and English-language text (Crockett & Messeri, 2026), so silicon samples may reproduce learned stereotypes, or the Western literature's assumptions about cross-cultural differences, rather than genuine psychological variation. For population simulation, the residual is not merely noise to be averaged out but the very population-specific variation the research aims to characterize. Synthetic data therefore cannot substitute for cross-cultural and field research on underrepresented populations. At best, simulations serve to generate hypotheses: targeted, falsifiable predictions about how personality structure varies across populations, to be tested with human data.

5 Discussion

Across the applications reviewed above, the case for automation rests on a common empirical premise: model-derived estimates reproduce substantial regularities in human personality data. Yet correspondence with human data is not an end in itself. Its scientific value depends on what models recover, what accounts for their discrepancies with human data, and whether those discrepancies matter for intended future uses. We therefore assess where automation currently stands and what theoretical and methodological conditions are needed to make it productive for future work in personality psychology.

5.1 Where automation stands

The evidence reviewed here supports a generalization: the more a task depends on regularities well represented in LLM training corpora, the more amenable it is to automation. The clearest evidence is that model-derived similarities between personality items and scales predict their empirical correlations in human data (Guenole et al., 2024; Hommel & Arslan, 2025; Wulff & Mata, 2025a). The strength and meaning of this correspondence depend on how a model was trained. Offthe-shelf models typically recover less structure than models fine-tuned on psychometric data (see Fig. 1B), and these results address different questions rather than representing points on a single accuracy scale. Recovery by off-the-shelf models bears on what their general linguistic training encodes, whereas gains following psychometric fine-tuning show what empirical structure can be learned and transferred across instruments. Both interpretations are complicated by training-data contamination: corpora may contain the benchmarks, correlation matrices, scale manuals, or published findings used to evaluate a model (Balloccu et al., 2024). Post-training can also distort generated responses through objectives such as helpfulness and user satisfaction, while psychometric fine-tuning can calibrate embedding models to the instruments and populations represented in the training data (Hartmann et al., 2023; Hommel & Arslan, 2025; Hommel et al., 2026; Salecha et al., 2024; Sharma et al., 2024). Model performance must therefore be interpreted relative to the complete pipeline through which the model was produced.

The residual is similarly ambiguous. It may contain measurement artifacts and sampling variability, limitations of the model or its training corpus, and meaningful individual-or populationspecific variation. It should therefore be treated neither as a monolith of "genuine psychology" nor simply as noise to be discarded. Model-derived estimates may be useful when the target is a stable population-level regularity, and the residual is incidental to the research question. Human data remain essential when the target concerns information on the individual level, developmental processes, underrepresented populations, or phenomena poorly documented in language (Atari et al., 2023; Henrich et al., 2010; Schröder et al., 2025).

5.2 What the field needs to make automation productive

We hope to have established that neither LLMhuman correspondence nor discrepancy is selfinterpreting. Productive use of LLMs in personality psychology therefore requires theoretical clarity about what each application should capture and methodological infrastructure capable of testing the resulting claims. We focus on four requirements.

Defining personality and its validation targets.

Before deciding where automation is justified, the field must clarify what personality encompasses. If personality is defined and measured primarily through verbal self-reports and traitdescriptive language, LLM-human correspondence bears directly on the intended object of study: LLMs reproduce a structure encoded in the language through which personality is expressed and measured. However, if personality is thought of as encompassing behavioral dispositions, developmental trajectories, and embodied experience, then that correspondence has a meaningful boundary, and what models fail to recover is both theoretically and practically informative. Ultimately, this choice also determines the appropriate validation target. Construct maps and scales should generalize only to relevant outcomes; individual assessments should predict relevant informant judgments, behavior, or longitudinal outcomes; and LLM-based simulations should reproduce held-out patterns in their intended populations. Convergence with target measures is an appropriate criterion only when an LLM-based method is intended to reproduce them, but it is insufficient if the method claims to capture different (e.g., narrower, broader, or novel) aspects of personality. In the latter case, divergence or unique variance is valuable only if it predicts an independent, theoretically relevant criterion. Definitional choices therefore will ultimately determine whether LLMs will be simply used as cheaper replicas of existing language-based methods or genuinely new tools for studying and predicting human personality.

Benchmark data and comparative designs.

Evaluating claims about the LLM-human correspondence requires task-specific empirical reference data, yet the current infrastructure is inadequate. Online panel data may be increasingly contaminated by LLM-generated responses (Rilla, Werner, et al., 2026), and existing benchmarks were not designed for LLM validation. The field therefore needs large-scale, multi-lab efforts to collect high-quality human data using contamination-resistant protocols, with portions protected from inclusion in future training corpora. Benchmarks must cover distinct targets, because success in predicting factor structure does not imply success in reproducing response distributions or criterion validity. These data should support comparisons that vary the proposed sources of fit and misfit. Models with known training histories can be compared before and after psychometric fine-tuning; performance can be assessed across well-and poorly represented domains and populations; and predictions can be evaluated against self-reports, informant reports, behavioral observations, and longitudinal outcomes. For example, if model-derived structure primarily reflects implicit personality theories (Borkenau, 1992), it should correspond more closely with self-and informant reports than with observed behavior. Such comparative designs are needed to separate model limitations and measurement artifacts from meaningful psychological variation.

Open models.

Conducting these comparisons requires accessible models with documented training histories. Existing models fine-tuned on personality data demonstrate that domain-specific training can improve the recovery of psychometric structure across instruments (Hommel & Arslan, 2025; Hommel et al., 2026; Wulff & Mata, 2025a). The next step is not to develop ever more isolated specialized models, but to build a coordinated family of open models trained and evaluated across multiple instruments, methods, and populations. Matched base and fine-tuned versions would allow researchers to identify what empirical optimization adds beyond the structure already present in a general-purpose model. Open weights, code, and detailed documentation of training provenance are essential for reproducibility and bias auditing, while access to participant-level training data may require appropriate privacy and governance arrangements (Wulff et al., 2024). Following the multitrait-multimethod framework (Campbell & Fiske, 1959), future models could extend beyond covariance structures to self-reports, observer ratings, and behavioral data. Such infrastructure would make it possible to test generalization systematically and identify which forms of human data contribute information beyond what models recover from language.

Reporting standards and methodological training.

The flexibility of LLM-based research-including choices of model, embedding, prompt, temperature, and fine-tuning strategy-creates substantial researcher degrees of freedom (Cummins, 2026) and can amplify familiar problems such as measure proliferation and the entrenchment of existing taxonomies (Anvari et al., 2025; Wulff & Mata, 2025b). Shared reporting standards are needed to distinguish robust findings from pipeline artifacts, building on general guidelines for LLM use in the social and behavioral sciences (Feuerriegel et al., 2026). Because transparency does not itself constrain analytic flexibility, these standards should complement validation benchmarks and processlevel safeguards such as Registered Reports (e.g., Hommel & Arslan, 2025). They should also be accompanied by training in prompting, embedding extraction, fine-tuning, and the conceptual judgment required to determine when automation is appropriate (Abdurahman et al., 2025; Hussain, Binz, et al., 2024). Together, reporting and training can help prevent deskilling and preserve researchers' capacity to evaluate the methods they use (Ferdman, 2026; Wulff & Mata, 2026).

5.3 Conclusion

We have argued that LLMs can automate core operations in personality psychology, including enhancing assessment, clarifying construct structure, and simulating human respondents.

The key scientific question, however, is not simply how closely model-derived estimates match human data, but what both matches and mismatches mean. Addressing this question requires clear theoretical targets, appropriate empirical comparisons, and transparent research infrastructure. Doing so will help establish where automation is justified, where human observation remains essential, and what language-based models can reveal about human psychology and behavior.
