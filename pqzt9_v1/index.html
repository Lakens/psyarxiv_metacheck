Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard), also Internal Review Board - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term); not IEC followed by a number, which is a technical standard - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee/Commission, in the singular or plural - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee, Bioethics Committee/Commission, Ethikkommission - Ethical Review Authority/Unit/Office/Agency - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Human Research Protection Office/Program, Office for Human Subjects Protection - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee(s) on/for (Medical and Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt - Ethics review that was not needed: Ethics review was not required, did not require further ethical review, exempt from formal human research ethics review, no new institutional ethics review

A template line the authors have not filled in (“[Add ethics approval … here.]”) is not a statement.

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

When a statement is expected

The module sorts each paper by where its data came from (the internal .data_source(); the signs and how the rule was tested are described in R/data-source.R): - own: the authors collected data from people or animals, for this paper, in an earlier study of their own, or (a protocol) will collect them; - existing_records: records about individual people that the authors obtained for this study, such as hospital records or a cohort; - existing_unclear: existing data about people from a source the module could not identify; - open_resource: an open resource any researcher can use, such as UK Biobank or NHANES; - none: no data about people or animals (a review, simulation, commentary, theory, tutorial, coding of published papers, or figures about groups); - uncertain: the signs were missing or pointed both ways; - no_text: the paper has no text.

A statement is expected for own, existing_records and existing_unclear. A paper without a statement in these classes, or in uncertain, is flagged. The module reads the text, and the results of power, stat_p_exact, stat_check, repo_check, data_check and prereg_check when they ran before it (run it after them); without stat_p_exact, it finds the p-values in the text.

On 199 PsyArXiv and medRxiv preprints labelled after the rule was fixed, the module decided right whether a statement is needed for 86.9% (the earlier live-data search: 83.4%). Its main errors: other people’s studies described in the words of a new study, records and specimens described only as counts, and open resources the paper does not call open.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 685 tests with 34 instances of inconsistent reporting, Statcheck correctly detected 34 of them, and incorrectly identified 26. Therefore 0% of true instances were missed, and 43% of detections were false positives. See https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/ for more details of the validation.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on 226 Psychological Science papers with 5,019 hand-coded p-values, 438 of them imprecise (scienceverse/validation, branch stat_p_exact). Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). Of the misses, 13 are starred table notes (“*p < .05”) that the module leaves out on purpose and 11 are p-values it did not find; of the wrong reports, 20 are p-values the ground truth does not contain (such as “ps < .05”) and 6 use ≤ or ≥. These figures measure agreement with the hand-coding, which has known errors in both directions (scienceverse/validation#48).

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on three hand-coded sets of papers (scienceverse/validation: 249 Psychological Science, 250 Economics and 250 MDPI papers). Of 1,783 non-significant p-values in the ground truth, version 0.4.4.3 found 1,719 (3.6% missed), and 94.8% of its 1,814 detections were in the ground truth. Some differences come from the ground truth: it lacks p-values with a correction name (“p Bonf = .061”), and counts partial eta squared written with its subscript after it (“η 2 p = .05”), “p = .05”, and some table notes and figure legends (“† p < .10, *p < .05”), which this module leaves out, as non-significant p-values. How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. Abstract

Parkinson's disease (PD) is characterized by nigrostriatal dopaminergic degeneration and basal ganglia dysfunction, yet these pathophysiological abnormalities alone do not fully explain its heterogeneous motor and non-motor manifestations. Over recent decades, electroencephalography (EEG) has evolved from a primarily descriptive technique into a quantitative approach for investigating cortical electrophysiological dynamics associated with PD. Accumulating electrophysiological evidence implicates altered oscillatory synchronization and interactions across cortical and subcortical circuits in PD-related dysfunction, suggesting disturbances in the temporal coordination of neural activity. Emerging approaches, including large-scale datasets, multimodal integration, individualized modeling and wearable EEG technologies, offer opportunities for physiological phenotyping and real-world monitoring. However, methodological heterogeneity, limited reproducibility, insufficient external validation and uncertainty regarding clinical utility continue to constrain translation. This Review critically synthesizes EEG-derived measures spanning spectral activity, oscillatory coupling, functional connectivity and dynamic brain states, examining their relationships with motor, cognitive and neuropsychiatric manifestations of PD. By distinguishing mechanistic interpretations, clinical associations and current levels of biomarker validation, it identifies opportunities and requirements for translating EEG-derived measures into reliable tools for individualized assessment and clinical decision-making.

1. Introduction

Parkinson's disease (PD) is among the fastest growing neurological disorders worldwide, with its prevalence projected to rise substantially over the coming decades as populations age and survival improves [1]. Global modeling studies project that the number of people living with PD could approach 25 million by 2050, reflecting population aging and other demographic and epidemiological changes [2] [3] [4]. Although historically defined by its cardinal motor manifestations, PD is now recognized as a clinically heterogeneous multisystem disorder in which cognitive impairment, neuropsychiatric symptoms, autonomic dysfunction and sleep disturbances contribute substantially to long-term disability and reduced quality of life [5] [6] [7] [8]. Longitudinal studies have also demonstrated substantial variability in disease trajectories, particularly with respect to cognitive decline and dementia risk [9] [10] [11]. This heterogeneity highlights the limitations of explanations centered exclusively on nigrostriatal dopaminergic degeneration and underscores the involvement of distributed neural circuits and multiple neurotransmitter systems [12].

This evolving view of PD has been accompanied by a transformation in the role of electroencephalography (EEG). Initially used primarily for descriptive assessment of cortical electrical activity, EEG has increasingly been applied to the investigation of large-scale neural dynamics through its millisecond temporal resolution. Advances in high-density recordings, source reconstruction, connectivity analysis and computational modeling have expanded the investigation of oscillatory interactions, large-scale functional organization and interindividual electrophysiological variability [13]. These developments have strengthened the use of EEG for investigating PD-related physiological abnormalities and exploring candidate biomarkers associated with clinical manifestations and treatment states [14, 15].

Classical pathophysiological models of PD emphasized dopaminergic degeneration within the nigrostriatal pathway and its consequences for neuronal firing within the cortico-basal ganglia-thalamo-cortical circuit [16] [17] [18]. Although these models provided an influential framework for understanding motor dysfunction, they offered a limited account of the broader clinical heterogeneity of PD, including cognitive and neuropsychiatric manifestations [19]. Growing evidence from electrophysiology, neuroimaging and neuropathology has increasingly supported a distributed circuit perspective, in which pathological brain states reflect altered interactions across cortical and subcortical structures rather than dysfunction confined to individual nuclei [20] [21] [22].

Central to this conceptual shift has been the recognition that communication across brain circuits is organized through rhythmic neural activity [23, 24]. Oscillatory synchronization coordinates information exchange across cortical and subcortical structures during motor, cognitive and affective processing [25] [26] [27]. In PD, dopamine depletion contributes to disturbances in oscillatory coordination, including excessive beta-band synchronization, changes in cross-frequency interactions and impaired modulation of corticosubcortical activity across behavioral contexts [28] [29] [30] [31]. EEG studies have characterized cortical oscillatory abnormalities associated with these broader circuit disturbances [32, 33]. Spectral slowing and related resting-state abnormalities have been associated with cognitive dysfunction, with longitudinal studies suggesting associations with subsequent cognitive decline [34] [35] [36]. Oscillatory coupling, functional connectivity and dynamic brain-state measures have additionally been linked to motor severity, cognitive phenotype and treatment response [32, 33, 37]. This Review integrates mechanistic, biomarker and translational perspectives to critically examine how EEG-derived measures of cortical activity may inform the understanding and clinical characterization of PD. Particular emphasis is placed on their complementary physiological significance, the maturity of supporting evidence and the requirements for translation into clinically informative measures. We first examine how EEG findings, interpreted alongside broader electrophysiological evidence, inform current understanding of oscillatory cortico-subcortical circuit dysfunction and its relationship to motor, cognitive and neuropsychiatric manifestations. We then evaluate candidate EEG biomarkers associated with cognitive impairment, disease progression, treatment-related motor states and clinical heterogeneity. Finally, we discuss methodological challenges and emerging translational opportunities, including multimodal integration and real-world monitoring, that may support individualized electrophysiological profiling and the development of clinically useful EEG-based approaches.

2. Evolution of EEG in Parkinson's disease

>Evolution of EEG in Parkinson's disease

2.1 Historical EEG findings

EEG has been used to investigate PD for more than half a century, although its role has changed substantially over time. Early studies conducted during the 1940s-1950s primarily relied on visual inspection of routine scalp EEG recordings and generally reported normal or only mildly abnormal background activity in non-demented patients [14, 38, 39]. EEG was regarded primarily as a nonspecific clinical investigation, used mainly to assess alternative neurological conditions rather than characterize PDrelated pathophysiology [40]. This limited emphasis on cortical electrophysiology was consistent with the predominantly nigrostriatal and motor-centered conceptualization of PD that shaped much of its subsequent pathophysiological research [41, 42].

During the late 1980s and early 1990s, increasing attention was directed toward the relationship between EEG abnormalities and cognitive dysfunction in PD. Comparative studies demonstrated that PD patients with dementia exhibited more pronounced background slowing than cognitively preserved individuals, while quantitative spectral analyses revealed increased theta and delta activity together with reduced alpha activity and slowing of dominant posterior frequencies [43, 44]. These findings established cortical slowing as an important electrophysiological correlate of cognitive dysfunction in PD, providing early evidence of disease-related cortical involvement and clinical heterogeneity [45].

In parallel, the application of event-related potentials (ERPs) to PD expanded electrophysiological investigations beyond resting-state activity to cognitive and motor processing. Studies examining P300, contingent negative variation (CNV) and related ERP components demonstrated abnormalities in attentional allocation, information processing and motor preparation, particularly among patients with cognitive impairment [46, 47]. Together, resting-state EEG and ERP studies suggested that cognitive dysfunction in PD is associated with abnormalities in both ongoing oscillatory activity and stimulus-evoked neural responses [48]. These developments established EEG as a tool capable of probing specific neural processes rather than merely detecting generalized slowing.

The broader adoption of quantitative EEG (qEEG) transformed the field by enabling systematic measurement of spectral features, while subsequent methodological advances extended analyses to connectivity and network-level metrics. During the late 2000s and early 2010s, qEEG studies characterized associations between cortical slowing and cognitive impairment across different stages of PD, while also revealing spectral differences between Parkinson's disease dementia and other neurodegenerative dementias [49] [50] [51] [52]. Longitudinal investigations further identified associations between baseline qEEG measures and subsequent cognitive decline or progression toward Parkinson's disease dementia [36, 53].

More recently, advances in quantitative feature extraction and machine-learning methods have extended EEG research from descriptive electrophysiology toward multidimensional characterization of diseaserelated cortical dysfunction [50, [54] [55] [56].

This historical progression parallels a broader shift in PD research from predominantly dopaminergic explanations toward models emphasizing dysfunction across distributed neural circuits [11] (Fig. 1). Within this framework, EEG offers a noninvasive means of characterizing cortical electrophysiological dynamics associated with PD, providing a basis for the mechanistic perspectives discussed in the following sections. Early observations of largely nonspecific EEG findings were followed by evidence linking cortical slowing to cognitive impairment. Advances in oscillatory cortico-basal ganglia models, exemplified by Brown (2003)*, provided a broader framework for interpreting cortical electrophysiological abnormalities. Recent multidimensional and multimodal approaches extend this framework toward individualized electrophysiological profiling and clinical translation.

2.2 EEG correlates of cognitive dysfunction and disease progression

Cortical slowing is among the most consistently reported EEG abnormalities in PD, particularly in association with cognitive impairment and subsequent cognitive decline. qEEG studies demonstrate a shift toward lower-frequency cortical activity, characterized by increased theta-and delta-band power, reductions in faster alpha and beta rhythms, and changes in dominant frequency across multiple scalp regions [57, 58]. Analyses of regional power distributions and frequency-specific dynamics additionally reveal abnormalities in theta-alpha balance and reductions in dominant occipital frequency, indicating broader alterations in cortical oscillatory activity [34, 53]. These spectral abnormalities may reflect disturbances in distributed cortical and cortico-subcortical activity, potentially associated with dopaminergic and cholinergic dysfunction [59, 60].

The clinical relevance of spectral abnormalities is particularly evident in their association with cognitive status and longitudinal progression. Increased slow-frequency activity, especially over posterior regions, has been observed across the spectrum from cognitively preserved PD to mild cognitive impairment and dementia [58, 61]. Longitudinal studies further suggest that slowing of posterior dominant rhythms and increased low-frequency activity may precede cognitive decline, supporting their potential prognostic relevance [62, 63]. Electrophysiological trajectories may also differ across PD phenotypes, reflecting heterogeneity in disease expression and progression [64].

Event-related EEG paradigms provide a complementary perspective by revealing abnormalities in neural responses during cognitive processing. Attenuation of delta-band responses during cognitive tasks has been associated with cognitive impairment in PD, suggesting disrupted recruitment of neural systems involved in information processing and cognitive control [65, 66].

At the network level, functional connectivity and graph-theoretical analyses reveal reduced segregation and altered integration across cortical systems, particularly within alpha-band networks [67] [68] [69] [70]. Dynamic connectivity analyses and hidden-state modeling further suggest abnormalities in the temporal organization of large-scale cortical activity, including altered transitions between recurring brain states [71] [72] [73]. EEG microstate studies similarly identify changes in microstate occurrence and transition probabilities in cognitively impaired individuals, with partial normalization following dopaminergic therapy suggesting sensitivity to physiological modulation [74, 75]. Connectivity and dynamic-state measures thus extend the electrophysiological characterization of cognitive impairment from regional oscillatory abnormalities to distributed network dynamics.

Beyond spectral measures, longitudinal studies suggest that EEG-derived network abnormalities may also evolve alongside clinical progression. Source-level alpha-band connectivity has been reported to decline with worsening clinical status and increasing dopaminergic treatment requirements [76]. Recent clustering approaches have also identified electrophysiological subgroups associated with distinct cognitive trajectories and rates of motor progression, suggesting that multidimensional EEG patterns may capture clinically meaningful aspects of disease heterogeneity [77].

Collectively, EEG studies identify complementary electrophysiological correlates of cognitive dysfunction in PD, including spectral slowing, altered task-related responses and network-level abnormalities. Evidence is most consistent for associations between cortical slowing and cognitive impairment, with longitudinal findings suggesting potential prognostic relevance. The reproducibility, incremental predictive value and clinical utility of connectivity, microstate and dynamic-state measures remain less well established. 3. EEG insights into oscillatory circuit mechanisms

>EEG insights into oscillatory circuit mechanisms

3.1 From firing-rate models to oscillatory circuit dysfunction

Classical firing-rate models of PD pathophysiology emphasized the consequences of dopaminergic degeneration for neuronal firing rates within basal ganglia circuits [17, 18]. Although these models provided a foundational explanation for motor dysfunction, they could not fully account for the complexity of parkinsonian circuit activity. Experimental and human electrophysiological studies subsequently demonstrated that dopamine depletion is associated not only with altered firing rates but also with pathological bursting, oscillatory synchronization and abnormal coupling across interconnected basal ganglia, thalamic and cortical structures [20, 78].

Invasive recordings from the subthalamic nucleus, globus pallidus internus and motor thalamus revealed abnormal rhythmic activity across multiple nodes of the cortico-basal ganglia-thalamo-cortical circuit [79]. Such observations shifted attention from changes in average neuronal firing rates toward the temporal coordination of activity across interconnected cortico-basal ganglia circuits [80, 81]. Within this framework, pathological synchronization has been proposed as a contributor to impaired circuit communication rather than solely an accompanying feature of the parkinsonian state [82].

These insights into distributed circuit physiology also provide a basis for interpreting cortical EEG activity in PD. Although scalp EEG does not directly record basal ganglia activity, cortical oscillations are influenced by interactions among cortical, thalamic and basal ganglia circuits, offering an indirect perspective on distributed circuit dysfunction [83, 84]. Resting-state EEG studies have identified abnormalities in cortical oscillatory activity, including increased slow-frequency power and reductions in faster rhythms [57, 58]. Subcortical electrophysiological recordings provide complementary evidence of pathological rhythmic activity within the basal ganglia, although these findings do not establish a direct correspondence with specific scalp EEG signatures [85]. Contemporary EEG analyses extend beyond conventional spectral power to examine functional connectivity, cross-frequency coupling, graphtheoretical metrics, microstates and dynamic brain-state organization [67, 71, 86].

Excessive beta-band synchronization (approximately 13-30 Hz) has emerged as a prominent physiological correlate of parkinsonian motor dysfunction. Such synchronization has been proposed to stabilize pathological circuit states and constrain the flexibility required for movement [87] [88] [89] [90] [91] [92]. Basal ganglia recordings demonstrate enhanced beta activity in the dopamine-depleted (OFF-medication) state and its modulation by dopaminergic therapy and deep brain stimulation, linking these oscillations to the functional state of motor circuits [93] [94] [95] [96]. Associations between beta activity and motor symptom severity have also been reported, although their strength varies across clinical and recording contexts [97] [98] [99]. These properties have motivated the investigation of beta synchronization as a candidate physiological biomarker and a target for adaptive neuromodulation [100, 101].

The functional significance of beta synchronization becomes particularly apparent during movement.

Movement preparation and execution are normally accompanied by transient beta desynchronization, a pattern thought to support flexible transitions between motor states [30, 100]. In PD, movement-related beta suppression is often attenuated, while persistent synchronization has been associated with impaired movement initiation, bradykinesia and rigidity [102] [103] [104]. Simultaneous cortical and subcortical recordings reveal coherent beta interactions across motor pathways, consistent with abnormal temporal coordination at the circuit level rather than dysfunction confined to individual nuclei [91, 105].

Together, these findings illustrate how oscillatory models extend classical firing-rate explanations by emphasizing the temporal organization of neuronal activity across distributed circuits. This framework provides a mechanistic context for interpreting EEG abnormalities as potential cortical manifestations of altered cortico-subcortical coordination, while recognizing that scalp EEG does not directly resolve their subcortical generators. 3.2 Cross-frequency interactions and circuit coordination

Although excessive beta-band synchronization is a prominent physiological feature of parkinsonian motor dysfunction, pathological oscillatory activity in PD extends beyond abnormalities within a single frequency band. Evidence increasingly implicates disturbances in interactions among beta, gamma, theta and alpha rhythms in the physiological organization of cortico-subcortical circuits [106, 107]. These rhythms contribute to different aspects of motor and cognitive processing, including local neuronal coordination, movement-related activity and distributed cognitive control [108].

One proposed mechanism for coordinating activity across oscillatory frequencies is cross-frequency coupling, particularly phase-amplitude coupling (PAC), in which the amplitude of higher-frequency activity varies systematically with the phase of a lower-frequency oscillation [109, 110]. Such interactions may support the coordination of neuronal activity across temporal scales, potentially linking local processing with communication across distributed circuits. In PD, elevated beta-gamma PAC has been reported in cortical and subcortical recordings, suggesting that abnormal interactions between oscillatory frequencies may contribute to altered circuit dynamics [111].

Movement-related EEG studies have shown that sensorimotor beta-gamma PAC varies with gait state and freezing-of-gait severity, suggesting that abnormal cross-frequency interactions accompany impaired locomotor control [112]. PAC increases in the OFF-medication state and is modulated by levodopa and locomotor state, indicating sensitivity to treatment-related and behavioral changes in oscillatory dynamics [113, 114]. These findings suggest that altered cross-frequency interactions may reflect changes in motorstate-dependent circuit coordination [115].

Cross-frequency abnormalities may also reflect broader changes in cortical and subcortical oscillatory organization. EEG connectivity analyses and simultaneous electrophysiological recordings reveal altered patterns of oscillatory coupling across distributed systems [83, 116]. Interactions involving motor, premotor, prefrontal and associative cortical regions may link abnormalities in local oscillatory dynamics to the distributed physiological processes supporting movement and cognition [117].

Collectively, these findings extend oscillatory models of PD beyond individual frequency bands, highlighting altered interactions across temporal scales and distributed circuits. This perspective supports the investigation of EEG-derived coupling measures as potential indicators of physiological state and clinical heterogeneity, although their mechanistic specificity and clinical utility require further validation [118].

4. Translational applications of EEG in precision neurology

>Translational applications of EEG in precision neurology

4.1 Biomarkers of treatment-related motor states

Motor symptoms and associated circuit activity fluctuate in response to dopaminergic therapy and neuromodulatory interventions, creating a need for objective physiological measures capable of tracking these changes. The sensitivity of cortical oscillatory activity to these interventions makes EEG a potential tool for monitoring changes in motor circuit physiology [119, 120]. Resting-state and task-based EEG studies have identified sensorimotor features that differ between ON-and OFF-medication states, suggesting sensitivity to treatment-related changes in motor function (Fig. 3) [68, 121]. Resting-state analyses further show that sensorimotor alpha-band nonlinearity distinguishes medication states, with effects localized primarily over bilateral sensorimotor regions [122]. Additional nonlinear and directedconnectivity measures have been explored for characterizing disease-and treatment-related electrophysiological states [122, 123]. Task-based EEG characterizes electrophysiological abnormalities during motor performance. During finger-tapping and related motor tasks, individuals with PD show altered coherence and reduced interhemispheric differentiation relative to healthy controls, consistent with abnormal motor-related synchronization [68]. Directed-connectivity analyses identify EEG-derived measures that distinguish PD from healthy controls and are associated with tremor severity, bradykinesia and motor performance [85, [124] [125] [126].

At the network level, high-density EEG and connectivity-based studies have identified alterations in cortical synchronization associated with motor dysfunction. Increased synchronization strength has been associated with freezing-of-gait severity, while altered phase-locking patterns suggest changes in coordination across distributed cortical systems [127] [128] [129].

Combined EEG-LFP recordings provide convergent evidence that therapeutic subthalamic stimulation suppresses pathological high-beta synchronization within hyperdirect cortico-basal ganglia pathways and modulates cortico-cortical high-gamma interactions [126, 130, 131].

Beyond conventional spectral and connectivity measures, beta synchronization, transient beta dynamics, PAC and waveform morphology have been investigated as complementary indicators of treatment-related physiological changes [132] [133] [134]. During voluntary movement, individuals with PD exhibit differences in cortical synchronization, cortico-muscular coupling and beta-gamma PAC, involving premotor, sensorimotor and prefrontal regions [132, 135, 136]. Partial normalization of these features following dopaminergic therapy suggests sensitivity to changes in motor circuit physiology [85, 137].

4.2 From electrophysiological phenotyping to precision neurology

The marked clinical heterogeneity of PD has shifted attention from population-level biomarkers toward individualized characterization of disease-related physiological abnormalities [138]. Within this framework, EEG offers a scalable approach to profiling individual patterns of cortical electrophysiological activity that may reflect dysfunction within distributed cortico-subcortical circuits.

Data-driven analyses suggest that multidimensional EEG features capture clinically relevant interindividual variation in PD [76, 139]. Rather than relying on single markers, these approaches integrate multiple EEG features to characterize patient-specific electrophysiological profiles potentially associated with motor, cognitive and neuropsychiatric heterogeneity. Electrophysiological phenotyping therefore extends the objective of EEG analysis beyond diagnostic discrimination toward individualized characterization of disease expression, with potential applications in patient stratification.

The overlapping nature of PD manifestations also highlights the limitations of conventional subgrouping approaches, which assume that patients can be assigned to a finite number of discrete categories. Normative modeling addresses this limitation by quantifying individualized deviations from expected electrophysiological patterns, thereby moving beyond conventional case-control frameworks and accommodating the continuous and multidimensional nature of disease expression [140] [141] [142]. This shift away from one-size-fits-all classification is particularly relevant in PD, where motor and non-motor manifestations rarely conform to clear biological boundaries [139, 143, 144]. Linking patient-specific electrophysiological deviations to clinical trajectories may ultimately improve patient stratification, disease monitoring and treatment selection. Machine-learning approaches provide another means of integrating multidimensional EEG information for classification and prediction. Studies using spectral, time-frequency and microstate-derived representations have demonstrated the feasibility of distinguishing PD from healthy individuals [86, [145] [146] [147], while emerging approaches have also investigated EEG-based characterization of cognitive status and disease heterogeneity [148]. Integration with neuroimaging, genetic and clinical data may further strengthen biological interpretation and enhance the clinical utility of EEG-derived profiles [140, 149, 150]. However, diagnostic classification and cross-sectional cognitive-status discrimination should not be equated with prospective prediction of cognitive decline or treatment response, which requires longitudinal validation.

The robustness and clinical generalizability of computational approaches also remain to be established across heterogeneous cohorts, recording protocols and analytical pipelines.

Together, electrophysiological phenotyping, normative modeling and machine learning outline a potential transition from population-level EEG associations toward individualized physiological characterization.

Their clinical value will depend on whether patient-specific EEG profiles provide reproducible information beyond conventional clinical assessments and meaningfully inform prognosis, monitoring or treatment decisions. 5. Challenges and future directions

>Challenges and future directions

5.1 Methodological and translational challenges

Despite substantial advances in understanding oscillatory circuit dysfunction in PD, the clinical translation of EEG-derived measures remains limited by uncertainty regarding their reproducibility, physiological specificity and clinical relevance across diverse settings. Differences in acquisition protocols, recording conditions and preprocessing pipelines further complicate cross-study comparisons and biomarker standardization [151] [152] [153].

Clinical heterogeneity further complicates biomarker interpretation, as disease duration, medication status, cognitive impairment and neuropsychiatric symptoms can influence cortical oscillatory activity.

Dopaminergic therapy, in particular, modulates oscillatory dynamics, complicating the distinction between relatively stable disease-related abnormalities and transient treatment-dependent physiological states [154, 155]. Careful phenotypic characterization and standardized assessment of behavioral and medication states are therefore essential for interpreting electrophysiological findings consistently across studies.

Analytical diversity also complicates biomarker validation. Contemporary EEG studies employ spectral, connectivity-based, nonlinear and machine-learning methods [156] [157] [158]. Although these approaches capture different aspects of electrophysiological activity, limited agreement on feature selection, analytical procedures and validation standards hinders comparison across studies [159, 160]. Future efforts should therefore prioritize physiological interpretability, reproducibility and external validation rather than incremental improvements in classification performance alone.

Clinical translation also depends on establishing robust links between cortical EEG measurements and the underlying circuit mechanisms they are intended to represent. Although EEG provides excellent temporal resolution and may reflect cortical manifestations of altered cortico-basal ganglia activity, scalp recordings do not directly measure deep brain structures or uniquely identify the anatomical generators of observed oscillations. Source reconstruction and multimodal integration with structural and functional neuroimaging can strengthen these physiological inferences, although these approaches introduce modeling assumptions that require careful validation [161, 162].

Ultimately, clinical translation will require EEG-derived measures that remain reproducible across centers and demonstrate reliable associations with clinically relevant states or longitudinal outcomes. Progress will depend on multicenter and longitudinal cohorts, harmonized acquisition protocols, transparent analytical workflows, normative reference datasets, data-sharing initiatives and rigorous external validation [163] [164] [165] [166] [167] [168] [169] [170]. Clinical implementation will additionally require evidence that EEG-derived measures provide actionable information beyond established clinical assessments.

5.2 Toward clinical implementation

The clinical potential of EEG may be strengthened by integrating electrophysiological measures with complementary biological and clinical information. Combining EEG with structural and functional neuroimaging, genetic information, motor and non-motor clinical assessments, digital behavioral measures and other physiological biomarkers may provide a more comprehensive characterization of PD heterogeneity than any modality alone [140, 171, 172]. Beyond multimodal characterization, EEG may also be investigated for assessing physiological changes following interventions such as MR-guided focused ultrasound and other lesion-based therapies. Establishing whether these interventions produce reproducible EEG alterations could help clarify their effects on circuit organization and support future assessment of therapeutic response [173].

Advances in wearable neurotechnology may expand the applications of EEG by facilitating prolonged electrophysiological monitoring beyond the laboratory environment. Portable EEG systems capable of recording neural activity during everyday behavior may capture fluctuations in motor and cognitive states that are insufficiently represented by conventional clinic-based assessments [174]. Such developments could help characterize within-patient variability and relate electrophysiological fluctuations to symptoms and treatment timing under real-world conditions, potentially supporting the integration of EEG into routine clinical practice. Through multimodal integration and real-world monitoring, EEG has the potential to bridge mechanistic neuroscience and clinically actionable precision neurology in Parkinson's disease.

6. Conclusion

EEG has evolved from a descriptive neurophysiological technique into a mechanistically informative platform for investigating PD. By measuring cortical oscillatory activity that could reflect dysfunction across distributed cortico-basal ganglia circuits, EEG provides physiological measures associated with motor impairment, cognitive dysfunction and therapeutic modulation. Together, mechanistic insights and methodological advances have expanded the scope of EEG research from identifying disease-associated abnormalities toward investigating individualized physiological profiles and treatment-sensitive states.

The future value of EEG therefore lies not in identifying a universal biomarker, but in characterizing the dynamic and heterogeneous electrophysiological manifestations of PD-related circuit dysfunction.

Advances in computational analysis and longitudinal, multimodal investigation may help establish whether these measures can inform prognosis, treatment-response assessment and individualized clinical decisions.

By linking cortical electrophysiological dynamics to clinically relevant disease states, EEG may contribute to a more individualized and physiologically informed approach to Parkinson's disease.
