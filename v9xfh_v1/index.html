We left out 4 files that software creates by itself, not the authors: operating system (4). They hold nothing a reader can use, but they clutter the repository, and software can mistake them for real files (for example a ._ copy of a .csv for data).

Remove these: Operating system files: macOS adds a __MACOSX folder and a ._ copy of every file it puts in a zip, and .DS_Store files to folders; Windows adds Thumbs.db and desktop.ini. On a Mac, create zips from the command line with zip -r -X, or remove them afterwards with zip -d archive.zip "__MACOSX/*" "*.DS_Store". RStudio session files (.Rproj.user/) hold open tabs and unsaved edits; R history (.Rhistory, .Rapp.history) lists the commands typed in a session and can hold file paths and passwords; Jupyter checkpoints (.ipynb_checkpoints/) are automatic copies of notebooks; compiled Python files (__pycache__/, .pyc) are recreated when the code runs; open-document lock files (~$...docx, .~lock...#) are left while a document is open; editor backups (file~, .bak, .swp) and temporary files (.tmp, .temp, an interrupted save of R’s workspace, .RDataTmp) are copies a program made by itself.

Keep only on purpose: version control folders (.git, .svn, .hg) hold the project’s history, which can include earlier versions of files and data that was later removed; editor settings (.idea, .vscode) can hold paths on your own computer. A .gitignore based on GitHub’s templates (https://github.com/github/gitignore) keeps them out of a repository.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

run_results$checked says what the run of each script shows about the code, in five groups, so that a script that does not reproduce is kept apart from one the check could not test: ran (it ran to the end); code_error (it did not reproduce: it stopped with an error of the code or the deposit, could not be read, or needs a file the deposit does not hold); dependency (a package or program was not available: a package that could not be installed or was not loaded, a program outside R and Python, a system library, or R crashing in a package’s compiled code); time (it did not finish within the time limit for one script, or was not started because the time for all scripts had run out); and not_checkable (it could not be checked automatically: it needs a person, the internet, a key or another setting of the authors’ computer, one platform such as Google Colab, Windows or OpenSAFELY, more memory than the run allows, a file that was not downloaded, a screen, or a program that runs it, or it is a file of a package of the code that Python runs only as a module of that package). It is NA for files run inside another script, files that are not an analysis, files not read, and a Docker failure. summary_table counts each group (repro_ran_n, repro_code_error_n, repro_dependency_n, repro_time_n, repro_not_checkable_n), and the report’s first sentence gives them. The traffic light reflects the static readiness signals by default; a script in code_error that stopped with an error makes it red, as a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure, and a script in one of the other three groups keeps it from being green. Calls that need a screen, which the run does not have (dev.print(), dev.copy2pdf(), dev.copy2eps(), savePlot(), windows(), win.graph(), x11(), quartz(), View()), are replaced before the run by calls that need none, which modifications records as screen_call_replaced. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Full text

Abstract

Statistical learning enables the extraction of structure from the environment, including word units from continuous speech. Yet inter-individual variability suggests contributions from other, potentially broader domain-general mechanisms. Here, we investigated whether individual differences in music rhythm processing are associated with the dynamics of statistical learning during speech segmentation, potentially reflecting shared sensitivity to temporal regularities across music and language. Adults were exposed to structured and random artificial speech streams while neural tracking at syllable and word rate frequencies was tracked over time using EEG to characterize the emergence of word representations. In addition, participants' abilities to process rhythm in musical materials were assessed using behavioral and neural (EEG) measures. Neural measurements revealed robust tracking at the syllabic level in both structured and random streams, whereas word-level tracking emerged over exposure only in the structured stream condition. Importantly, individual differences in neural synchronization to musical rhythms were associated with the temporal dynamics of word extraction. These results suggest that individual differences in the ability to process temporal regularities in musical material are related to the efficiency with which statistical regularities are extracted from sequential language material. More broadly, the findings provide evidence for domain-general mechanisms supporting sensitivity to temporal structure regularities across auditory domains.

1.1 Introduction

Understanding how humans make sense of information unfolding over time is a central question in cognitive science. Both speech and music processing require the extraction of structure from continuous temporal sequences, allowing listeners to organize incoming information and generate predictions about future events. Although temporal sequence extraction mechanisms are often studied separately in speech and music, increasing evidence suggests possible overlap between them as both rely on the perceivers' sensitivity to sequential structures unfolding over time. Sequencing abilities, the capacity to perceive, represent and predict events as well as execute actions in a particular order, are fundamental to human cognition (Janata & Grafton, 2003; Savalia et al., 2016; Tillmann, 2012; Zatorre et al., 2007), and are suggested to be a core component of language processing (Conway et al., 2009; Conway & Pisoni, 2008; Daltrozzo et al., 2019; Tillmann, 2012). Central to sequencing is the ability to generate predictions about future elements both in terms of "what" will occur (the identity of sequence elements, such as words or musical units) and "when" they will occur (the timing of these elements; Jones & Boltz, 1989; Molinari & Masciullo, 2019). While predictions about identity appear domainspecific (e.g., Patel, 2003), temporal predictions may rely on domain-general neural and cognitive mechanisms (e.g., Cona & Semenza, 2017; Harding et al., 2025; Large & Jones, 1999), with rhythm providing a key framework for understanding temporal mechanisms across domains.

Rhythm processing relies on the extraction of temporal regularities and the ability to anticipate events at expected moments in time. Both music and language are characterized by hierarchically organized temporal patterns, such as beats and meters in music, and syllabic, prosodic, and phrasal rhythms in speech (Fiveash et al., 2021; Fiveash & Tillmann, 2024; Goswami, 2011; Poeppel & Assaneo, 2020; Tillmann, 2012). These temporal regularities facilitate prediction and enhance the processing of events occurring at expected time points (e.g., Large & Jones, 1999; Nobre & Van Ede, 2018). The present study aimed to investigate whether listeners' rhythmic processing abilities in musical material contribute to statistical learning (SL) in speech segmentation. SL refers to the extraction of probabilistic regularities (or units emerging thereof) from continuous sequences. Using artificial languages concatenating artificial word units without surface cues provides a powerful paradigm for investigating sequencing mechanisms (Frost et al., 2019). In auditory SL tasks, participants are exposed to structured streams of spoken syllables (or other materials, such as tones, or timbres), with high transitional probabilities between the events within structured units ("words") and low transitional probabilities occurring between units and marking word boundaries (Hoch et al., 2013; Perruchet & Pacton, 2006; Saffran et al., 1996 Saffran et al., , 1999;; Tillmann & McAdams, 2004). EEG studies have revealed that structured streams typically elicit neural tracking at both syllable and word frequencies, with the emergence of neural tracking at the word rate reflecting word learning; whereas random streams evoke only syllable-frequency responses, reflecting the absence of word chunking (Batterink & Paller, 2017; Buiatti et al., 2009; Kudo et al., 2011).

Although SL and rhythm processing have often been studied separately, increasing evidence suggests they may interact as both involve processing of structures that unfold over time. For instance, in a speech segmentation context, adults who spontaneously synchronized their speech production to the rhythm of an external syllable stream showed stronger neural entrainment and superior performance in speech segmentation tasks than did adults who did not spontaneously synchronize their speech production to the rhythm of a spoken syllable stream (Assaneo et al., 2019). More recently, Van Der Wulp et al. (2025a) examined the relationship between SL (with speech material) and rhythmic, musical and cognitive abilities in adults, as measured by EEG and behavior, respectively. Although their preregistered analyses did not reveal significant associations between neural tracking of the speech stream and rhythmic behavioral performance, exploratory analyses suggested moderate relationships. The authors interpreted their lack of strong associations as potentially reflecting limitations in cross-method sensitivity, particularly when correlating behavioral and neural measures to capture inter-individual differences. Supporting this interpretation, a developmental study by the same group (van der Wulp, 2025b) reported associations in neural measures between SL of speech and neural tracking of rhythm in infants.

However, the question remains whether this relationship is evident only early in development, or is also present in adults. Therefore, our study investigated the association between statistical learning and music rhythm processing abilities in adults using EEG measures for both SL and rhythm processing.

In both music and language, temporal regularities have been shown to facilitate not only perception, but also learning and memory (e.g., Falk & Dalla Bella, 2016; Fiveash & Tillmann, 2024; Plancher et al., 2018). In particular, research investigating SL suggests that the temporal structure of the to-be-learned regularities matters, and that temporally structured input can enhance learning, such as when words of the same length are grouped rhythmically, or when artificial grammars are presented with regular metrical rhythms rather than variable timing (Hoch et al., 2013; Selchenkova, et al., 2014a; Selchenkova, et al., 2014b). Here, we examine SL of "what" (i.e., the speech stream structure) while investigating whether individual differences in rhythmic abilities (i.e., the capacity to predict "when") influence the learning of this structure (what). This approach goes beyond studying the influence of temporal structures that are implemented in the stimulus and investigates the influence of perceivers' temporal processing abilities on statistical learning abilities.

A further motivation for the present study comes from research showing that rhythmic abilities correlate with language proficiency (e.g., Ladanyi et al., 2023; Gordon et al., 2015; Kalashnikova et al., 2019; Meng et al., 2022; Nayak et al., 2022). Rhythm perception and production skills are associated with a wide range of language-related abilities, such as phonological processing (Ozernov-Palchik et al., 2018; Politimou et al., 2018), grammar processing (Gordon et al., 2015; Swaminathan & Schellenberg, 2020), and reading and spelling abilities (Flaugnacco et al., 2015; Kertész & Honbolygó, 2023). Together with other theoretical frameworks, these findings led to the processing of rhythm in speech and music (PRISM) framework (Fiveash et al., 2021), which posits that rhythm processing in speech and music rely on shared mechanisms, such as precise auditory analysis, synchronization or entrainment of neural oscillations to external stimuli, and sensorimotor coupling (Fiveash et al., 2021; Fiveash & Tillmann, 2024).

Rhythm and sequence-processing abilities are also relevant to language impairments. Individuals with dyslexia and other language-related disorders have been reported to show difficulties with sequential and statistical learning as well as with rhythm processing, including reduced sensitivity to temporal regularities and atypical neural entrainment to speech rhythms (Evans et al., 2009; Howard et al., 2006; Ladányi et al., 2020; Lense et al., 2021; Obeid et al., 2016; Ozernov-Palchik et al., 2023; Ringer et al., 2025; Saffran, 2018; Singh & Conway, 2026). This convergence suggests that temporal processing abilities may contribute to extracting structure from continuous auditory input: reduced sensitivity to temporal regularities could, in principle, make it harder to organize and segment sequential information. Thus, examining whether individual differences in rhythmic neural tracking covary with neural indices of statistical structure extraction may help clarify whether temporal sensitivity is related to variability in speech statistical learning.

1.2 Current study

The present study investigated whether individual differences in musical rhythm processing are associated with neural tracking of statistical structure in a continuous speech-like stream in adults. For this aim, we employed an artificial language paradigm with EEG recordings, together with both EEG and behavioral measures of rhythmic processing abilities. To assess SL, participants were exposed to a continuous speech stream composed of repeated threesyllable triplets forming artificial "words", following the seminal work by Saffran et al. (1996).

Within each triplet, syllables had high transitional probabilities (TPs), whereas TPs between triplets were lower, allowing participants to gradually extract word-like chunks from the continuous speech input. To capture this learning process online, we used EEG measures of neural tracking during exposure. Specifically, we quantified neural tracking at the syllable and word frequencies and examined how these responses changed over exposure, with earlier neural tracking of word frequencies reflecting more efficient statistical learning. We further combined the neural (EEG-based) indices of SL in an artificial speech stream with the behavioral and neural (EEG-based) measures of rhythm processing in musical material. This approach allowed us to test whether rhythmic processing abilities may be related to the segmentation and learning of structured and temporally organized sequences in artificial spoken language material. We hypothesized that neural tracking at the word rate would emerge over exposure (reflecting statistical learning), and that stronger neural tracking of musical rhythm would be associated with earlier maximal word-rate tracking and/or stronger overall word-rate tracking. We also collected information about participants' musical background and self-reported musical abilities, based on previous work suggesting that musicians show enhanced sensitivity to structured auditory input (François et al., 2012; Francois & Schön, 2011; Mandikal Vasuki et al., 2017). Additionally, working memory ability was tested as a broader cognitive factor, given its potential role in supporting sequence processing and learning (see also Van Der Wulp et al., 2025a), although previous studies show conflicting results (e.g., Conway et al., 2009; Siegelman et al., 2017).

2. Methods and materials

>Methods and materials

2.1 Participants

Thirty-two French undergraduate students (29 women, 3 men) took part in the experiment. All descriptive statistics on participant characteristics, including age, years of musical training, Barcelona Music Reward Questionnaire (BMRQ) total score, and self-reported rhythmic abilities can be seen in Supplementary Information, Table S1 (link to the https://osf.io/awy7e/overview?view_only=2f19d8c8722641cfb53660b29ed53261 in the Data Availability statement). The participants were screened with the following inclusion criteria by self-report: no uncorrected vision (accepted if they wore glasses) or hearing deficits, no language impairments, being a native French speaker, and being between 18 and 35 years old.

The participants were nonmusicians, with only eight participants reporting some musical training (M = 2.88 years, median = 3.00; SD = 2.03, range = 1-7 years). Written informed consent was obtained from each participant before the start of the experiment. The study was carried out in accordance with the guidelines of the Declaration of Helsinki and approved by the ethics committee of the Université Bourgogne Europe (CERUBFC-2023-10-05-044). The target sample size was determined using a power analysis for a two-tailed Pearson correlation, with α = .05 and 80% power. The sample size estimation was also informed by previous literature (e.g., Abla et al., 2008; Batterink & Paller, 2019; Franco et al., 2015). Under these assumptions, a sample of approximately 32 participants was required to detect an association of this magnitude.

2.2 Artificial language materials for the SL EEG recordings

The artificial language was composed of 12 consonant-vowel syllables, drawn from a set of four consonants (/t/, /p/, /k/, /f/) and four vowels (/a/, /o/, /i/, /u/). Syllables were synthesized using MBROLA software (Dutoit et al., 1996) with the fr4 French female voice from the MBROLA database. The complete list of syllables and words is available on https://osf.io/awy7e/overview?view_only=2f19d8c8722641cfb53660b29ed53261. These syllables were combined to form four trisyllabic "words" with high internal transitional probabilities (TPs = 1.0 within words; TP = 0.33 across word boundaries), following the design established by Saffran et al. (1996). Each syllable lasted 250 ms (giving a syllable rate of 4 Hz), and the syllables were presented in immediate succession, resulting in triplets with a total duration of 750 ms (giving a word rate of 1.33 Hz). To reduce potential confounds from specific syllable combinations, five alternative languages were used, which contained the same set of syllables, but rearranged into different triplets (Perruchet & Desaulty, 2008; see Iorio et al., 2026). These five languages had been tested in our behavioral study reported in Iorio et al., (2026; Experiment 1); they had been pretested and selected from six originally created artificial languages to ensure that the artificial languages did not unintentionally contain or evoke existing French words (see Iorio et al., 2026 for details).

Both structured and random streams consisted of continuous sequences of spoken syllables chained without acoustic markers or pauses. The structured stream comprised 160 repetitions of the four trisyllabic words, concatenated in a pseudorandom order with two constraints: no word appeared consecutively, and any given sequence of two words could not be repeated twice in a row. This stream was divided into ten equal segments, each containing an identical number of words. Streams were regenerated as needed to maintain an average TP between syllables of adjacent words between 0.3 and 0.35. The resulting exposure stream had a duration of 5 minutes.

The random stream included all 12 syllables repeated 100 times each (1200 syllables in total) and was constructed to remove the statistical regularities present in the structured stream. To avoid simple repetitions or predictable patterns, the same syllable never occurred twice in a row, and the order of syllables was constrained to a maximum of two consecutive switches. The 1200 syllables were divided into 20 segments, each regenerated to balance syllable frequency and ensure uniform TPs. The random stream had a total duration of 5 minutes.

2.3 Temporal processing and rhythm measures 2.3.1 Behavioral measures

To assess rhythm perception, we used the Beat-Based Advantage Task (BBA; Fiveash et al., 2022; Gordon et al., 2015; Grahn & Brett, 2009), consisting of 16 trials. Each trial contained two identical rhythm patterns, followed by a third rhythm pattern. On eight trials, the third rhythmic pattern repeated the first two (participants were to press the key for "same"); on eight trials, there was a change in the third rhythm pattern ("different"). Crossed with this, half of the trials had simple rhythmic patterns (strong metrical structure) and half had complex rhythmic patterns (weaker metrical structure with syncopation). Each rhythm was presented at one of four pure tone frequencies (294, 411, 470, or 587 Hz) and separated by 1500 ms (see Fiveash et al., 2022 for details). The sequence lasted 12-13 s, with each rhythm lasting approximately 3 s. This task was run in OpenSesame (Mathôt et al., 2012).

Rhythm perception and production abilities were further assessed using two tasks from the Battery for the Assessment of Auditory and Sensorimotor Timing Abilities (BAASTA; Dalla Bella et al., 2017 Bella et al., , 2024)). Rhythm perception was evaluated using the Beat Alignment Test (BAT; Iversen & Patel, 2008), in the adaptation of Fiveash et al. (2022). Participants listened to four musical sequences (two piano excerpts from Bach's Badinerie and two from Rossini's William Tell Overture, referred to as Music1 and Music 2, respectively, ~38s each, but cut in half for the perception task, leading to 4 items lasting ~16s each, inter-beat interval, IBI, 600 ms; Dalla Bella et al., 2017). Approximately 3-4s into each sequence, an isochronous sequence was introduced (high-pitched triangle timbre) that was either aligned, phase misaligned (shifted ±33% of the inter-beat interval), or period and phase misaligned (with a tempo 10% faster or slower than the beat). Participants judged whether the triangle sequence was aligned with the musical beat, responding as soon as they felt confident. This task contained 48 trials (see Fiveash et al., 2022 for details) and was run in OpenSesame (Mathôt et al., 2012).

Rhythm production was assessed with a tapping task (from the BAASTA), in which participants were asked to tap along with the beat of the same musical sequences (Badinerie -Music1 and William Tell Overture -Music 2; ~38s each, IBI = 600 ms, 64 beats each). The task was implemented as an application on a tablet device (Samsung Galaxy Tab E running Android 7.1; Dalla Bella et al., 2024b; Puyjarinet et al., 2017) and participants tapped within a green rectangle (10.0 × 8.8 cm) displayed on the touchscreen. However, due to technical issues with this implementation, participants' rhythmic production performance was only partially retrievable (see below for further details).

Musical short-term memory (STM) was assessed using a serial-order STM task, in which participants judged whether two sequences of tones were identical or differed in their order of presentation (based on Talamini et al., 2022 Talamini et al., , 2026)). One sequence consisted of five tones with different pitches (chosen from a set of six tones of the F-Major scale, i.e., F2, G2, A2, Bb2, C3, D3, ranging from 175 Hz to 294 Hz), with each tone played using an acoustic piano timbre for a duration of 500 ms, and presented in the sequence with silent 100 ms inter-tone-intervals. The two sequences of a pair were separated by a silent delay of 2 s. On different trials, the order of the items of the sequence was changed. Participants were required to respond "same" or "different" by pressing one of two keys on the computer keyboard. They performed four example trials (with response feedback), followed by 40 experimental trials (without response feedback), presented in pseudorandom order. This task was run with Presentation software (Neurobehavioral systems).

Additionally, participants completed a forward Digit Span 1 (Wechsler, 2008) task to estimate their short-term memory capacity for verbal sequences. Participants listened to sequences of digits read aloud by the experimenter (with one digit per second) and were asked to repeat them in the same order.

1 While a forward digit span task is associated with verbal working memory and depends on the phonological loop, the backward Digit Span task is more associated with executive functioning and cognitive control (e.g., Ostrosky-Solís & Lozano, 2006). Therefore, here we used only the forward digit span (see also Van der Wulp et al. 2025b).

2.3.2 EEG measures

The musical stimuli used during the EEG measurement were those from the behavioral BAASTA tasks (see above): the two excerpts Music 1 and Music 2 in their entire duration (38 s each and without the triangle sound). Additionally, we used an isochronous sequence of the same duration (referred to as "Metronome" hereafter); it included a repeatedly presented tone (played with a woodblock timbre) chained without silence (with an inter-tone-interval of 600 ms, thus matching the IBI of the musical excerpts).

2.4 Procedure

2.4.1 EEG measurement

Participants were first informed about the full experimental procedure and provided written informed consent. They were then prepared for the EEG recording. Following preparation, participants were seated comfortably in an acoustically and electrically shielded booth. They were instructed to maintain their gaze fixed straight ahead, remain relaxed, and to listen attentively to the auditory stimuli throughout the exposure. To avoid potential confounds, no information about the nature of the auditory stimulation was provided, preventing participants from intentionally attempting to discover word units in case they recognized the stimuli as language. The experimental session began with exposure to the structured artificial language stream, followed by the random stream. This order was intentionally fixed to avoid potential carryover effects associated with presenting the random stream first; previous work from our group showed that prior exposure to random sequences can interfere with SL performance and reduce the extraction of regularities from subsequently presented structured streams (Iorio et al., 2026). Participants then listened to the musical materials (Music1, Music 2, and Metronome streams), which were presented in randomized order, five times each. All auditory streams throughout the experiment were separated by silent intervals randomly varying between 2 and 2.5 s. Stimuli were delivered binaurally via speakers in the booth. The experiment was coded and run using the software Psychtoolbox for MATLAB (Kleiner et al., 2007).

2.4.2 Behavioral measures

Following the EEG session, participants were invited to wash their hair and then seated in a quiet room outside the recording booth for the battery of behavioral tasks. The BAT and the BBA rhythmic tasks were administered in a computerized format, with stimuli presented via headphones. Rhythmic production abilities were then assessed through a tapping task, implemented in the tablet-based version of the BAASTA battery via headphones. Short-term memory was then assessed using a serial-order musical STM task and using the classical forward digit span task, administered with standard procedures (Wechsler, 2008).

Finally, participants completed a questionnaire assessing their musical background and selfestimated musical abilities, including the BMRQ (Mas-Herrero et al., 2013). The entire experimental session took about 90 minutes.

2.5 EEG recordings and data analyses

The electroencephalogram (EEG) was recorded from 64 active scalp electrodes arranged according to the international 10-20 system using a BioSemi ActiveTwo system. During data acquisition, BioSemi's Common Mode Sense (CMS) and Driven Right Leg (DRL) electrodes served as the reference and ground loop, respectively. Additional electrodes were positioned on the left and right mastoids (A1, A2 for offline re-referencing). All electrodes were filled with conductive Signa gel to ensure optimal signal transmission. EEG signals were digitized at a sampling rate of 512 Hz and recorded using ActiView software.

2.5.1 Neural data preprocessing

For both the SL and music/metronome exposure phases (referred to as statistical learning and rhythm tasks, respectively), EEG data were band-pass filtered between 0.5 and 20 Hz and eyeblink artifacts were then removed by independent component analysis (ICA) using the EEGLAB toolbox (Delorme & Makeig, 2004).

For the SL task, the EEG data were then preprocessed using custom MATLAB scripts following the APICE preprocessing pipeline previously used for SL data (Fló, Benjamin, et al., 2022), and subsequently re-referenced to the average reference. The preprocessed EEG data were segmented from the onset of each condition into 0.75 s segments corresponding to individual triplets. Segments containing artifacts (labeled by APICE) were excluded from further analysis.

On average, 94.24% ± 7.29 of the segments were retained for the random streams, and 96.48% ± 6.56 for the structured stream. These segments were then reshaped into nonoverlapping trials of 7.5 s (10 triplets, 30 syllables), while preserving their chronological order.

For the music material and the metronome exposure, EEG data were segmented into nonoverlapping 30-s trials (corresponding to 50 successive, nonoverlapping 600-ms segments) and preprocessed using an Artifact Blocking algorithm (Fujioka et al., 2011; Mourad et al., 2007), during which artifacts were identified using a voltage threshold of ±75 µV. The data were subsequently re-referenced to the average reference.

2.5.2 Spectral analysis of EEG

Spectral analyses were performed to quantify neural tracking of stimulus regularities in both the SL and rhythm tasks. For each task, EEG data from each trial were transformed into the frequency domain using Fast Fourier Transform, yielding complex-valued frequency components. Neural alignment to stimulus regularities was quantified using inter-trial coherence (ITC), computed separately for each electrode and condition for the SL and rhythm tasks. ITC measures the consistency of phase alignment across trials at a given frequency, derived from the phase of the Fourier-transformed signal, and is defined as ITC(f) = 1/N |∑ 𝑁 𝑖=1 𝑒 𝑗𝜙 (𝑓,𝑖) |, where N is the number of trials and 𝜙 (𝑓,𝑖) denotes the phase of the signal at frequency 𝑓 in trial 𝑖 (Lachaux et al., 1999; Tallon-Baudry et al., 1996). ITC values range from 0, indicating completely desynchronized activity across trials, to 1, indicating perfect phase locking across trials. To assess the specificity of neural alignment at the frequencies of interest, ITC values were normalized relative to neighboring frequency bins. For each target frequency, the mean and standard deviation of ITC were computed across the twelve surrounding frequency bins (-1 to -6 and +1 to +6 bins; frequency resolution = .133 Hz for the SL task and .033 Hz for the rhythm task). The ITC at the target frequency was then z-scored by subtracting the mean of the neighboring bins and dividing by their standard deviation, as implemented for ITC in Fló et al. (2022), yielding a normalized measure of neural alignment, hereafter referred to as normalized ITC.

2.5.2 Neural tracking of syllable and word rates over time

To characterize changes in neural tracking of syllable and word rates over exposure to structured and random streams, normalized ITC was quantified using sliding time windows of 2 min with a step size of 1.5 s, at both the syllabic and word-related frequencies (Flo et al. 2022). For the structured stream, individual time courses were obtained by averaging ITC over the electrodes identified in the population-level analyses. The same procedure was also applied to the random stream using electrodes showing significant syllable-rate responses. For comparability between analyses, individual ITC time courses were smoothed using a movingaverage filter (10 consecutive time points), and the time of the maximum smoothed response was extracted as the time-to-peak measure. For the structured stream, this measure was interpreted as the point during exposure at which word-rate neural response was strongest, reflecting the statistical learning of the words. Group-level time courses were obtained by averaging ITC values across participants.

2.5.3 Statistical analyses of neural data of statistical learning and musical rhythm

Neural tracking of the statistical regularities was examined in structured and random streams for the entire exposure duration. The target frequencies were the syllable and word rates for the SL exposure stream, corresponding to 4 Hz and 1.33 Hz, respectively. For the musical stimuli, the primary temporal rate was the beat level (1.66 Hz), with 3.33 Hz and 6.66 Hz included as subdivisions (half and quarter durations) of the beat and because clear acoustic peaks revealed by the acoustic power spectral density (PSD) analyses were present at these frequencies (see Figure S.1 in the supplementary materials). We calculated a rhythm-related index that was an omnibus measure of neural alignment to the musical temporal structure, averaging the three frequencies (1.66 Hz, 3.33 Hz, and 6.66 Hz) across significant electrodes and both musical sequences (Music1 and Music2), thus a measure of general temporal-regularity tracking (rather than focusing on a beat-specific measure). The same frequency set was used for the metronome condition for consistency, although this stimulus contained additional acoustic peaks (see All statistical analyses were performed in MATLAB (MathWorks). Normality was assessed using the Lilliefors test (Lilliefors, 1969). For the statistical analysis of neural responses to stimulus regularities, normalized ITC values corresponding to each electrode and each frequency of interest were compared against zero using one-tailed t-tests, testing the hypothesis that normalized ITC at a given frequency would exceed zero, indicating the presence of neural alignment. To control for multiple comparisons across electrodes (N = 64), p-values were corrected using the false discovery rate (FDR) procedure (Benjamini & Hochberg, 1995). To assess the significance of the neural response over the learning time course, normalized ITC values of each time window were compared against zero using one-tailed t-tests, testing whether normalized ITC exceeded zero. P-values were corrected for multiple comparisons (N = 121) across time windows using the FDR procedure.

To examine the relationship between the emergence of word tracking (i.e., reflecting the learning of the statistical regularities) and sensitivity to rhythmic structure in the musical material (Music 1, Music 2), Pearson correlations were computed between SL and rhythmrelated neural indices. SL was indexed by two measures, each averaged over electrodes showing significant responses at both the syllabic and word rates at the population level: (i) ITC values at syllabic (4 Hz) and word (1.33 Hz) frequencies during exposure to the structured stream and (ii) the time point of maximal neural response during exposure (i.e., the time point at which neural responses reached their maximum value during exposure to the structured stream for each participant). To further evaluate whether individual differences in rhythm processing relate to learning of statistical regularities, we compared the SL time course between the two extreme groups of participants (10 per group) selected from the upper (upper extreme) and lower (lower extreme) ends of the distribution of neural response to rhythmic structure (as measured by the rhythm-related index). Differences in the time point at which learning reached its maximum value were compared between groups using a two-sample t-test.

2.5.4 Behavioral data analyses

For the Beat-Based Advantage (BBA), Beat Alignment Test (BAT), and musical short-term memory (STM) tasks, performance was quantified as the number of Hits minus the number of False Alarms (FAs), providing a measure of sensitivity while accounting for response bias. In the BBA task, Hits corresponded to correctly identifying trials in which the third rhythm differed from the first two, whereas FAs corresponded to incorrectly judging the third rhythm as different when it was identical to the preceding rhythms. In the BAT, Hits corresponded to correctly detecting a misaligned metronome, whereas False Alarms corresponded to erroneously reporting a misalignment when the metronome was correctly aligned with the beat.

Tapping data from the BAASTA were pre-processed following the procedures described in Dalla Bella et al. (2024). Due to technical issues affecting the recording of the tapping performance, only data from the Music 1 condition could be analyzed. As in Dalla Bella et al.

(2024), two measures of synchronization were extracted from the performance, based on circular statistical analyses: synchronization consistency and synchronization accuracy.

Consistency was quantified as the length of vector R, ranging from 0 to 1, with lower values indicating a uniform distribution of tapping phases around the circle (i.e., absence of synchronization) and higher values indicating greater phase consistency (i.e., reduced variability). Accuracy was defined as the angle of vector R (θ, or relative phase, in degrees), reflecting whether participants tapped before (negative values) or after (positive values) the pacing stimulus. Accuracy was calculated only for participants showing above-chance synchronization performance, as determined by the Rayleigh test for circular uniformity (Fisher, 1993; Wilkie, 1983). For statistical analyses, synchronization consistency values (vector length) were logit-transformed to reduce skewness following previous approaches (e.g., Falk et al., 2015); whereas accuracy was analyzed using the vector direction (relative phase as in Dalla Bella et al., 2024), considering only values falling between -90 to 90 degrees for suitability in linear analyses, and expressed as percentage of the inter-stimulus interval.

In the musical STM task, Hits corresponded to correctly identifying trials in which the tone sequences differed in presentation order, whereas False Alarms corresponded to incorrectly judging identical sequences as different.

Working memory performance was indexed using the forward digit span task, with scores corresponding to the maximum sequence length correctly recalled according to standard scoring procedures (Weschler, 2008).

For all analyses, Bayes factors were computed using JASP software (JASP, Team, 2019). A standard noninformative Cauchy prior with a default width of 0.707 was applied. We report BF₁₀ values, where values between 3 and 10 indicate moderately strong evidence in favor of the alternative hypothesis (H₁; Doorn et al., 2021).

4 Discussion

The present study investigated whether individual differences in behavioral and neural sensitivity to rhythmic structure are related to the neural extraction of statistical regularities during speech segmentation. To address this question, we combined EEG measures of SL in an artificial speech stream with behavioral and neural indices of rhythm processing in musical material. Several findings emerged. First, the structured speech stream elicited reliable neural tracking at both the syllable and word frequencies, whereas the random stream elicited syllablerate tracking but no reliable word-rate tracking response. Second, in the structured stream, the temporal evolution of the syllable and word-rate responses differed: syllable-rate tracking was present from the beginning of exposure and remained relatively stable, whereas word-rate tracking emerged progressively over time. Third, individuals with stronger neural synchronization to rhythmic musical structure showed better syllable-rate tracking during speech exposure and a faster emergence of neural encoding of word rate. Together, these findings indicate that individual differences in neural sensitivity to temporal structure are related to how neural sensitivity to higher-order statistical structure emerges during speech exposure. They are therefore compatible with the hypothesis that temporal prediction contributes to sequential structure extraction across domains. Consistent with previous frequency-tagging studies of SL (e.g., Batterink & Paller, 2017; Buiatti et al., 2009), the structured speech stream elicited neural responses at both the syllabic and word frequencies, whereas the random stream elicited only syllable-rate tracking. The presence of word-rate entrainment specifically in the structured stream is consistent with the extraction of higherorder word-like units from the continuous syllable stream, as the word-rate periodicity is a direct consequence of the statistical organization imposed by the triplets.

Importantly, examining the temporal evolution of neural responses further revealed distinct dynamics for syllabic and word rate responses. Syllable-rate responses were already present at the onset of exposure and remained stable throughout the task, reflecting tracking of the syllabic input. In contrast, word-rate responses increased over exposure and reached a significant level later in the stream, suggesting the progressive emergence of neural sensitivity to the higher-order regularities. Thus, the time-varying analysis provides information beyond the presence of word-rate entrainment by further characterizing how sensitivity to the emerging structure develops during exposure.

A central finding was that individual differences in neural response to temporal structure in music were related to the dynamics of word rate entrainment during speech exposure. Stronger neural synchronization to musical temporal regularities was associated with an earlier time to maximal word-rate ITC, whereas no such relationship was observed for the syllable-rate response. In the group comparison, participants exhibiting stronger rhythm-related neural responses reached their maximal word rate response earlier than those with weaker responses, suggesting that individual differences in rhythm sensitivity may influence the time course of statistical learning. This finding extends previous evidence that learners differ in the temporal emergence of neural signatures of learning. For instance, Abla et al. (2008) reported variability in the temporal emergence of neural signatures of learning, distinguishing slower and faster learners during artificial language acquisition. Our results further suggest that stronger neural synchronization to rhythmic structures in music may be associated with more rapid extraction of regularities from speech. One possibility is that individuals who are more sensitive to temporal regularities are better able to align attention with expected events, facilitating the detection of recurring structures. This interpretation is consistent with theoretical accounts in which rhythm provides a temporal scaffold for prediction (Jones & Boltz, 1989; Large & Jones, 1999) and with evidence that temporal synchronization can influence speech processing and segmentation. For example, Assaneo et al., (2019) showed within the speech domain that spontaneous synchronization to an external syllable rhythm was associated with stronger neural responses and superior speech segmentation performance. Beyond temporal prediction, the association may also reflect shared mechanisms for processing sequential and hierarchical structures, as suggested by links between musical rhythm and grammar skills (Gordon et al., 2015) and shared neural resources for rhythm and syntax (Heard & Lee, 2020). Our findings thus extend previous work across domains by showing that neural sensitivity to musical temporal structure is associated with the timing of neural responses to higher-order structure in speech.

The subsequent decline in word-rate responses observed in our study may further reflect the dynamic nature of learning, with neural sensitivity peaking during the extraction of regularities and decreasing as the emerging structure becomes more familiar or predictable. A similar pattern was reported by Abla et al. (2008), who observed a decrease in word-onset-related ERP responses as learning progressed in high learners. Interestingly, the authors also speculated that the word-onset effect observed in middle learners might have disappeared with more prolonged exposure, as their responses peaked during the last session. This interpretation is particularly relevant to our findings, as participants with weaker rhythm-related responses reached their maximal word-rate response later and may therefore have exhibited a similar decline had exposure been extended. A comparable decrease in neural responses during learning has also been reported in neonates during bisyllabic word learning (Flo et al., 2025), although responses remained significant. Differences in exposure duration may partly account for this discrepancy, as the longer exposure in our study may have allowed the decline in neural responses to become more pronounced. Importantly, the absence of a comparable decline in the syllable-rate response argues against a general deterioration in data quality and supports an interpretation in terms of learning-related dynamics.

Overall, our findings suggest that neural sensitivity to musical temporal structure is associated with the timing of neural sensitivity to higher-order structure in speech. However, the correlational nature of these findings precludes conclusions about the direction of this relationship. Although sensitivity to rhythmic structure may facilitate statistical learning, it is also possible that individual differences in statistical learning abilities influence rhythmic processing, or that a shared underlying factor contributes to variability in both abilities. Further research is needed to disentangle these possibilities and clarify the relationship between rhythmic processing and statistical learning.

Our findings are broadly compatible with previous results showing overlapping neural resources suggesting shared, domain-general temporal prediction and sequencing networks that apply to music, language and action. At the neural level, temporal prediction and auditorymotor integration have been linked to networks involving auditory, frontal, and motor regions, including pathways connecting auditory cortex with inferior frontal and motor regions (Hickok & Poeppel, 2007; Poeppel & Assaneo, 2020). More broadly, meta-analytic evidence points to a cortical-subcortical prediction network supporting temporal prediction across domains (Siman-Tov et al., 2019). Rhythm and sequence processing have also been associated with the supplementary motor area, basal ganglia, cerebellum, auditory cortex, primary motor cortex, and prefrontal regions (Cona & Semenza, 2017; Janata et al., 2002). These regions are thought to encode temporal and ordinal properties of sequences independently of stimulus identity, thereby supporting domain-general sequencing operations. In line with this perspective, the Procedural Deficit Hypothesis proposes that disruptions in procedural memory circuits, particularly involving frontal and basal ganglia networks, may contribute not only to language impairments but also to broader deficits in sequence learning and statistical learning (Lum et al., 2013; Ullman, 2001; Ullman & Pierpont, 2005). Although the present study did not directly assess procedural learning mechanisms, our findings are compatible with the view that individual differences in the efficiency of such domain-general sequencing systems may influence both rhythmic entrainment and the extraction of sequential regularities during speech segmentation.

The present results also align with broader evidence linking rhythm processing abilities to language skills. Rhythm perception and production have been associated with phonological processing, grammar, reading, and spelling abilities (e.g., Flaugnacco et al., 2014; Gordon et al., 2015; Politimou et al., 2018), while impairments in rhythm processing are frequently observed in populations with language-related disorders, such as dyslexia and developmental language disorder (Goswami, 2011; Ladányi et al., 2020). These populations also show impairments in SL (e.g., Kapa & Mettler, 2024), suggesting shared difficulties in extracting and predicting sequential structure. These findings have motivated frameworks such as PRISM (Fiveash et al., 2021), which propose shared temporal processing mechanisms across speech and music.

Interestingly, in the present study, the strongest associations with SL involved EEG measures rather than behavioral indices of rhythm processing, as no significant behavioral-EEG relationships emerged. This pattern is consistent with recent work by Van Der Wulp et al., (2025a), who argued that relationships between SL and rhythm processing may be more reliably captured when similar measures are used across domains. Indeed, Van Der Wulp et al., (2025a) did not observe a relationship when comparing SL and rhythm processing across methods (EEG, behavior) in adults, but they did observe a relationship when using the same method for SL and rhythm processing in their developmental study: using neural frequency-tagging measures in both rhythm and SL tasks revealed associations in infants (van der Wulp, 2025b). The present study followed this rationale by using EEG to characterize both SL and rhythmic processing. The behavioral measures of rhythm provided converging assessments of rhythmic individual differences, whereas the EEG measure captured neural sensitivity to temporal regularity using a method directly comparable to the EEG measure used for speech.

In the present study, we did not include a behavioral measure of SL, which could have been a completion, but probably not sensitive enough when only placed at the end of the exposure phase as generally done in SL studies. Future research should rather target the inclusion of online measurements of SL over time, as recently introduced (e.g., Franco et al., 2015; Hunt & Aslin, 2001; Poulin-Charronnat et al., 2017), but which would need to be cleverly adapted to allow for simultaneous EEG recording to avoid motor response influences.

Several implications follow from our findings. First, the present findings suggest that individual differences in the temporal dynamics of neural statistical-structure tracking may be an informative target for future research on SL. Second, they raise the possibility that temporal sensitivity is one source of variability in how rapidly higher-order structure becomes neurally expressed.

Future studies should now go beyond correlational studies and investigate potential causality.

For example, implementing rhythmic training (e.g., Dalla Bella, 2022; Jamey et al., 2024; Jamey, et al., 2026a; 2026b) or directly manipulating rhythmic structures of statistical sequences (see Selchenkova, et al., 2014a; 2014b with artificial grammar learning) will help determine whether enhancing rhythmic processing directly facilitates speech segmentation.

Interestingly, participating in more general musical activities (even though including more activities than solely rhythmic processing) has been shown to increase word segmentation abilities in children (in comparison to painting activities; Francois et al., 2013). Future work could further investigate the specific contribution of auditory-motor synchronization abilities to the observed relationships, in line with previously shown motor involvement for rhythm processing (e.g., Fujioka et al., 2012 Fujioka et al., , 2015)). Importantly, such work should be interpreted in light of ecological constraints, as natural speech is not composed of isochronous syllables and is characterized by substantial coarticulation and prosodic variability. The present paradigm was designed to isolate the contribution of statistical regularities by removing potentially covarying acoustic and temporal cues that naturally accompany word boundaries (following the seminal work by Saffran et al., 1995). While this approach allows a more precise investigation of SL mechanisms, real-world language learning likely relies on the integration of statistical, temporal, and prosodic information. As for instance, Hoch et al. (2013) showed that participants learned artificial speech units more effectively when units had consistent temporal structure (e.g., sequences of equally timed syllables) compared with units of varying lengths. Such temporal regularity may facilitate learning by providing reliable anchors for attention, consistent with theories of dynamic attending, which propose that rhythmic structures shape attentional cycles and enhance processing at predictable moments (Jones & Boltz, 1989; Large & Jones, 1999). Determining whether the relationship observed here generalizes to more naturalistic speech, where temporal and statistical cues interact, will be a promising direction for future research.

In conclusion, the present study revealed that neural sensitivity to rhythmic temporal structure in music is associated with the temporal dynamics of neural tracking of higher-order statistical structure in continuous speech in adults. The structured speech stream elicited word-rate neural tracking that emerged progressively over exposure, and individuals with stronger musical rhythm-related neural responses tended to reach maximal word-rate entrainment earlier. These findings suggest a cross-domain association between neural sensitivity to temporal regularity and the dynamics of speech-structure tracking. Our findings suggest that individual differences in temporal processing may be related to how efficiently neural sensitivity to sequential structure emerges across auditory domains, providing a basis for future experimental work
