Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine, Daniel Lakens and Cristian Mesquida

Overall, among all instances flagged as power statements, 90.6% were correct (positive predictive value).

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How Marginal Significance works

The marginal module searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The list of terms is a subset of those listed in a blog post by Matthew Hankins. The module returns all sentences that match terms describing ‘marginally significant’ results.

Some of the terms identified might not be problematic in some contexts, and there are ways to describe ‘marginal significance’ that are not detected by the module.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). Of the misses, 13 are starred table notes (“*p < .05”) that the module leaves out on purpose and 11 are p-values it did not find; of the wrong reports, 20 are p-values the ground truth does not contain (such as “ps < .05”) and 6 use ≤ or ≥. How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on three hand-coded sets of papers (scienceverse/validation: 249 Psychological Science, 250 Economics and 250 MDPI papers). Of 1,783 non-significant p-values in the ground truth, version 0.4.4.3 found 1,719 (3.6% missed), and 94.8% of its 1,814 detections were in the ground truth. How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). How Reproducibility Check works

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. 1. Introduction: two opposite errors

A school leaver with the grades for university does not apply. One interpretation is that the student considered further study and decided against it. Another is that university never became a practical alternative. Rational action models of educational differentials give a prominent place to decisions at educational branching points (Breen & Goldthorpe, 1997). Research on habitus and horizons for action examines how social conditions shape which routes students regard as possible (Ball et al., 2002; Hodkinson & Sparkes, 1997). Both approaches offer resources for explaining non-participation. Neither the decision nor its absence, however, is contained in the bare record of non-application.

The problem extends beyond education. An employee remains in a post, a resident renews a permit, and a learner undertakes no pronunciation training. Continuity may follow a considered resolution, an unquestioned routine, or a project to change that has not been carried out. Gambetta's (1987) question, Were They Pushed or Did They Jump?, directs attention to the mechanisms behind educational outcomes. Attempts to combine rational action and habitus approaches develop that inquiry further (Glaesser & Cooper, 2014). The present article examines how evidence warrants attributing a particular relation to an alternative.

Institutional procedures make this a theoretical as well as a methodological problem. In one arrangement, staying requires a signed renewal; in another, it happens automatically. The same continued participation then produces an explicit response in one setting and no response in the other. A signature can be routine, while an unrecorded continuation can follow private deliberation. Institutions organize both conduct and the evidence through which researchers interpret it. Unless those processes are distinguished, differences in the visibility of decisions can be mistaken for differences in the decisions themselves.

The term choice requires specification. An economic model may describe a selection without asserting conscious comparison, and meaningful action need not involve deliberation. This article concerns claims that an actor considered a practical alternative and resolved upon a course, and claims denying this within a specified episode. Deliberation need not be verbal, calculating, or prolonged, but it entails more than an observer's identification of an available option. A model that uses choice in a purely formal sense has a different evidential task.

Two errors become possible. The first infers a decision from a non-event: the student did not apply, therefore the student decided not to apply. The second infers non-choice from failure to establish a decision. Yet private comparison, an earlier resolution, and routine continuation can leave the same administrative record. This is the problem of negative evidence in choice attribution. Failure to establish that a decision occurred does not by itself establish that none occurred. Missing evidence may nevertheless favor non-choice when its production and preservation are understood.

Weber's treatment of omission makes subjective orientation essential to the interpretation of nonoccurrence. Schutz's analysis of projects explains why the observer's alternatives cannot substitute for the actor's possibilities (Schutz, 1951 (Schutz, /1962a). Etzrodt's (2024) reconstruction of non-choice makes habitual continuation a substantive alternative to renewed decision. Eberle's (2025) discussion of adequacy sharpens the requirement that an intelligible interpretation fit the concrete course of action. These resources define what needs reconstructing. Process-tracing methodology helps specify when available or missing evidence can support that reconstruction (Bennett, 2010; Fairfield & Charman, 2017).

The article makes three contributions. First, it adds the deferred project to the configurations behind unchanged conduct. The concept rests on Schutz's distinction between projecting, purposing, and executing. It identifies an adopted change whose occasion of execution has passed, so that present continuity coexists with an orientation toward change. Second, it states the evidential value of missing traces as a comparison across these configurations, rather than as a contrast between decision and its absence. Third, it shows that institutional response rules and defaults produce the visibility of choice. Records generated under different rules can create apparent differences in agency between groups whose relations to an alternative are similar. A published comparison of default and active-decision enrollment in retirement saving is the worked case (Carroll et al., 2009). Three premises and three propositions state the framework; the last proposition develops conditional expectations about responses to salience prompts and execution support.

The approach combines conceptual reconstruction with analysis of published studies and schematic cases. Section 2 locates the problem in research on educational choice and non-action.

Sections 3 and 4 develop the relevant commitments in Weber and Schutz. Section 5 distinguishes the four configurations. Section 6 connects them to evidence and states the premises and propositions. Section 7 develops applications, including the worked case, before the conclusion.

2. One non-event, two readings

>One non-event, two readings

2.1. Non-change represented as a decision

Breen and Goldthorpe's (1997) model represents educational branching points as decisions involving costs, benefits, and subjective probabilities of success. Class differences in educational outcomes are connected to the aim of avoiding downward mobility. The model specifies a decision mechanism through which social position can affect continuation. Whether a particular student's departure involved a corresponding episode of consideration remains an empirical question. Sen's (1973) criticism of reading preference directly from behavior identifies a related difficulty: observations support claims about preference only through additional assumptions. The present concern comes earlier. When a researcher invokes considered rejection, the existence of consideration itself requires support. Erikson et al. (2005) provide a clear example of the distinction. Their decomposition of class differentials at age 16 assumes an idealized sequence from performance through choice to outcome. The transition variable records whether students continued to A-level education, rather than whether they deliberated about continuing (p. 9730). Interpreting performance-adjusted class differences as differences in choice is a model-based interpretation. Their discussion also proposes an anticipatory decision against A-level study that might reduce examination preparation and so affect measured performance (p. 9733). The analysis includes class background, academic performance, and transition outcomes, but does not directly observe the deliberative episodes involved. The authors explicitly identify early choice as unobserved and call for more detailed data. The point is not an error on their part; they flag the gap themselves.

Their discussion locates an evidential task: determining whether students weighed continuation, followed an unquestioned course, or retained an unexecuted plan.

Other research measures the model's subjective terms directly (Kroneberg & Kalter, 2012). Stocké (2007), for example, examines families' beliefs about educational success and status maintenance in explaining secondary-school track selection. Such designs add evidence about the proposed mechanism beyond the transition itself. How those beliefs organized a particular episode, and whether an alternative was considered and resolved, can then be investigated rather than inferred from non-transition alone.

Aggregate distributions can support a mechanism through comparisons, additional predictions, and the elimination of competing explanations. Agreement with an outcome distribution alone leaves the deliberative structure of a specified episode open. The step that needs scrutiny is the move from a formal representation to an account of what actors considered. Ball et al. (2002) examine how class-specific schemes of perception shape higher-education choices. Hodkinson and Sparkes (1997) describe career decisions as pragmatically rational and situated within horizons for action. These approaches investigate the formation of the field within which decisions become possible. A route outside that field may remain unconsidered rather than be considered and rejected. Neither approach requires that every instance of non-participation have this structure. Reay and Ball's (1997) study of secondary-school choice illustrates why a dispositional explanation does not settle the question of consideration. Their analysis of self-elimination includes parents who considered private schooling before questioning whether their child would fit in. It also includes a parent who describes thinking about and deciding against a school (pp. 93-94). The authors also recognize that an open-ended account of a child's future admits more than one interpretation (pp. 94-95). Self-elimination here concerns the social formation of evaluations and can operate through deliberation. It leaves open whether an option ever became a practical question. That further attribution needs evidence about the episode. Etzrodt (2024) places non-choice at the center of a comparison of Parsons, Schutz, and economics. In his reconstruction of Schutz, familiar conduct continues while it remains unproblematic; alternatives need not be evaluated anew. His coffee example shows how a disruption can bring another possibility into consideration (2024, p. 226). He also rejects redescribing the elimination of choice as a choice not to choose (2024, p. 229). These issues extend his exchange with Esser about Schutz and the definition of the situation (Etzrodt, 2000; Esser, 2000).

2.2. Non-change explained through horizons and habit

The account explains how continuation without renewed choice is possible. Habit is a substantive explanation of continuity, and attributing it to an episode cannot rest on failure to observe a decision. Gambetta (1987) distinguishes constraints, causes operating behind actors' backs, and purposive orientations toward future rewards. Glaesser and Cooper (2014) combine rational action and habitus approaches, while Kroneberg (2014) specifies conditions for shifts between automatic and reflecting modes. This work makes the conditions of decision central to explanation.

2.3. Mechanisms and their attribution

The present concern is the step from a possible mechanism to a claim about an episode. Outcome distributions can leave several actor-level processes compatible with the result, and retrospective accounts require interpretation as statements made later. Emirbayer and Mische (1998) offer the closest general framework. They divide agency into three elements. The iterational element is oriented to the past through habit, the projective element imagines alternative futures, and the practical-evaluative element judges among possibilities in the present. The configurations developed below map onto this triad only in part. Nondeliberative continuation is mainly iterational, and deliberate continuation and abstention are practical-evaluative. The deferred project is a projective orientation that has not passed into enactment. The present question also differs from theirs: it asks what evidence licenses attributing one of these elements to a specified episode.

2.4. Neighboring work on non-action

Scott's (2018) sociology of nothing treats absences and non-doing as objects of sociological inquiry. The present focus is the evidential reconstruction of the actor's projective relation to a particular non-event, including its temporal scope. Bachrach and Baratz's (1963) concept of nondecision-making concerns power exercised through the exclusion of issues from political agendas. It differs from an individual's lack of deliberation.

The two questions meet where institutional arrangements shape which alternatives enter personal projects, although the relation must be shown rather than assumed.

3. Weber: a meaningful omission is not a mere non-occurrence

Weber defines action through the subjective meaning attached to conduct and social action through its orientation to others. His definition includes omission and acquiescence (Weber, 1978, pp. 4, 22). This extends action beyond visible doing without converting every non-occurrence into an intentional omission. A record can show that no application was sent without showing that the actor withheld one.

The distinction prevents the observer's counterfactual from taking the place of the actor's orientation. An analyst may identify enrollment as objectively possible, but the student may never have made it a project. It also prevents duration from being read as repeated consent. Remaining in a job may involve a fresh resolution, the continuing effects of an earlier decision, or routine activity within an arrangement that is not being reconsidered.

Weber's requirements of adequacy on the level of meaning and causal adequacy (1978, pp. 11-12) connect interpretation to explanation. A proposed meaning must be intelligible, and its explanatory role must be supported. When a decision is inferred solely from the non-enrollment it is then used to explain, it supplies no independent evidence for the proposed mechanism.

Acquiescence sharpens the issue. Continued participation in an authority relation may be meaningfully oriented to others, yet its persistence does not show whether it was accepted, reluctantly endured, or never reconsidered. Meaning can be present without a deliberative decision, so Weber supplies a foundation for the inquiry without equating meaningful action with considered choice.

4. Schutz: the actor's alternatives are not the observer's alternatives

>Schutz: the actor's alternatives are not the observer's alternatives

4.1. Projects and their alternatives

Schutz analyzes action through projects in which the actor anticipates the act as completed. Inorder-to motives concern the future state toward which action is directed; because-motives make that orientation intelligible through biography and prior experience (Schutz, 1932 (Schutz, /1967)). A past reason for entering a practice and a present project organizing its continuation require separate reconstruction.

In "Choosing Among Projects of Action," Schutz (1951 Schutz ( /1962a) ) examines how alternatives are constituted in inner time. Projects do not confront the actor as a ready-made list equivalent to the observer's inventory. Schutz distinguishes open possibilities from problematic possibilities (pp. 81-84). Open possibilities belong to the taken-for-granted horizon of expectation.

Problematic possibilities form a field of alternatives among which the actor must decide. His criticism of utilitarian action models concerns their treatment of observed conduct as if it occurred within an already constituted field of problematic alternatives (pp. 83-84). A possibility can be known without becoming a matter to be settled within a current project. Schutz's later analysis of relevance specifies how such a matter arises. It distinguishes topical relevance, which makes something a theme, from the interpretational and motivational relevances that govern how the theme is understood and acted on. A theme can become topical because the actor turns to it or because a situation imposes it (Schutz, 1970). Schutz also treats purposive abstention as action (1953/1962b, p. 20), allowing non-performance to be the object of an intention.

In the essay on choosing, Schutz also separates projecting from purposing. A project is an anticipation of conduct in phantasy. It becomes a purpose when a voluntative "fiat" supervenes and transforms it (Schutz, 1951 (Schutz, /1962a, p. 67), p. 67). Projecting, purposing, and executing are three moments that can come apart in time. This distinction will matter for the deferred project in Section 5. An employee's access to a recruitment website does not show that changing jobs became a practical question. The employee may also have weighed a change without applying anywhere.

Objective availability, awareness, practical consideration, and resolution are distinct, and their relationship is an empirical matter.

4.2. Habit and the limits of choosing

Schutz's account of the taken-for-granted world explains how conduct can proceed through familiar knowledge, expectations, and routines. Projected action stands under the idealization "I can do it again" (Schutz, 1953 (Schutz, /1962b, p. 20), p. 20). This is the assumption that under typically similar circumstances one may act in a typically similar way. Routine knowledge is not consulted as a list of alternatives. It is relied upon until it fails (Schutz & Luckmann, 1973). In Etzrodt's (2024) reconstruction, habitual conduct persists while it remains unproblematic. A disturbance can make alternatives relevant without implying that they were being compared before the disturbance.

An observer who sees repeated coffee drinking cannot infer whether juice never arose as an alternative or was considered and rejected last month. Both histories are compatible with the visible repetition. Equally, a previous decision can be followed by routine execution. A theory explaining the possibility of non-choice needs an accompanying account of the evidence for attributing it to an episode.

4.3. Adequacy

Schutz's postulates of subjective interpretation and adequacy require scientific constructs to retain a relation to actors' meanings (Schutz, 1953 (Schutz, /1962b)). Tuckett (2014) situates these within a wider set of postulates, including compatibility with tested observation. Eberle (2025) traces adequacy historically and argues that Schutz's familiar formulation emphasizes understandability at the expense of agreement with the concrete course of action. His proposed strengthening makes empirical fit from the actor's perspective central again.

This bears directly on accounts of non-participation. It is understandable that a student might reject university because of cost, and also that university might never become a practical alternative. Understandability does not decide between them. A stronger requirement of adequacy calls for evidence that can, and it applies equally to reconstructions of habit and of considered rejection.

5. What exactly has not changed? Four configurations

>What exactly has not changed? Four configurations "The employee did not change jobs" can refer to continued employment, an unsubmitted application, a declined offer, or the absence of a search.

5.1. Action-relative description

These concern different acts. Someone can decline a particular offer while intending to leave the occupation, or continue daily work routinely after a considered decision to remain.

An attribution must therefore specify the focal act or course, the period concerned, and the project within which it is situated. A claim that no decision occurred this week differs from a claim that the alternative was never considered. The following four configurations identify interpretations and combinations that the evidence must distinguish. Warrant depends on discriminating incompatible claims at the same level of action and over the same period, while allowing compatible configurations to coexist. For this purpose the set need not be exhaustive or mutually exclusive.

5.2. Non-deliberative continuation

A practice continues non-deliberatively when no renewed consideration of a practical alternative organizes its maintenance during the specified period. This does not imply ignorance of alternatives, absence of subjective meaning, or absence of a past decision.

A learner may continue language study without asking each day whether to proceed. An earlier decision and a standing project can organize that routine without renewed comparison. Bratman (1987) makes the parallel point for intentions: a settled intention is normally not reopened, and leaving it unreconsidered can be reasonable. Non-deliberative continuation thus includes the routine execution of a standing intention as well as habit in Etzrodt's sense. Evidence supporting this interpretation concerns the present mode of continuation; it need not support the stronger claim that no choice ever occurred.

5.3. Deliberate continuation

Deliberate continuation occurs when maintaining a practice becomes the object of a resolution in relation to an alternative under consideration. The process may be brief or nonverbal. Its defining feature is that keeping the existing course is settled as the course to pursue.

An actor who considers retraining and resolves to remain in the current occupation exemplifies this configuration in that episode. A subsequent period may involve routine implementation of the resolution. Evidence of the earlier decision supports a historical attribution and may explain later conduct without showing repeated deliberation throughout it.

5.4. Intentional abstention

Intentional abstention concerns the projected non-performance of a specified act. An employee may decide not to submit one application while remaining undecided about the occupation. Conversely, resolving to stay need not involve rejecting every possible application individually.

The distinction from deliberate continuation concerns the object of the intention: maintaining a course or withholding a particular act. The configurations can overlap when abstention implements a resolution to continue. They should not be treated as exclusive categories merely because the observer describes the same outcome in different words.

5.5. The deferred project

A deferred project is an adopted project whose execution remains pending beyond an anticipated occasion or period, while the actor retains an orientation toward carrying it out. The alternative has become a practical undertaking, but its execution has not followed as envisaged. An employee intends to begin a search after an assignment, repeatedly moves the starting point as other tasks intervene, and continues to plan a departure. A learner identifies training to undertake, plans to start during a particular term, and postpones doing so while retaining that plan.

Adoption distinguishes the deferred project from an aspiration. "I would like a different job someday" provides weaker grounds than a plan identifying steps, an occasion, or preparations.

No single indicator is decisive, and projects need not be fully articulated. Applications drafted, consultations arranged, and preparations made can support the reconstruction when considered in context.

The evidence for adoption should, where possible, be contemporaneous with the plan. Dated drafts, booked appointments, registrations begun, and notes of consultations record the project before its fate is known. A later statement that one "always meant to" act is useful but secondary (Section 6.3). It is given after the outcome and may be shaped by it. The ordering matters because the configuration most easily overlooked is also the one most easily claimed after the fact.

The temporal qualification is equally important. A plan to apply next year is not deferred merely because no application was made this year. The analyst needs evidence that an anticipated occasion has passed or that implementation has been displaced. If no expected occasion can be reconstructed, the safer description is an adopted project awaiting execution; whether it has been deferred remains open.

Deferral can coexist with intentional abstention. Someone may intend to apply eventually and deliberately refrain this week. The relevant questions are what is being postponed and over which period. A continuing project can also coexist with routine conduct in the present. Its explanatory importance is that current continuity can occur despite an orientation toward change.

Two neighboring cases fall outside the concept. Someone who considers applying but remains undecided has not adopted a project. Someone prevented from applying by a closed system has met a barrier rather than postponed execution. These cases show why the four configurations do not exhaust all relations to an alternative. In Schutz's terms, adoption corresponds to the fiat that turns a project into a purpose (Schutz, 1951 (Schutz, /1962a, p. 67), p. 67). Deferral corresponds to a purpose whose execution has not followed at its anticipated occasion. The deferred project occupies the interval between purposing and executing. Neighboring fields describe the same interval in other ways. Planning theories of intention treat future-directed intentions as partial plans that are filled in over time (Bratman, 1987). Social psychology documents a substantial gap between intentions and behavior (Sheeran, 2002). Economics models repeated postponement as procrastination by people with presentbiased preferences (O'Donoghue & Rabin, 1999). These accounts explain why execution is delayed. The present concern comes before that explanation: how an observer knows that a purpose was adopted and that its anticipated occasion has passed. An inference from nonparticipation alone can overlook the adopted but unexecuted project whether the preferred explanation invokes choice or habit.

6. The two burdens of choice attribution

>The two burdens of choice attribution

6.1. Positive and negative claims

A positive attribution asserts that consideration and resolution occurred in a specified episode. A negative attribution denies that occurrence within a specified scope. They are opposed claims about the event, but failure to support one does not support the other. The evidence can leave the matter unresolved.

This distinction concerns warrant rather than an all-or-nothing view of evidence. A record can favor non-choice without conclusively establishing it. The important question is whether the record would have been more likely under a decision than under its absence, given how observations were produced and retained. A missing trace can favor non-choice when that trace would have been more likely under decision than under non-decision. Its weight depends on the comparison: a procedure that rarely captures private deliberation may provide little discrimination. This is the logic of the likelihood ratio. Process-tracing methodology has formalized it, including the weight of absent evidence (Bennett, 2010; Fairfield & Charman, 2017). This article specifies the configurations and institutional conditions that such likelihood assessments require when the outcome to be explained is unchanged conduct.

The logic is symmetric, but the burdens are not equal in practice. The asymmetry comes from typical values: private decisions often leave few traces, so the probability that a decision is recorded is frequently low. A credible contemporaneous record of comparison and resolution can support a positive attribution. A negative attribution requires attention to the coverage of observation and to the kinds of decision that could have escaped it. Neither burden is met simply by adopting the interpretation that sounds more plausible.

6.2. The observer's counterfactual and independent evidence

Analysts identify actions that could have occurred: applying, moving, enrolling, or retraining.

Such counterfactuals can inform causal explanation. They do not show that the actor considered the action as a practical alternative.

Evidence for deliberate continuation should support the reconstruction of consideration and resolution. Evidence for abstention should connect a particular non-performance to an intention.

Evidence for deferral should document adoption, the anticipated occasion of execution, and the project's persistence. Evidence for non-deliberative continuation should concern how the episode proceeded and what opportunity there was to detect reconsideration.

Evidence is independent of the outcome in the relevant sense when it adds information about these relations rather than redescribing non-performance. This requires neither statistical independence nor a separate source: an unsubmitted draft and an interview about its preparation may jointly support a project reconstruction.

6.3. Retrospective accounts

A respondent's present statement that staying was a choice is evidence for a reconstruction of past conduct. Its weight depends on the relation between the present account and the earlier situation. Mills's (1940) analysis of vocabularies of motive and Scott and Lyman's (1968) account of explanatory statements show why the circumstances of elicitation matter. Recent methodological work makes the same point for interviews. Verbal accounts are weak guides to situated conduct (Jerolmack & Khan, 2014). Accounts of motivation are also exposed to recall error and to biases toward presenting action as reasonable and intentional (Small & Cook, 2023).

Research on non-participation in higher education often works with such accounts. Focus groups with young working-class non-participants, for example, have documented their constructions of the risks, costs, and benefits of university (Archer & Hutchings, 2000). Material of this kind shows how non-participation is accounted for. Its relation to earlier episodes is a further question.

"Why did you not apply?" presents non-application as conduct calling for a reason. A respondent can supply a coherent reason even if the alternative was not previously a practical question. The interview may then elicit an account with the form of a decision rather than recover an earlier decision. Conversely, "I never really decided" may summarize a complicated history that included episodes of deliberation.

Retrospection is neither disqualifying nor self-validating. Asking when an alternative first arose, what steps followed, and what was expected at the time clarifies the temporal target. Questions about reasons are more informative after questions establishing whether and when there was something to decide.

Schutz's distinction between intrinsic relevances and those imposed by situations outside the actor's control helps specify those conditions (Schutz, 1946 (Schutz, /1964, pp. 126-129), pp. 126-129). A school may require a destination statement, an employer a renewal response, and a residence system an application. Such occasions are called imposed choice points here because an institution requires a response concerning an alternative. The term describes an arrangement without presuming that it produces deliberation.

Three evidential levels must be distinguished. Delivery of a form documents an opportunity for exposure. A completed response may support actual exposure, subject to delegation and ritual completion. Evidence of comparison and resolution supports a deliberative attribution. An actor may complete a mandatory form automatically or deliberate without recording the process.

Records are also produced for the institution's purposes rather than the researcher's. Garfinkel's (1967, chap. 6) study of clinic files showed that what looks like poor record keeping follows from what the organization needs its records to do. A destination form records what a school must report, not what a student considered.

Imposed choice points change the prospects of attribution. Compulsory responses increase coverage, and requiring an act to continue makes continuity visible, but neither shows that deliberation occurred. A prospective study outside a formal choice point can also make missing traces informative if its observation process discriminates the competing possibilities.

Defaults expose the difference between organizing conduct and recording it. Where continuation requires renewal, staying leaves a response. Where it is automatic, the same outcome can leave none. Automatic enrollment in retirement saving plans illustrates how rules also affect participation itself. Madrian and Shea (2001) found substantial retention of default contribution rates and allocations and discussed inertia and the interpretation of defaults as advice. Retaining a default, or relying on it as advice, can itself be considered or routine. Section 7.2 examines a published comparison in which such a rule was varied.

An institutional rule can change actual conduct, the visibility of the process, or both. Comparing records across settings without distinguishing these pathways can produce apparent differences in choosing even where actors' projective relations are similar. Independent reconstruction is needed to assess whether a changed rule altered the relation, the record, or their connection.

6.5. Adequacy and evidential assessment

An employee might remain for security, from routine, or while intending to leave, and all three are understandable. Adequacy in Eberle's (2025) stronger sense requires deciding which fits the episode, and that is an empirical task.

For attribution, a study should state its focal act, temporal scope, reconstructed relation to the alternative, and evidential basis. It should also name the competing interpretations and what would distinguish them. This permits qualified conclusions: an adopted project may be well supported while the reason for its non-execution remains uncertain.

6.6. Premises and propositions

P1 to P3 are premises drawn from action theory. They state what the argument assumes rather than what it predicts. P4 to P6 are the propositions proper. P4 states the condition for warrant, and P5 connects missing evidence to observation and institutional rules. P6 develops conditional hypotheses for empirical investigation.

P1 (underdetermination; premise).

An unchanged outcome, considered alone, leaves the actor's relation to an alternative undetermined. It is compatible with non-deliberative continuation, deliberate continuation, intentional abstention, and a deferred project, among other configurations.

P2 (the observer's alternatives; premise).

Identifying an objectively available alternative does not show that the actor considered it as a practical possibility in the target episode.

P3 (separate burdens; premise).

Failure to establish that a decision occurred does not by itself establish that none occurred, and failure to establish that none occurred does not by itself establish that one did. Evidence may nevertheless favor one attribution to a degree that depends on the observation process and the competing interpretations.

P4 (warrant for attribution).

An attribution gains warrant through evidence about the focal act, the target period, and the actor's projective relation to the alternative that adds information beyond the unchanged outcome. The strength of the attribution depends on the evidence's coverage, credibility, and capacity to discriminate relevant alternatives.

P5 (observability and missing traces).

The absence of a relevant trace favors one configuration over another when, under the specified observation process, that trace would have been more likely under the second. In the binary case, absence favors non-choice when the trace would have The two expectations concern different outcomes, the emergence of consideration and the execution of a project, and either can occur without the other.

Testing P6 requires establishing prior configurations through evidence such as plans, consultations, or prospective observation, without defining them by the response they are used to predict. Baseline measurement carries a risk of reactivity, since asking people about their intentions can change their later behavior (Morwitz et al., 1993). A question about plans may make an alternative problematic before any intervention. Estimating this effect requires groups without baseline measurement, as in a four-group design that crosses measurement with treatment (Solomon, 1949). Classifying configurations from records produced before the study avoids the problem.

Onset of consideration, resolution, and enactment should be recorded as separate outcomes. The comparisons concern treatment effects within prior configurations, with randomized assignment where feasible. Some results would count against the expectations. One is a precise estimate showing no increase in consideration, for example one whose interval excludes the smallest effect judged meaningful. Another is no larger execution effect for deferred projects. An inconclusive result would leave the expectation open. Classification uncertainty belongs in that assessment.

These expectations draw on accounts of disrupted routine and variable rationality (Etzrodt, 2024; Kroneberg, 2014). Research on implementation intentions shows that specifying when and where to act raises enactment among people who already intend to act (Gollwitzer, 1999). That supports execution support for adopted projects, but it does not test the comparison in P6 between deferred projects and deliberate rejection.

7. Applications and boundaries

>Applications and boundaries

7.1. Educational non-participation reconsidered

Consider a schematic example, not an empirical finding. An administrative record shows that a student submitted no university application during the final school year. Eligibility data show that application was possible.

Neither fact identifies the student's projective relation to applying. Aggregate patterns may support broader explanations, but this episode remains compatible with several configurations. Suppose a contemporaneous consultation note records that the student planned to apply by a given date, and a dated draft shows preparation. The date passes without submission. This supports adoption of a project more strongly than the administrative outcome alone. It does not yet distinguish postponement, abandonment, a changed decision, or an external obstacle. Evidence that the student rescheduled the application and continued preparing would favor deferral. Evidence of a subsequent rejection of university would support a transition away from the project. If the anticipated date has not yet arrived, non-submission does not yet indicate deferral.

A later statement that the student "always intended to apply" can contribute to this reconstruction.

Its relation to the dated materials and the interview situation should be examined. Likewise, a statement that university was "not for people like us" could describe an earlier horizon, a later explanation, or both. Its meaning cannot be fixed from its wording alone.

The same logic applies to institutional records. A mandatory destination form can document a response to an institutionally presented question. Whether it captures deliberation depends on how the question was encountered and answered. A missing reference to university in a record designed only to list destinations carries different weight from its absence in a detailed prospective record of options discussed. Neither should be treated as a transparent window onto the student's projects.

A study could document initial plans and reasons, then compare salience prompts, execution assistance, and a control condition. P6 does not predict that information leaves decided students unchanged: information about funding may alter a rejection grounded in cost. The approach complements rational action and habit-based explanation by stating the evidence needed when either attributes a concrete process. A study explaining enrollment through fees or eligibility without attributing an episode of decision has a different task. The framework reads this comparison in three steps. First, the rule changed conduct: more employees enrolled. Second, it changed the record. Under standard enrollment, a non-participant at three months left no distinguishing trace. A considered refusal, a plan to enroll later, and never attending to the plan looked the same. Under active decision, non-participation was meant to take the form of a stated choice, though not returning the form carried no formal penalty. Third, the slow catch-up under standard enrollment, together with the authors' explanation, points to many early non-participants who meant to enroll but kept moving the occasion. That is close to what this article calls a deferred project, and the imposed choice point supplied the occasion their own plans kept postponing.

7.2. A worked case: defaults and active decisions

Two misreadings follow if records are taken as direct measures of choosing. A comparison of the two cohorts' files would show many more decisions under the active regime. Part of that difference is the rule producing records, not employees becoming more deliberative. Conversely, reading non-participation at three months under standard enrollment as revealed rejection of saving would attribute rejection to people many of whom later enrolled. The study does not observe individual projects, so the configurations remain an interpretation of aggregate patterns.

One aggregate result is compatible with different mixtures of configurations, and a rule can change both the mixture and its visibility. Consent rates for organ donation, which differ sharply between opt-in and opt-out countries, show a related pattern (Johnson & Goldstein, 2003).

7.3. A second illustration: pronunciation

Four learners undertake no pronunciation training during a term. One follows an established study routine without reconsidering training during that period. Another compares training with other uses of time and resolves to continue the current routine. A third declines one course while leaving broader plans open. A fourth has adopted a plan to attend training, misses the intended starting occasion, and continues to prepare for a later one.

These are configurations, not permanent types. The first learner may have considered training previously, and the fourth's postponement may include abstention from a particular session. An account must keep the same focal act and period when weighing the interpretations.

The example also separates the social value of a practice from the actor's project. A prestigious pronunciation may be rewarded in a linguistic market. That reward does not mean that training became a practical undertaking for a given learner, and lack of training does not indicate indifference. Evidence about routines, comparisons, course refusals, and prior plans is needed to specify the relation.

7.4. Power, alternatives, and the record of agency

Projects are socially situated. Institutions, resources, and expectations shape which alternatives are available, which are known, and which become practical questions. Bachrach and Baratz's (1963) analysis of agenda exclusion directs attention to arrangements that can prevent possibilities from becoming matters for decision. Demonstrating such a mechanism requires evidence about how exclusion operates and how actors encounter it.

A constrained position does not by itself indicate non-deliberation. People can consider and reject alternatives under severe constraints or adopt projects they cannot execute, and apparent agreement can reflect the exclusion of alternatives from consideration. Structural and projective explanations can be connected without inferring the latter from the former.

The framework adds a second institutional question: whose orientations become visible? Groups subject to explicit renewal or destination requirements may leave more responses than groups whose participation continues automatically. They may then appear more deliberative even when the difference lies in the production of records. Conversely, an arrangement that restricts available routes can still demand an explicit response, giving constrained conduct the documentary appearance of selection. These are conditional implications of P5; the pension case in Section 7.2 shows the pattern in one setting.

Institutional procedures thus belong inside an explanation of choice attribution. They organize the alternatives, the occasion for considering them, and what is documented. Treating the resulting record as a direct measure of agency merges these effects. The remedy is to reconstruct their connection for the same focal act and period across groups.

7.5. Scope of the argument

The logic of negative evidence is general, and Bayesian process tracing supplies it. This article adds two things for cases of unchanged conduct: the configurations over which that logic must run, and the institutional conditions that set the likelihoods. Destination forms, renewal requirements, and defaults make similar outcomes differently visible, and the deferred project shows that evidence of consideration is not evidence of rejection. Attributions can differ in scope and strength. A dated plan may support adoption while its later status remains uncertain. A recorded refusal may support abstention from one offer without implying a commitment to stay.

The configurations do not require unique behavioral signatures. Even where later responses coincide, rejection and an adopted but unexecuted project remain different explanations of the earlier episode. An intervention can supply evidence about that difference, but it can also change the relation it investigates, so prior reconstruction and later response must be assessed together.
