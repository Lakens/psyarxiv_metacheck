Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard), also Internal Review Board - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term); not IEC followed by a number, which is a technical standard - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee/Commission, in the singular or plural - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee, Bioethics Committee/Commission, Ethikkommission - Ethical Review Authority/Unit/Office/Agency - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Human Research Protection Office/Program, Office for Human Subjects Protection - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee(s) on/for (Medical and Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt - Ethics review that was not needed: Ethics review was not required, did not require further ethical review, exempt from formal human research ethics review, no new institutional ethics review

A template line the authors have not filled in (“[Add ethics approval … here.]”) is not a statement.

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

When a statement is expected

The module sorts each paper by where its data came from (the internal .data_source(); the signs and how the rule was tested are described in R/data-source.R): - own: the authors collected data from people or animals, for this paper, in an earlier study of their own, or (a protocol) will collect them; - existing_records: records about individual people that the authors obtained for this study, such as hospital records or a cohort; - existing_unclear: existing data about people from a source the module could not identify; - open_resource: an open resource any researcher can use, such as UK Biobank or NHANES; - none: no data about people or animals (a review, simulation, commentary, theory, tutorial, coding of published papers, or figures about groups); - uncertain: the signs were missing or pointed both ways; - no_text: the paper has no text.

A statement is expected for own, existing_records and existing_unclear. A paper without a statement in these classes, or in uncertain, is flagged. The module reads the text, and the results of power, stat_p_exact, stat_check, repo_check, data_check and prereg_check when they ran before it (run it after them); without stat_p_exact, it finds the p-values in the text.

On 199 PsyArXiv and medRxiv preprints labelled after the rule was fixed, the module decided right whether a statement is needed for 86.9% (the earlier live-data search: 83.4%). Its main errors: other people’s studies described in the words of a new study, records and specimens described only as counts, and open resources the paper does not call open.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download information from other preregistration sites, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 685 tests with 34 instances of inconsistent reporting, Statcheck correctly detected 34 of them, and incorrectly identified 26. Therefore 0% of true instances were missed, and 43% of detections were false positives. See https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/ for more details of the validation.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on 226 Psychological Science papers with 5,019 hand-coded p-values, 438 of them imprecise (scienceverse/validation, branch stat_p_exact). Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). Of the misses, 13 are starred table notes (“*p < .05”) that the module leaves out on purpose and 11 are p-values it did not find; of the wrong reports, 20 are p-values the ground truth does not contain (such as “ps < .05”) and 6 use ≤ or ≥. How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on three hand-coded sets of papers (scienceverse/validation: 249 Psychological Science, 250 Economics and 250 MDPI papers). Of 1,783 non-significant p-values in the ground truth, version 0.4.4.3 found 1,719 (3.6% missed), and 94.8% of its 1,814 detections were in the ground truth. Some differences come from the ground truth: it lacks p-values with a correction name (“p Bonf = .061”), and counts partial eta squared written with its subscript after it (“η 2 p = .05”), “p = .05”, and some table notes and figure legends (“† p < .10, *p < .05”), which this module leaves out, as non-significant p-values. How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. Abstract

In Western cultures, jealousy is felt when a valued relationship is feared to be lost to a rival. The Middle Eastern near-cognate emotion gheirat calls our attention to a broader range of relational boundary violations (RBVs) which are worthy of study as social emotion appraisals. This qualitative study takes a step back from specific emotion words to examine reactions to RBVs in European dignity and honour cultures, including the innovation of situations that would elicit gheirat but not jealousy. Semistructured interviews were conducted in the UK (N = 24), Spain (N = 21), and Greece (N = 24), and analysed using hybrid inductive-deductive thematic analysis. Results revealed a shared tripartite structure across all three samples. Relational rivalry and harm elicited identical experiences of jealousy and protectiveness. However, relational intrusion showed cultural differences, producing an unlabelled and elusive experience among British participants, discomfort among Spanish/Greek women, and territoriality among Spanish/Greek men. Meta-belief themes (emotion regulation, reputational concerns, gendered norms, and generalised mistrust) showed greater cultural variability than phenomenological themes, aligned with gheirat's honour-related, moralised, and gendered characteristics. Together, these experiential and interpretive patterns inform our understanding of gender-based relational violence in European contexts, beyond the narrow label "jealousy".

Beyond Mere Jealousy: The Experience of Relational Boundary Violations in Three European Cultures

Emotions play a foundational role in close relationship dynamics (Finkel et al., 2017).

Specifically, one kind of emotion is felt when relational boundaries are overstepped or violated by the relational other, a third person, or both. The most prominent emotion associated with relational boundary violations (RBVs) in Western contexts is jealousy, which is felt when a valued relationship is feared to be lost to a rival (Parrott & Smith, 1993). But emotions are conceptualised differently across languages and cultures (Shweder et al., 2008), often resulting in non-exact or absent equivalents in translation (Russell, 1991).

One cultural analogue of jealousy found in Middle Eastern societies, gheirat, has recently sparked interest among scholars (e.g., Atari et al., 2017 Atari et al., , 2020 Atari et al., , 2021;; Razavi et al., 2023). Gheirat is felt in response to an actual or potential intrusion by an unentitled third party into relational space. It is felt regardless of whether the intrusion is caused by the third party's actions or the relational other's own actions, and regardless of whether the relational other resists, welcomes, or is indifferent to the intrusion (Razavi et al., 2023). This concern, then, is broader than Western jealousy.

Specifically, Western accounts of jealousy typically centre on a relational other's [potential] involvement with a rival (White & Mullen, 1989), rather than situations where a third party approaches an uninterested close other (romantic or non-romantic), a phenomenon that remains understudied (Vera-Gray, 2016).

While previous research on gheirat has involved Iranian participants, we intentionally set out to reverse the usual current of ideas in cross-cultural psychology. Most often, concepts prevalent in contemporary Western culture are studied for their applicability to other cultures. Instead, we take inspiration from the broader elicitors of gheirat to investigate whether and how respondents from European societies handle RBVs in general. Given gheirat's documented connection to honourrelated cultural beliefs (Razavi et al., 2023), we also intentionally compared European societies established as high and low in ethics of honour (Rodriguez Mosquera et al., 2002; Guerra et al., 2013).

This approach offers promising contributions to theories of emotion and culture (Mesquita & Frijda, 1992; Shweder & Haidt, 2000; Mesquita et al., 2016), to practices of epistemic diversity in cultural studies (Anjum & Aziz, 2024; Kitayama & Salvador, 2024), and to the wider understanding of social issues impacted by relationship attitudes, such as intimate partner violence (IPV) (Vandello & Cohen, 2003 , 2008). Studying the English concept of "jealousy" as a dominant form across cultures, without considering other cultural analogues, limits our understanding not only of global-majority cultures but also of Western ones (Adams et al., 2015). Studying a given emotion's source context beyond its culture of origin could furthermore be a promising technique for developing new theoretical accounts.

Our research thus uses a culturally well-developed concept from Middle Eastern traditions to study relevant reactions among Westerners who may not have such an emotion discourse around the full scope of RBVs. The closest analogue we could find in the emotion literature comes from Niiya et al. (2006), who compared US and Japanese participants' reactions to an eliciting situation associated with the Japanese emotion amae. Inspired by this study and by other cross-cultural work in which an emotion term from one culture's lexicon is absent from another (e.g., Breugelmans & Poortinga, 2006), this research presented people from European cultures with the eliciting situations themselves, without assuming specific emotion words. Within Europe, we also contrast the United Kingdom with two Southern European countries (Spain and Greece), more frequently described as honour-based cultures (Rodriguez Mosquera et al., 2002; Guerra et al., 2013). Throughout, we will compare these findings to previous studies that focused on Iranian understanding of RBVs and gheirat.

The Western Conceptualisation of Jealousy: Core Features and Limitations

Most theoretical accounts of jealousy in the West seem to agree on two main points: Firstly, jealousy is elicited by a threat to a valued relationship, coming from perceived involvement of the relational other with a rival (Buss, 2000; Harris & Darby, 2010; Hupka, 1984; Parrott, 1991; Salovey & Rodin, 1986; White, 1981; White & Mullen, 1989). This involvement could be real or imagined (White, 1981), and while most research on jealousy has focused on romantic or sexual relationships, jealousy in friendships and families also exists (e.g., Hart & Legerstee, 2010; Toohey, 2014). Secondly, jealousy is generally considered to be negative: unpleasant to experience, harmful to relationships, and detrimental to mental well-being. Correspondingly, jealousy co-occurs with negative states such as fear, anxiety, sadness, and anger (Sharpsteen, 1991; Hupka, 1984). In its trait form, jealousy is linked to low self-esteem and insecurity (e.g., Salovey & Rodin, 1984; Buunk, 1997; Bringle, 1981), to maladaptive cognitions such as suspicion and rumination (e.g., Mullen, 1991) and to relationship problems such as conflict, control, and aggression (e.g., Buss, 2000; Bringle, 1991).

The current English-language definition of jealousy has narrowed over time. Jealousy historically has carried two distinct meanings in Western culture. One was tied to relational rivalry, and another, connected to the kindred word zeal, was tied to the protection of honour, power, and other valued social assets independent of any rival (Stearns, 2010). This second protective sense has diminished as honour-based values gave way to individualism in the West (Nye, 1993). Consequently, contemporary uses of "jealousy" words in European languages may leave less conceptual space for situations in which a third party intrudes upon a valued relationship, unprovoked by the relational other.

This conceptual detachment from protectiveness has led to a shift in how the emotion is experienced and evaluated: a morally valued emotion gradually became unpleasant and shameful (Clanton, 1989). The social stigma of jealousy in Western contexts intensified alongside concern for women's rights, as controlling and violent behaviour of men against their female partners became problematic. Contemporary psychological research reinforces this negative framing, frequently associating jealousy with insecurity (e.g., Mullen & Martin, 1994; Karakurt, 2012; Zandbergen & Brown, 2015) and with maladaptive or even pathological behaviour (e.g., Parker & Barrett, 1997; Seeman, 2016; Costa et al., 2015; Singh et al., 2017). This negative framing is also reflected in established measures of jealousy, with most subscales focusing on maladaptive dimensions (e.g., the Multidimensional Jealousy Scale; Pfeiffer & Wong, 1989). Examining responses to RBVs more broadly, including protectiveness, may help clarify how far this negative shift can extend beyond mere jealousy.

Gheirat: Empirical Perspectives

As an emotion, gheirat resembles Western jealousy in some ways. First, it also involves a subject (self), an object (the valued person), and a third person who crosses a relational boundary.

This is implied by the term itself, as gheir means "the other" or "others" (Mosawi, 2019). Secondly, gheirat is also experienced in relational threat from the close person's involvement with a third party, although it is not limited to this elicitor (Bakhtiar, 2015; Razavi et al., 2023). Thirdly, the emotional experiences associated with gheirat encompass multiple affective clusters overlapping those experienced during jealousy, such as anger, fear, and anxiety (Razavi et al., 2023; Bakhtiar, 2015).

Lastly, gheirat is also believed to have a negative and pathological side, though not generally considered negative (Razavi et al., 2023).

However, gheirat differs from jealousy in its relational scope and eliciting conditions. Whilst jealousy is typically elicited by a threat to a relationship arising from the close other's behaviour, gheirat goes beyond jealousy-eliciting situations. Razavi et al. (2023) identified three main elicitors of gheirat (Figure 1). The prototype experience was elicited by a third person overstepping the relational boundary. Two less frequently associated variants were elicited by a perception of harm or insult to the close person, and by the close person violating the relational boundary. The latter variant would also include jealousy. The prototype experience intersected with the two variants, with most situations containing two appraisals.

Due to its inherent protectiveness, gheirat has also acquired a morally positive character at odds with the negative view of jealousy today in the West (Razavi et al., 2023). Empirical evidence for this moral dimension is provided by Atari et al. Gheirat is also widely believed to be an exclusively masculine experience (Razavi et al., 2023).

However, gender exclusivity was not supported by empirical evidence from Iran: women reported experiencing those feelings, even as they concealed their expression to meet gender norms (Razavi et al., 2023). Conversely, male participants viewed gheirat as an automatic and uncontrollable emotion (Razavi et al., 2023), suggesting that gendered moralisation may shape how individuals understand their capacity to regulate it. As gheirat forms an important part of men's moral character, it may be purposefully expressed or exaggerated to save face or protect one's reputation, particularly within an honour-based cultural logic in which men are expected to protect their dependents (Mosawi, 2019; Razavi et al., 2023).

The Present Study

Building on the elicitors of gheirat proposed by Razavi et al. (2023), this study took an exploratory approach to examine experiences of RBVs in individuals from three different European countries which do not have such a ready emotion vocabulary to express a single, positively valued feeling of protectiveness. Among our sites, the UK can be taken as a non-honour (dignity) culture, and Spain and Greece as honour cultures. Using a qualitative methodology and semi-structured interviews, we sought a deeper understanding of people's thoughts, feelings, and actions when they perceived either a close person or a third party overstepping their relational boundaries during an interaction.

Our main aims were to find out: first, whether a broader relational protection framing shows a different phenomenology than reporting only in terms of jealousy would unearth; and second, whether Mediterranean cultures of honour would show more gheirat-like reactions, especially in valorising protective concerns, than the UK culture of dignity would. We also sought to understand whether, and how the cultural logic of honour and reputation norms might influence people's experiences of RBVs, and their regulation of affective experiences and potential tendencies towards violent behaviour.

Methods

To examine a little-studied situation (RBV) in a population likely to lack ready-made vocabulary and constructs to discuss it, we chose a qualitative methodology, specifically Thematic Analysis (Braun & Clarke, 2006). Furthermore, this study was theoretically informed by findings from previous research on gheirat in Iranian samples (Razavi et al., 2023); therefore, a hybrid inductivedeductive thematic analysis was adopted (see Data analysis).

Reflexivity is a crucial component of qualitative research (Berger, 2015; Braun & Clarke, 2019). Here, we describe the cultural standpoints of the people involved in interpretive work (see Credit statement for a complete enumeration of these people). The first author comes from an Iranian cultural background with some experience living in English-speaking countries. Based on the polarised attitude towards gheirat in Iran (valued by some and condemned by others), as confirmed in earlier research, the first author explicitly adopted an objective standpoint, viewing it as an emotion with both healthy and unhealthy expressions. This cultural background sometimes led to emotional experiences (such as surprise or frustration) during data collection and analysis. For example, the first author was initially unaware of the extent of existing reluctance to admit to feelings of jealousy in the UK due to its social stigma. This realisation developed as data collection proceeded. Furthermore, the first author remained aware that their previous findings from Iranian samples (Razavi et al., 2023) would shape their frame of reference when analysing European accounts. This shaping was not resisted but rather accepted with mindful flexibility in response to potential variations. Socialised in the same culture as their respective samples, the site collaborators for Spain and Greece had firsthand experience and awareness of the sensitive and intimate nature of such experiences in their own cultures, which facilitated a deeper understanding of the participants' experiences and narratives, and helped establish a closer connection during the interviews.

To maintain reflexivity and address potential bias, all authors responsible for data collection and analysis practised bracketing and judgment suspension by writing down their assumptions and cultural beliefs before each interview and each attempt at analysis. Spanish collaborators attempted to enhance rigour through critical reviews of the interviews and peer discussions. The first author also kept a reflexive journal of their thought processes and conceptions about the data throughout the project from the beginning of data collection to the end of data analysis.

To minimise any potential power imbalance, extra care was taken to build rapport during the initial 5 minutes of the interview and to verbally reiterate ethical principles and regulations before each interview, ensuring that participants were fully aware of their rights, even if they had not carefully read the study information sheet.

Participants

Participants were 69 men and women from the UK (n = 24), Spain (n = 21), and Greece (n = 24), recruited via convenience and snowball sampling. Online interviews were conducted during January-February 2024 (UK), October-November 2024 (Spain), and October 2024-June 2025 (Greece). Undergraduate student samples were recruited from psychology departments at universities in the UK, Spain, and Greece via an advert and compensated with course credits for their participation. The UK participants were all White British, as we preferred an ethnically homogeneous sample for a qualitative inquiry. Age and gender characteristics are shown in Table 1. Guidelines by Guest et al. (2006) suggest that saturation is usually achieved after 12 interviews, and other sources suggest a range of 9-17 for relatively homogeneous qualitative samples (Hennink & Kaiser, 2022). Under the assumption that male and female participants might have different experiences of RBVs, an initial number of n = 24 per sample (12 male and 12 female) was determined before recruitment. This was later modified due to resource limitations and saturation across all three sites, resulting in the retention of a minimum of 8 interviews per gender group. The overall evaluation of the data was made with close reference to the study itself, rather than to decontextualised numerical guidelines for sample size (Vasileiou et al., 2018).

Materials and Procedure

After obtaining informed consent and demographic information via an online Qualtrics survey, participants completed a semi-structured online interview, which was recorded on Microsoft Teams. Interviews lasted between 20 and 90 minutes. The interview schedule (Appendix A) was designed to gather information on different dimensions of experience (affective, cognitive, behavioural), self-evaluation, social perception, norm compliance, and relational outcomes.

During the interview, participants were asked to recall and describe a personal experience of RBV in as much detail as possible. Specifically, they were asked to think of a time when someone close to them interacted with a third person, and they felt that a boundary was overstepped by one or both parties. An illustration of possible RBV scenarios was shown to the interviewees to facilitate a clear understanding of the scenario (Figure 2). Interviewees were then prompted to describe their feelings, thoughts, actions, and evaluations. At the end of the interview, participants were debriefed.

Data analysis

Data transcripts were analysed according to a recent revision of well-known guidelines for thematic analysis (Braun & Clarke, 2019), which specified choices to be made. In this study, themes were defined as patterns of meaning that articulate participants' experiences of RBVs. The analysis adopted a hybrid inductive-deductive approach. "Key" themes were determined based on their relevance to the research questions, with attention to previous findings from studies of Iranian samples (Razavi et al., 2023), while remaining inductively flexible to explore modifications and nuances arising from linguistic differences and cultural orientations (e.g., honour).

Even though some precedence was given to what the interviewees explicitly articulated, themes were predominantly latent rather than semantic, and their development required interpretation for several reasons. Firstly, the episodic and complex nature of emotions makes it hard for participants to readily and clearly understand what they were experiencing. Secondly, at times, even though the interviewees knew how they felt, they did not have the right words to describe it.

Thirdly, some feelings related to RBVs (such as jealousy) are stigmatised in Western cultures; therefore, participants were likely to deny such feelings. In line with the nature of the themes, a middle stance was taken on epistemology. Although exploring the interviewees' experiences began from a realist point of view, which reflected their true feelings, the analysis would lean toward constructionism when considering factors that alter and shape those reported accounts, potentially distancing them from the participants' experiences.

After initial coding and data familiarisation, eight interviews (four from Spain and four from Greece) were excluded from the final analysis as irrelevant to the research questions. These interviews either included accounts which did not refer to RBVs or were too vague to be coded. The final analytic sample, therefore, comprised 61 interviews: 24 from the UK, 17 from Spain, and 20 from Greece.

Discussion

The Shared Phenomenology of RBVs: Jealousy and Protectiveness

The RBV experience in European samples generally showed a tripartite structure with two of the three themes being similar across all samples and gender groups at all three subtheme levels (elicitors, feelings, and thoughts). All participants experienced jealousy in response to relational rivalry, mainly felt as sadness, followed by thoughts of low self-worth, and experienced protectiveness in response to harm, primarily in the form of anger, followed by thoughts of responsibility. These findings suggest that when relational threats are unambiguous, whether harm to a close person or the loss of that person to a rival, emotional appraisals, and therefore experiences, tend to converge across cultures (Frijda, 1993; Mesquita & Frijda, 1992). In contrast, relational intrusion, where neither the close person nor an evident harm defines the violation, is more ambiguous and appears more open to culturally shaped construal (Mesquita et al., 2016), as discussed below.

Cultural moderation of relational intrusion

Experiences elicited by relational intrusion (theme 2) demonstrated two main cultural differences: firstly, there were differences between low honour (i.e. British) and high honour (i.e. Spanish and Greek) female participants in feelings and thoughts. Secondly, unlike the British sample, there were pronounced gender differences in the high-honour samples, with male participants experiencing territoriality rather than the discomfort reported by females.

Feminine possessiveness in honour-oriented samples

While British participants struggled to label their experience of relational intrusion, female Spanish/Greek participants referred to it as discomfort. Furthermore, British participants reported disgust and a desire to distance themselves and their loved one from the situation and the third person, while Spanish/Greek female participants reported anger, with a stronger tendency to approach than to avoid. This could be explained by cultural dynamics in high-honour populations where violations of relational norms are considered "harmful" because of their effects on people's reputations (Cohen & Nisbett, 1994; Rodriguez Mosquera et al., 2008). In other words, people are more likely to construe this type of RBV as harmful rather than undesirable but avoidable, thereby eliciting anger, which is more strongly associated with harm than disgust is (Gutierrez & Giner-Sorolla, 2007; Russell & Giner-Sorolla, 2011; Giner-Sorolla et al., 2012).

Possessive thoughts also varied between samples. British participants expressed possessiveness implicitly, disguising it as protectiveness, while female Spanish/Greek participants explicitly referred to their partner as their possession. This could be due to the normalisation of possessiveness in cultures of honour, where one's partner may embody one's sense of worth. In lowhonour or "dignity" cultures, people are not normatively viewed as extensions of the self (Nisbett & Cohen, 1996; Vandello & Cohen, 2003). These findings are also consistent with research suggesting that feminine honour norms function as a form of ideological mate guarding, making possessive attitudes toward romantic partners more socially acceptable and therefore more openly expressed in honour-oriented cultures (Kupfer & Gul, 2023). Furthermore, evidence suggests that, as Western attitudes toward jealousy have changed historically, the expression of jealousy and possessiveness has become more stigmatised in societies with a lower honour orientation, leading people there to suppress or conceal these feelings (Stearns, 1989). British men have also been reported to cope with jealousy via denial and avoidance (Mullen & Martin, 1994).

Masculine territoriality in honour-oriented samples

Compared with the female participants, the differences between British and Spanish/Greek male participants were even more pronounced. Unlike British participants and female Spanish/Greek participants, who described two distinct but intersecting experiences in response to relational intrusion and harm, Spanish/Greek male participants reported territoriality nested within protectiveness. This experience was a direct response to perceived harassment, and the intrusion was consistently appraised as harmful. It elicited markedly more intense anger, described as hatred, and was accompanied by thoughts of vigilant guarding. Following constructivist (e.g., Mesquita et al., 2016) and social-functional (e.g., Keltner & Haidt, 1999 , 2001) accounts of emotion, this territorial hatred may have developed as a protective, compensatory response in social environments where harassment carried high reputational costs (Cohen & Nisbett, 1994; Rodriguez Mosquera et al., 2008), women had limited means to retaliate, and legal systems offered little formal recourse or deterrent. These conditions may also explain why female Spanish/Greek participants reported a generalised mistrust of people, especially men (theme 7).

Cultural Differences in Meta-Beliefs About RBVs

Compared with phenomenological themes, meta-belief themes varied more across cultures.

This pattern aligns with the view that cultural differences are larger at the levels of beliefs, attributions, and opinions than in the experience, structure, and organisation of emotions (Matsumoto, 2006; Matsumoto et al., 2007).

Emotion regulation during RBVs

British participants maintained firm control over their emotional reactions to RBVs, whereas Spanish/Greek participants showed two distinct regulatory styles. Female participants controlled their reactions through suppression and self-censorship, which appeared to be environmentally driven, stemming from fear of the consequences of expression or hopelessness about its effectiveness rather than a fully autonomous choice. In contrast, male participants demonstrated precarious emotion regulation, where violence was typically considered the next best option or the perceived societal norm. These results could be explained by emphasised gender roles in cultures of honour, where double standards are set by prescribed norms of emotional expression (Uskul et al., 2023; Ford & Mauss, 2015). Male aggression is legitimised or accepted in social situations, whereas female violence would carry higher social costs or is deemed less useful. This leads to men being more uninhibited in expressing aggression, while making women more cautious and hesitant to do so. This finding is supported by evidence that gender-based violence is prevalent in societies high in honour orientation (Alan Dikmen & Ilknur Munevver, 2020; Gul & Schuster, 2020; Brown et al., 2018).

The precarious male regulation also reflects previous findings: high-honour participants favoured withdrawal from a rude affront but confrontation of a false accusation (Cross et al., 2013), were slower to respond to minor annoyances, but were more aggressive once they did (Cohen et al., 1999) and remained polite and constructive in conflicts that did not involve an insult (Harinck et al., 2013; Shafa et al., 2015).

Reputation and public evaluation of reactions to RBVs

British participants understood and described their emotional reactions to RBVs as intrinsic experiences, without considering how they might affect their reputations. For many participants, reputation was not within their conscious awareness. Some found the question strange, while others replied that they would not care about other people's judgments. Conversely, Spanish/Greek participants consistently remained self-conscious of how their expressions might elicit societal approval or disapproval. Even when participants considered themselves iconoclasts who did not care what others thought, they were still acutely aware of how others would view their behaviour. This finding supports other evidence of the importance of reputation concerns and reputation management in honour cultures (Travaglino et al., 2024; Novin & Oyserman, 2016).

Gendered social reality

Spanish/Greek participants frequently categorised men and women as different in characteristics, thoughts, feelings, behaviour, and violent tendencies. Gender was considered a determining factor in whether a violation was likely to occur or to be prevented. Specifically, men were seen as both more likely to violate a boundary and more likely to prevent others from doing so by their mere presence. In contrast, British participants did not refer to gendered beliefs or gendered descriptions of individuals. Events were attributed either to people's personalities or to the specific situation, not to gender. This finding aligns with the view that social reality is more gendered in honour cultures, due to stricter gender norms and polarised gender roles (Nisbett & Cohen, 1996; Vandello & Cohen, 2003).

Generalised mistrust

British female participants viewed their experiences as isolated incidents that did not affect their attitude toward people in general. Compared to Spanish/Greek accounts, RBVs were infrequent and less severe, so they did not become a preoccupying concern. By contrast, several Spanish/Greek female participants mentioned that through experience, they have learned that they cannot trust people in general, whether women making advances toward their loved ones or men harassing their friends. They indicated that people, even those they know, are unpredictable and potentially dangerous. This generalised feeling of unsafety went hand in hand with a perception that RBVs, and especially sexual harassment, are frequent. Other research also shows that honour ideology is associated with a higher chance of men engaging in violent and sexually coercive behaviour towards women (Brown et al., 2018).

European RBVs and Middle Eastern Gheirat: Cross-Cultural Synthesis

Similarities across all cultures

The thematic structure of RBV experiences in European samples resembled the model in Figure 1, which was based on gheirat experiences in Iranian samples (Razavi et al., 2023). This resemblance should be interpreted with caution, since the conceptualisation and investigation of RBVs in the present study were informed by this earlier research. Similar to Iranian samples, all three European samples shared experiences involving all three eliciting conditions and their overlapping scenarios. Although the small qualitative sample precludes population-level inferences about scenario choice, Spanish and Greek participants described RBVs elicited by relational intrusion and harm more often than those elicited by relational rivalry, whereas British participants described all three elicitors about equally often (Table 2).

Beyond the elicitation structure, the affective and cognitive components in our European samples resembled those reported in previous research on Iranian samples for the two variants of gheirat. Razavi et al. (2023) found that, in Iranian samples, violation by the close person, which corresponds to the relational rivalry elicitor in our study, was associated with greater self-blame (e.g., embarrassment) and social fear (e.g., loneliness) components. This is consistent with our findings, in which participants mostly associated relational rivalry with sadness, a core affective component of loneliness (Yanguas et al., 2018) that is highly associated with depressive affect (Luo & Shao, 2023; Mansfield et al., 2021). Razavi et al. (2023) also linked this variant to the cognitive appraisal components of insecurity and low self-worth. The latter is consistent with the low self-worth subtheme identified in European accounts.

Consistent with Razavi et al. (2023), harm-elicited experiences in our study involved a stronger sense of protectiveness and responsibility in European participants. Furthermore, for harmrelated experiences in the Iranian samples, the hostility component included seven emotion items (angry, loathing-hateful, hostile, disgusted, upset, excited, and irritable), which overlap with the anger reported in European samples.

Similarities across honour cultures

Gender differences in the Spanish/Greek samples paralleled those found previously in Iranian samples. In the Spanish/Greek samples, men's accounts of relational intrusion centred on harassment, which they experienced as harm and territoriality, while women's accounts were broader, covering relational intrusion which sometimes co-occurred with relational rivalry and with harm. In Iranian samples, Razavi et al. (2023) found similar gender differences. A larger proportion of men reported experiences involving harm and a third-person boundary violation, whereas a higher proportion of women described scenarios in which a close person violated a boundary. Iranian men were also more likely to evaluate the elicitor of gheirat as improper and immoral, whereas Iranian women were more likely to consider the event as having a negative effect on their relationship.

Meta-beliefs about RBVs in the Spanish/Greek data also resembled those reported in previous research on Iranian samples. Regarding emotion regulation, Razavi et al. (2023) suggested that Iranian women masked their expression of gheirat because it was seen as socially undesirable and invited negative character judgements. This resembles the restrained regulatory style of female Spanish/Greek participants, who refrained from expressing anger for different reasons, such as fear of retaliation or escalation. Furthermore, Spanish/Greek participants considered their emotional response to RBVs important for their reputation, and a failure to react potentially damaging to it.

Likewise, Razavi et al. (2023) found that some Iranian participants expressed gheirat to save face.

Finally, gendered beliefs about RBVs appeared in both Spanish/Greek and Iranian data. Some Iranian participants considered gheirat exclusive to men, or more intensely experienced by them (Razavi et al., 2023). Spanish/Greek participants did not go as far as confining the experience to men, but they voiced strong beliefs about gender roles and masculine values.

Limitations

This study relied on student participants for accessibility, which limited sample diversity.

Although a qualitative design aims at theoretical rather than statistical generalisability, across the pooled samples, over 75% of participants were aged 18-22, and their limited life experience may have narrowed the range of identified themes and the richness of the described experiences. For example, some participants had not yet experienced romantic relationships, and few had experienced RBVs as parents. Future research could extend the sample to non-student participants to capture a broader range of relationships and family roles. Furthermore, there was a strong presence of mature students in male Greek accounts retained for final analysis (55.6% over 40 and 77.8% over 25). This may have been a contributing factor to the final thematic structure, alongside cultural differences, likely reflecting life-stage dynamics, family roles, or cultural expectations associated with older Greek men.

Secondly, the thematic analysis of this study was intentionally theory-driven and informed by prior studies of Iranian samples. Fully inductive inquiries might offer new ways of understanding and interpreting RBVs.

Thirdly, although valuable, qualitative inquiry is not designed to assess individual differences or support statistical generalisation. A quantitative extension of this work would be especially informative since honour endorsement can be measured at the individual level rather than only the country level, allowing future research to test whether cultural differences in RBVs are attributable to honour.

Finally, cultures differ in how they conceptualise so-called "basic" emotions such as anger and disgust, and much of this variability may be lost in translation, especially in qualitative studies.

Language-independent measures, such as dimensional ratings of valence and arousal, ratings of dynamic facial expressions in surveys, or physiological responses in experiments, could help address this problem and clarify cross-cultural differences in the semantic boundaries of emotion categories.

Future research

This study suggests several directions for future research. Firstly, adopting an RBV framework broader than the elicitors captured by the English concept of jealousy could provide a more comprehensive understanding of the affective experience of jealousy and its analogues across cultures, regardless of whether the violation comes from a close person or a third person.

Experimental designs that manipulate the source of the violation could test whether and how it shapes the emotional response. Secondly, similarities between Middle Eastern and Southern European samples in the gendered nature of emotional reactions to relational intrusion and attitudes toward it suggest that honour may shape these experiences. Direct comparisons between Southern European and Middle Eastern samples could provide valuable insights into how honour manifests in social norms and relationships across honour cultures, and how it might interact with other cultural logics.

Thirdly, our findings could inform Western-based sociological and criminological frameworks of controlling behaviour (e.g., Stark, 2009) which often reduce it to a product of patriarchal norms and sexism, rather than examining its motivations, such as protective responses to anticipated harmrelated RBVs. For example, the established construct of coercive control (Stark & Hester, 2019) presents "dominance" as the ultimate goal and goes no further. Similarly, when jealousy is examined as a risk factor for coercive behaviour and other forms of IPV, it is often framed as pathological (i.e. paranoid, obsessive, or anxious) fear of romantic or sexual infidelity and loss of the relationship (Mullen, 1995; Puente & Cohen, 2003; Delgado et al., 1997). Given the possessiveness and territoriality observed in honour-based samples, it would make sense to extend the motives of controlling behaviour to protective goals. Such "noble" intentions may be seen as morally justified, making the behaviour harder to recognise as abusive. Future studies could focus on individuals engaging in controlling behaviour to investigate how their motives and experiences differ across RBV types in honour and non-honour cultural contexts, and to inform interventions that address and modify these behaviours.

Conclusion

This study showed that compared to a jealousy-only framing, reconstructing gheirat within the RBV framework elicited more comprehensive accounts of affective and cognitive experiences in European samples, where this concept is absent. As in the Iranian samples, European participants reported a wide range of emotions (sadness, disgust, hatred, anger) and thoughts (low self-worth, possessiveness, territoriality, responsibility) in response to different relational threats (rivalry, intrusion, harassment, and harm). The aim of this study was not to merge heterogeneous experiences under a single label, but to examine them as variants of one underlying phenomenon.

Compared with the British sample, Spanish/Greek participants, due to cultural similarities in honour-based values, were closer to Iranian samples in both the prototype experience of gheirat and meta-beliefs. Specifically, a gendered experiential structure, with RBVs being more harm-prone for men, was associated with a higher propensity for violence and hostility and looser regulation for men, whereas women showed suppression and silence and a generalised mistrust of people, all of which were accompanied by reputational concerns.

Gheirat may therefore operate not simply as an individual emotion, but as a culturally available framework through which relational boundaries and gendered responsibilities are interpreted. Within such a framework, honour-based values tie people's reputation and social value to their adherence to gender roles. This may place men under pressure to prove a precarious masculinity through more forceful advances, which may cross into violence, and women under pressure to resort to emotional suppression and self-censorship to protect their femininity, or simply their own safety and that of their loved ones. These responses are shaped by shared norms and expectations rather than determined by them, and individuals can interpret, negotiate, or resist them.

In utilising these findings in the context of domestic violence and controlling behaviour, one must not only focus on how RBVs might contribute to violence but also examine the protective motives that may underlie such behaviour. Recognising these motives helps explain controlling and violent behaviour but does not excuse it, as such behaviour remains harmful regardless of its intent.

This understanding can inform the design of sustainable interventions that are culturally-informed and closer to the person's cultural orientation and values, while challenging the norms that make such behaviour appear justified.
