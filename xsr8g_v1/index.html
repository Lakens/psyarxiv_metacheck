To check Non-Significant P Value Check

What we found

We found 27 non-significant p values that should be checked for appropriate interpretation.

Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download information from other preregistration sites, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). Statcheck might still miss one-sided tests that are described in another sentence, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. A TRO uses them for each trov:TransparentResearchPerformance. * run_results$exit_status: the exit status of the process that ran the script (0 = ended normally); NA when the process did not report one (a time limit, or Docker failing to start the container). * artifacts: one row per file, with stage (deposit: as downloaded from the repository, hashed from the downloaded file; before_run: the run folder just before the first script ran, after metacheck’s own changes, such as rewritten paths and unpacked archives; after_run: only the files the scripts made (status new), changed (changed) or removed (removed), as an unchanged file is the one listed before the run), path (relative to the run folder, as valid UTF-8, a byte of another encoding written as <xx>), size (bytes), sha256, mime_type (by extension, NA when unknown), source (the file’s address in the repository, for deposit). The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). Files metacheck itself copied or made between passes (see modifications, output_copied, file_placed) show in after_run too. A TRO uses the rows as trov:ResearchArtifacts and each stage as a trov:ArtifactArrangement. * modifications$sha256_before, modifications$sha256_after: the hash of the changed script as downloaded and as it ran; NA when the script is not at the same path in both (a script made from an R Markdown file). * environment: the system that ran the code (trov:TransparentResearchSystem in a TRO): metacheck_version, started_at, ended_at, host (R version, platform, operating system), sandbox, network_isolated (TRUE only when every Docker run had --network none; the process sandbox does not stop the network), internet, timeout_per_script, total_timeout, python_local (the Python run outside Docker and its version), and docker: runs (for each kind of run, r_run, r_install, python_run, python_install, the image and the docker run arguments, such as --network none, --read-only, --cap-drop ALL, no-new-privileges, the process limit and the memory and processor limits) and images (for each image, its ID and digests from docker image inspect, its creation time and the R and Python versions its environment declares).

run_results$checked says what the run of each script shows about the code, in five groups, so that a script that does not reproduce is kept apart from one the check could not test: ran (it ran to the end); code_error (it did not reproduce: it stopped with an error of the code or the deposit, could not be read, or needs a file the deposit does not hold); dependency (a package or program was not available: a package that could not be installed or was not loaded, a program outside R and Python, a system library, or R crashing in a package’s compiled code); time (it did not finish within the time limit for one script, or was not started because the time for all scripts had run out); and not_checkable (it could not be checked automatically: it needs a person, the internet, a key or another setting of the authors’ computer, one platform such as Google Colab, Windows or OpenSAFELY, more memory than the run allows, a file that was not downloaded, a screen, or a program that runs it, or it is a file of a package of the code that Python runs only as a module of that package). It is NA for files run inside another script, files that are not an analysis, files not read, and a Docker failure. summary_table counts each group (repro_ran_n, repro_code_error_n, repro_dependency_n, repro_time_n, repro_not_checkable_n), and the report’s first sentence gives them. The traffic light reflects the static readiness signals by default; a script in code_error that stopped with an error makes it red, as a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure, and a script in one of the other three groups keeps it from being green. Calls that need a screen, which the run does not have (dev.print(), dev.copy2pdf(), dev.copy2eps(), savePlot(), windows(), win.graph(), x11(), quartz(), View()), are replaced before the run by calls that need none, which modifications records as screen_call_replaced. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

How Replication Check works

This module checks references and warns for citations of original studies for which replication or reproduction studies exist in the FLoRA database.

The Replication Check module compares the reference list against studies in the FLoRA (FORRT Library of Replication Attempts) database based on the DOI. If a study in the database is found, a reminder is provided that a replication or reproduction of the original study exists, and should be cited. Introduction

A growing body of empirical research has shown that several characteristics of art (formal elements of art) are associated with aspects of mental health and psychological functioning (Pénzes et al., 2020).

Grounded in psychoanalytic (Freud, 1915; Jung, 1968), and psychodynamic theories (Naumburg, 1966) art-based assessments, such as House-Tree-Person (HTP; Buck, 1969), Draw-A-Person (DAP; Goodenough, 1926; Harris, 1963), and Person Picking an Apple from a Tree (PPAT; Gantt & Tabone, 1998), offer a standardised frameworks for evaluating cognitive, emotional, and psychological functioning. Within these frameworks, art is understood as a medium through which the unconscious is projected, providing insight into underlying psychological states and processes that may be otherwise inaccessible through traditional methods (Betts, 2006; Rockwell & Dunham, 2006).

However, in clinical contexts these assessments mostly rely on symbolic inference and subjective interpretation, leading to considerable criticism regarding objectivity, reliability, and validity in both clinical practice and empirical research (Betts, 2006; Lilienfeld et al., 2000; Malchiodi, 2012). The development of standardised rating instruments, such as the Formal Elements Art Therapy Scale (FEATS; Gantt & Tabone, 1998) and the Diagnostic Drawing Series (DDS; Cohen et al., 1988), represent a broader effort to establish empirical rigour. Structured scoring systems systematically convert observable variables in formal elements of art used to construct artworks, such as colour, space-use, detail, and line, into quantitative data, enabling statistical examination of relationships between individual elements and psychological states. Evidence supports the diagnostic potential and clinical applicability of instruments such as the FEATS and DDS across disorders, including schizophrenia, major depressive disorder, bipolar disorder, ADHD, and Alzheimer's disease (Cohen et al., 1988; Gantt & Tabone, 1998).

Two theoretical frameworks provide a foundation for understanding the relationship between art elements and psychological functioning. The first framework draws on psychodynamic theory, which posits creative expression as a means of unconscious projection, whereby internal states, conflicts, and repressed emotions bypass conscious defences, offering more direct access to underlying intrapsychic processes (Johnson, 1998; Kapitan, 2016; Naumburg, 1966). While psychodynamic approaches have predominantly focused on the symbolic interpretation of imagery, formal elements such as colour use, degree of realism, repetition, use of space, and composition have been proposed as quantifiable indicators of these processes (Malchiodi, 2012).

The second framework rests, in part, on the well-established relationship between affective states and psychomotor functioning, including fine-motor control, muscle tension, and range of movement (Burdick et al., 2009; Morsel et al, 2015). As drawing is inherently dependent on these psychomotor processes, requiring sustained control of grip, pressure, and movement, it follows that affective state influences the physical execution of a drawing, reflected in art elements such as line quality, pressure, Implied Energy, and use of space, irrespective of the intended subject matter (Russo et al., 2014).

Aligning with this framework the Expressive Therapies Continuum (ETC; Hinz, 2009; Lusebrink, 1990), a three-level model comprising Kinaesthetic/Sensory, Perceptual/Affective, and Cognitive/Symbolic levels, which is used to understand the mechanisms of art in psychological functioning. Each level is associated with distinct brain functions and modes of visual, sensory, and affective processing. Within this model, variations in formal elements such as line quality, colour use, form, and space use reflect shifts in affective and cognitive processing (Lusebrink, 2010).

To date, no systematic evaluation of existing literature has been conducted. As such, it remains unclear whether quantitative art-based measures demonstrate adequate validity and reliability, and whether they show the theorised relationships with psychiatric symptoms. This review, therefore, aimed to synthesise empirical evidence on the relationship between formal elements of art and psychiatric symptoms measured using quantitative art-based rating instruments. Specifically, the review aims to: (1) evaluate the direction, strength and consistency of statistical relationships between formal elements of art and symptoms associated with psychiatric disorders; (2) identify which formal elements of art and quantitative art-based measures show the most consistent evidence of association with specific symptom domains; (3) provide recommendations for researchers and clinicians considering the use of quantitative artbased measures in research or clinical assessment.

These objectives are addressed using a predefined protocol and an adapted PICO framework to guide study selection, data extraction, and synthesis.

Methods

This systematic review followed PRISMA 2020 guidelines and was prospectively registered with PROSPERO (CRD42024565810). The study selection process is illustrated in the PRISMA flow diagram (Figure 1).

Search Strategy

A multi-step search strategy was developed following an initial scoping phase to identify highly relevant studies. Ten highly cited papers in the field were used as test cases to guide database selection and refine key concepts. PsycINFO, Web of Science, CINAHL, Scopus, and ProQuest were searched from inception to September 2024, with no limits on language, publication type, or date. The strategy was informed by an adapted Population, Intervention, Comparison, and Outcome (PICO) framework, with a focus on Population and Intervention terms to optimise sensitivity. Search terms were grouped into three concept clusters: psychological constructs, art-based measures and descriptors, and assessment or empirical terminology. Search strings were iteratively piloted and refined using Boolean operators, truncation, nesting, and wildcards, and performance was evaluated against a test set of studies. A specialist health sciences librarian independently reviewed and tested the strategy, and final search strings were adapted for each database. Full search strategies for each database are provided in Appendix A.

Study Selection

Studies that met the following criteria were included: (1) quantitative art-based measures (such as rating scales) with defined grading criteria allowing assessment of inter-rater reliability; (2) evidence demonstrating the strength of a statistical relationship between at least one art-based metric and at least one symptom associated with a psychiatric disorder.

Studies involving non-clinical participants with organic disorders, learning disabilities, cognitive impairment, behavioural disorders, or self-reported psychological distress were considered if they met these criteria. Exclusion criteria were: (1) studies involving art-based assessments that did not include objective or quantitative methods of analysis; (2) literature focusing solely on art therapy as an intervention, treatment approach, or discipline without detailing quantitative assessments; (3) grey literature, review papers, commentaries, conference abstracts, editorials, and qualitative studies; and (4) studies not available in English.

Screening

Eligibility criteria were structured using an adapted PICO framework. Detailed screening procedures are shown in Appendix B. Potentially eligible studies identified by the search were uploaded to EndNote (version 21). Duplicates were removed using EndNote's automated features and manual screening. Studies were classified as "include" or "exclude", and references of included studies were exported into an Excel spreadsheet. An EndNote native (.ris) file was exported to create a new EndNote library for each stage of the screening process. Results were screened for eligibility in three stages by the first author (CC), with a randomly selected sample of 20% double-screened for inclusion by the second author (WM). This process was conducted in three stages: (1) title n = 8,042 (Agreement = 98.66%; κ = 0.85), (2) abstract n = 427 (Agreement = 92.19%; κ = 0.85), ( 3) full text (n = 122) with 100% agreement between both authors. An additional three studies were found through hand-searching the reference lists and forward citation search of included studies, yielding 21 studies in total. The study selection process is illustrated in Figure 1 (PRISMA 2020 flow diagram).

Quality Appraisal

At the time of this review, there was no existing protocol for assessing the quality of evidence in studies involving quantitative art-based measures. Therefore, in line with best-practice recommendations (Petticrew et al., 2013), a structured quality assessment checklist was created, modelled on the Mixed Methods Appraisal Tool (MMAT; Hong et al., 2018) and the JBI critical appraisal tools (Joanna Briggs Institute [JBI], 2017). The checklist was piloted using test studies identified in the initial scoping stage and iteratively refined between the three authors (CC, WM, JS) until consensus was reached that all items were relevant, clearly worded, and captured key sources of bias in quantitative art-based measures. In this context, key sources of bias included participant selection and description, the clarity and appropriateness of the study design and art-based measures, the validity and reliability of data collection and analysis, and the completeness and transparency of reporting and interpretation.

Studies were appraised in two parts. The first section confirmed that the included studies provided adequate data for analysis, including visual examples and relevant quantitative outcomes. It consisted of six yes/no criteria to confirm eligibility and determine whether a study provided supporting material relevant to this review. Specifically, studies were required to: (1) involve psychiatric (2) apply objective measures of art, such as rating scales or grading criteria; (3) analyse quantitative relationships between properties of art and psychiatric symptoms; (4) include examples of participant drawings; (5) incorporate visual examples within the rating criteria where applicable; and (6) contribute meaningful data relevant to the aims of the review. The second section critically appraised methodological quality across 18 items. Studies were evaluated on reporting clarity, research aims, population, study design, data collection and analysis validity, management of potential biases, replication transparency, appropriateness of statistical analyses, interpretation of findings, external validity, generalisability, and acknowledgment of study limitations and future directions. CC assessed full texts, and the results were reviewed collaboratively with WM and JS. Quality appraisal outcomes were not used to exclude studies but were considered when interpreting and weighting findings during data synthesis. The quality appraisal checklist and corresponding table are presented in Appendix C.

Data Synthesis and Analysis

The following framework guided the evaluation and synthesis of evidence: (1) formal elements of art, when measured through structured rating scales, demonstrated quantifiable relationships with psychological symptoms and cognitive functioning, and (2) observable variables in art elements are measurable indicators of underlying psychological states. A data extraction form was developed specifically for this review with three sections: (1) general study information (e.g., aim, population, diagnoses or symptoms, methodology, findings), (2) quantitative results (e.g., effect sizes, correlations), and (3) specifications for art-based measures. The data extraction form is provided in Appendix D. Data were extracted from included studies by CC and reviewed collaboratively with co-authors (WM, JS). Given the heterogeneity of the included studies, it was not possible to undertake a meta-analysis.

Instead, a narrative synthesis was conducted, which enables the systematic synthesis and interpretation of quantitative data where meta-analysis is not feasible (Popay et al., 2006). To manage the wide range of art-based measures, an initial scoping analysis was conducted, identifying individual art scales (n = 677). Scales were classified into two categories based on their primary focus: (1) those assessing formal elements of art, and (2) scales developed for directed drawing tasks or specific artbased assessments (e.g., Draw-A-Person in the Rain). Scales within the second group were evaluated to determine whether they aligned sufficiently to be included in category one. Data were synthesised through a multi-step process. A data summary table was first constructed, capturing key details for each study, including sample size, psychological variables, rating scales and measures, statistical outcomes, reliability and validity indices, main findings, limitations, and study aims. An initial scoping table was then used to map all psychological variables to the rating scales measured across studies. Where variables reflected overlapping psychological constructs, these were consolidated and remapped accordingly. A series of tables were subsequently developed for each overarching art element, indexing specific rating scales to their associated psychological variables.

Finally, summary tables were developed for each formal art element to consolidate all relevant information needed for a focused and structured analysis. Each table detailed the sample size, the specific rating scales or other art measures used, art-based assessments and psychological measures used, statistical analysis methods, quantitative data and interpretations, reliability (if reported), and the main study outcomes.

Results

The following sections summarise findings for the elements of art with some consistency in the findings: Colour, Detail, Line, Realism, Implied Energy and Space-Use. Characteristics of the included studies are presented in Appendix E, with statistical results presented in Appendix F.

Studies varied considerably in design, sample size (n = 30 -942), participant characteristics, and statistical reporting. Most studies employed cross-sectional designs, (Broadbent et al., 2006; Dörken, 1954; Lehmann & Risquez, 1953; Reynolds et al., 2007). There was almost no consistency to the manner in which the data were coded and analysed, precluding meta-analysis. For example, some studies examined differences across diagnostic groups (e.g., Cohen et al., 1988; Hacking et al., 1996), whereas others assessed mood symptoms as a continuous measure (e.g., Shella, 2020). The participant groups were diverse, including psychiatric patients and non-clinical controls (e.g. Hacking et al., 1996), people with medical conditions such as heart failure, multiple sclerosis or cancer (Broadbent et al., 2006; Kim & Chung, 2021; Shella, 2020), and adolescents in conflict with the law (e.g. Kruthi & Manickam, 2023). Standardised formal art elements tests were often used (i.e. the FEATS and DDS), but some used bespoke measures (e.g. Reynolds et al., 2007) some studies analysed as composite scores (e.g., Conrad et al., 2011), with a wide range in terms of the total number of formal elements studied in each study (2 to 36). Importantly, only some studies used analyses that would correct for multiple comparisons, such as regression (e.g., Sheng et al., 2019), whereas others reported each correlation without correcting for multiple comparisons (e.g., Kim & Chung, 2021). Given the diversity, statistical results have been synthesised narratively to describe the direction of associations reported, and the strength of association is reported only where studies tend to consistently find effects in a similar direction.

Colour

The range of colour used in drawings was a key focus of two early, large-sample studies (Dörken, 1954; Lehmann & Risquez, 1953). Both studies found a narrowed colour range in depression relative to non-clinical controls, and relative to mania. Furthermore, Dörken (1954) incorporated a repeatedmeasures design, finding that colour range in patients who recovered from psychotic depression but remained unchanged in those who did not, a pattern consistent with the group differences. In contrast, colour range in mania did not differ from that of controls and did not change significantly with recovery, although after treatment, recovered patients used a wider colour range than those who remained chronically manic (Dörken, 1954).

The Prominence of Colour is a formal element similar to colour range, and it showed a similar association with less depressive symptoms in a study across inpatients and outpatients (Swan-Foster et al., 2003). In a non-clinical sample of university students, Prominence of Colour correlated with both anxiety and depression, and higher symptom levels predicted fewer colours used (Au et al., 2024).

Similarly, the use of monochrome or black has been shown to be associated with higher anxiety (Sheng et al., 2019) and greater symptom severity in a mixed diagnostic group (Teneycke et al., 2009), which fits the same pattern if monochrome can be regarded as the most extreme narrowing of colour range. However, some studies did not find the general association described here between negative mood states and a narrower range of colours, despite using similar metrics and having adequate sample sizes (Conrad et al., 2011; Shella, 2020). Within two studies of adolescent samples, however, these relationships were not observed, and weakly reversed (Kruthi & Manickam, 2023; Manickam & Sajani, 2016). The picture within psychosis was also contradictory and somewhat limited in scope, with one study reporting a lower range of colour use in schizophrenia relative to non-clinical controls (Cohen et al., 1988), yet another study finding more extensive use of colour to be associated with the greater severity of hallucinations and delusions (Zhou et al., 2019). Moreover, some studies examined differences in the specific colours used, but their findings did not reveal a consistent pattern across studies (Hacking et al., 1996; Hacking & Foreman, 2000). Taken together, these studies find a consistent trend for greater mood symptoms (depression, anxiety, mania) to be associated with restricted colour range, to the point of focusing on black or monochrome, but with some failures of replication, and some notable but isolated exceptions in psychotic symptoms and in adolescents.

These studies suggest a trend whereby greater depressive and anxiety symptoms are associated with a restricted colour range, sometimes extending to a focus on black or monochrome. Greater weight is given to the two early, large-sample studies in light of their convergent group differences and Dörken's (1954) repeated-measures finding that colour range increased as depressive symptoms remitted, providing within-person support for the association. The findings for mania are more complex: in the early group-comparison studies, depression was associated with a narrower colour range than mania, whereas Dörken (1954) also reported an increase in colour range following recovery from mania.

Detail

Depressed mood correlated with less detailed drawings, whether in group comparisons between people with depression and non-clinical controls (Deng et al., 2022; Manickam & Sajani, 2016; Swan-Foster et al), correlations with severity of depression (Manickam & Sajani, 2016; Deng et al., 2022).

However, each of these effects was small in size, and there were examples of adequately powered studies with no relationship found (Shella, 2020), and studies indicating that lower Detail associated with greater impairment across diagnostic groups (White et al., 2004). There was no evidence of difference in Detail when comparing adolescents with and without ADHD (Saneei et al., 2011), and evidence of greater Detail associated with depressive and phobic symptoms, but not anxiety, in adolescents in conflict with the law (Kruthi & Mainckam, 2023). Overall, these findings indicate a possible relationship between depression and less detailed drawings, but it is weak, somewhat inconsistent, and could indicate a general impairment.

Line

A relationship between Line Quality and depression has not been established, with no significant associations between in two cross-sectional studies of adolescents (Kruthi & Manickam, 2023; Manickam & Sajani, 2016). However, lighter line pressure did emerge as a distinguishing feature of individuals with dysthymia relative to those with schizophrenia and the nonpatient group in a large psychiatric sample (n = 239; Cohen et al., 1988). The pattern within anxiety was equally mixed.

Whereas one study using the DDS found that breast cancer patients with higher anxiety used more 'medium 'pressure (versus 'light 'or 'heavy') in their drawings of feelings specifically (Kim et al., 2018), a study using the synthetic house-tree-person test in cancer patients found that particularly weak or heavy lines were associated with anxiety, but not other line features. The element of lines studied in other groups also provided a complex picture, with anxious children with ADHD using thicker and sharper lines than non-clinical controls (Saneei et al., 2011), and people with mood disorders drawing fewer lines than people with schizophrenia or controls, whilst Painted Lines did not vary across groups (Hacking et al., 1996; Hacking & Foreman, 2000).

Realism

Realism, broadly defined as the degree to which drawings depict figures and scenes accurately and coherently, was not consistently associated with symptoms. Whilst two studies showed that depressed and 'impaired thinking 'group participants drew less realistic drawings compared to those in control groups (Lehmann & Risquez, 1953; White et al., 2004), depression severity was not linked to Realism scores in adults with multiple sclerosis (Shella, 2020). In adolescent samples, realism showed no relationship with anxiety or depression (Manickam & Sajani, 2016; Kruthi & Manickam, 2023; Saneei et al., 2011). Surprisingly, only one study reported on psychosis, and yet found a longitudinal increase in the Reality Contact of drawings after improvements in schizophrenia, and after recovery from psychotic depression (Dörken, 1954).

The clearest evidence came from psychotic and severe mood disorders. In Dörken's (1954) repeatedmeasures study, Contact with Reality scores increased between first and final paintings in patients whose schizophrenia had improved (M = 4.90 to 8.55, p < .01) and in those who had recovered from psychotic depression (M = 3.20 to 9.27, p < .01), but did not change in chronic groups. Lehmann and Risquez (1953) similarly reported lower Contact with Reality scores in the first paintings of patients with schizophrenia, depression and organic disorders than in non-psychotic patients, although test statistics were not reported for this scale. Among children and adolescents with emotional and behavioural disorders, those with impaired thinking produced less realistic drawings than those without (White et al., 2004). By contrast, realism was unrelated to depression severity in adults with multiple sclerosis (Shella, 2020) and in school-aged adolescents (Manickam & Sajani, 2016), showed only weak correlations with anxiety and depression among adolescents in conflict with the law (Kruthi & Manickam, 2023), and did not differ between children with and without ADHD (Saneei et al., 2011).

Using the DDS, Cohen et al. (1988) found mixed representation (Realism) to be associated with dysthymia and less common among nonpatients. In contrast, representational drawings in free pictures were not associated with depression, nor was biomorphic abstraction in feeling pictures associated with schizophrenia. Taken together, reduced realism was most evident in conditions involving disturbed reality testing or cognitive disorganisation and tended to recover alongside clinical improvement, whereas associations with depressive or anxious symptoms in non-psychotic samples were largely absent.

Implied Energy

Implied Energy is a measure scoring the perceived physical effort conveyed through the marks and strokes in a drawing. Some studies linked lower Implied Energy to greater depressive severity, but findings varied across studies. Support came from studies tracking risk and change: pregnant women at highest depression risk produced the lowest Implied Energy scores (Swan-Foster et al., 2003), and Dörken (1954) found scores rose with recovery from psychotic depression but stayed flat in chronic cases. By contrast, no association appeared in adults with multiple sclerosis (Shella, 2020) or related to dysthymia (Cohen et al., 1988), and Manickam and Sajani (2016) found a less implied energy associated with greater depression across their continuous sample of adolescents, but the opposite pattern when comparing depressed and control groups. Anxiety showed no association with implied energy either in adolescents (Kruthi & Manickam, 2023) or in adults with multiple sclerosis (Shella, 2020), whereas Implied Energy correlated significantly with both anxiety and depression in university students, although coefficients were not reported (Au et al., 2024).

Space-Use

Space-Use in drawings is typically operationalised as the proportion of the page occupied by the drawing. For depression, less use of the page was found among pregnant women at high risk for depression compared to low-risk women (Swan-Foster et al., 2003), and a similar pattern held for depressed adults and adolescents relative to non-depressed peers (Kim & Chung, 2021). No such association appeared among psychiatric inpatients (Cohen et al., 1988) or adults with depression (Manickam & Sajani, 2016), and Space-Use was likewise unrelated to depression in adults with multiple sclerosis (Shella, 2020). A similar inconsistency appeared for anxiety. Less space was used in DDS-directed tree drawings by participants with anxiety than by controls. However, no differences emerged in free or feeling-directed drawings (Kim et al., 2018). Only weak, non-distinguishing associations between Space-Use and anxiety measures were found among adolescents in conflict with the law relative to typically developing adolescents (Kruthi & Manickam, 2023). By contrast, in cardiac illness, larger drawn area was associated with higher cardiac-specific anxiety among heart failure patients, both before and after heart failure onset (Reynolds et al., 2007), and larger heart drawings were similarly linked to greater cardiac anxiety three months after a heart attack. However, this association did not persist at six months (Broadbent et al., 2006). The findings for psychotic disorders followed a similar, inconsistent pattern. Greater use of space by all clinical groups relative to controls was reported in one study (Hacking & Foreman, 2000), whereas the opposite was found in another, with the non-clinical group producing the highest mean scores, exceeding affective disorder, non-affective psychosis, and substance use groups (Hacking et al., 1996); these opposing results may partly reflect differences in how space was operationalised between the two studies. No significant association between space and schizophrenia was found in a further sample (Cohen et al., 1988).

Discussion

The evidence indicates a possible association between certain elements of art and psychological symptoms across diverse populations, but the only indication of a consistent relationship is for colour and detail. Yet even the strength of these relationships remains relatively weak within these domains.

One exception is the longitudinal study by Dörken (1954), which reported a large effect for an increase in their idiosyncratic index of colour variety as patients recovered from depression. As is typical from the huge heterogeneity in this field, no other studies have replicated this design from a very early study, even with different measures of formal elements. The lack of a clear conclusion across this review is often due to inconsistencies in the operationalisation of samples, research designs and formal art elements across studies, with different features or properties used to represent the same element.

The design of the reviewed studies can be considered from the perspectives of foundational frameworks. Potentially, theoretical frameworks on psychomotor functioning, such as the ETC, would offer a basis for these findings, as Implied Energy reflects perceived movement, vigour, and intensity in drawings; lower scores can be understood as manifestations of reduced psychomotor functioning and kinaesthetic engagement that often accompany depressed mood, whereas higher scores may indicate greater activation and expressive energy, including in states of recovery. However, given the inconsistent findings, it is not possible to confirm or disconfirm this theory based on the studies reviewed.

Similarly, from a psychodynamic and psychoanalytic perspective, realism in drawings is thought to be an indirect measure of psychosis and mania (Killick, 2017; Naumburg, 1950), such that increasingly fragmented, disorganised, or poorly integrated depictions in drawings are indicative of greater symptom severity, while more realistic depictions are considered consistent with intact reality testing.

Within the Expressive Therapies Continuum, realism is associated with higher-order cognitive processing, and variation in realism is therefore interpreted as reflecting differences in cognitive integration, which, from a psychodynamic perspective, may also be understood in terms of the degree of contact with Reality. However, the mixed findings in relation to the formal elements of Reality do not confirm or disconfirm this theory.

Viewed collectively, quantitative art-based measures appear to have at least some sensitivity to mood states and symptom severity; however, the direction and interpretation of change were not uniform, with inconsistencies evident even within the same art element. To take one example of the six studies examining the FEATS colour subscales (Prominence of Colour and Colour Fit) and depression, two reported negative relationships, two reported positive relationships, and two reported non-significant relationships (e.g., Conrad et al., 2011; Manickam & Sajani, 2016). Art-based assessments differed considerably in their drawing directives, materials, scoring criteria, and the operationalisation of rating scales across studies. Statistical approaches also varied, ranging from group comparisons to correlational analyses, with many studies underpowered for the relationships being examined.

Together with small cross-sectional samples, the absence of test-retest data, and limited control of confounding factors such as artistic training, cognitive ability, and medication use, these factors restricted statistical power, causal inference, and meaningful comparison across studies. Therefore, it was not possible to draw out implications for theory or practice based on this systematic review of this large but diverse literature.

Limitations

Despite an extensive, multi-stage search process with iterative refinement, the inherent ambiguity of key terminology may have inadvertently led to some eligible studies being missed. No established critical appraisal tool was available for studies employing quantitative art-based assessments, necessitating the development of a new customised tool adapted from existing frameworks. While consistent with best-practice recommendations, this process may have introduced an element of subjectivity into the quality ratings. Substantial heterogeneity in samples, diagnostic and symptom profiles, art-based assessments, rating scales, and analytical methods precluded meta-analysis and limited the review to narrative synthesis without pooled effect estimates. Key psychometric information was underreported, particularly indices of reliability and validity, making it difficult to evaluate the quality of the art-based scales and reducing confidence in the strength of the reported associations.

Finally, the necessary grouping of 677 diverse art-based scales and overlapping psychological constructs for synthesis required subjective categorisation, which may have introduced interpretive bias.

Future Research

Psychometric evaluation, standardisation of art-based scales and scoring systems should be prioritised to enable more robust synthesis and comparison across studies. Future research should employ longitudinal, within-person designs with repeated assessments alongside concurrent measurement of symptomatology using validated clinical instruments (e.g., PHQ-9, YMRS) to establish the sensitivity, specificity, and reliability of these measures. Large-scale samples spanning multiple diagnostic groups and symptom severity are also needed to clarify whether observed associations are diagnosis-specific or reflect broader dimensions of psychological functioning.

Separately, the development and validation of automated, digital scoring systems may enhance objectivity and scalability, provided these systems are benchmarked against ratings from trained human evaluators.

Conclusion

In conclusion, there is evidence to support further empirical investigation to determine the extent to which elements of art reflect symptom fluctuations and severity across psychiatric disorders and broader psychiatric disorders. Overall, the evidence highlights a promising yet understudied field, with art-based approaches offering novel ways to capture aspects of psychiatric disorders that may not be accessible through conventional methods.

Appendix A Search Strategy

The search strategy was developed through a multi-step refinement process informed by a preliminary scoping phase to identify highly relevant studies. The ten most cited papers were selected as test cases to inform database selection and the development of the search strategy. PsycINFO, Web of Science (WOS), CINAHL, Scopus, and ProQuest were selected based on the relevance and specificity of the returned results. To build the initial search strategy, an adapted Population, Intervention, Comparison, and Outcome (PICO) framework (Higgins et al., 2022) was used to determine concept groups and generate a list of keywords and synonyms.

Search strings were subsequently piloted in PsycINFO using the previously identified test studies to evaluate the sensitivity and specificity of the retrieved results.

Developing a targeted search strategy proved challenging due to the ambiguity of key search terms. Extensive testing using Boolean operators, truncation, nesting, and wildcards either yielded an unmanageable volume of irrelevant results or failed to capture the test studies. This was largely attributed to highly polysemous keywords such as "art," "drawing," "image," and "picture," prevalent in unrelated contexts such as "state-of-the-art," "artery," "draw attention to," "drawing conclusions," "fMRI images," or "big picture." Similarly, keywords such as "mood," "psychological," "disorder," "emotion," "behaviour," "diagnosis," "instrument," "tool," "scale," "validity," "empirical," "objective," and "quantitative" were too broad and lacked the necessary precision.

To address this issue, a detailed analysis was conducted on the titles, abstracts, and keywords of twenty-five studies identified during the initial scoping phase as potentially meeting inclusion criteria. A word-frequency analysis was performed to identify the most used keywords, including variants (e.g., draw, drawing, drawings) and synonyms, across concept groups. Keywords from each study were then colour-coded according to their respective concept group to determine whether all four groups were represented.

Notably, a pattern emerged in which only three concept groups were consistently present in the abstracts rather than all four. Accordingly, keywords were reorganised into three new concept groups: 1) psychological, 2) artbased measures and descriptors, and 3) assessment and empirical terminology, with care taken to ensure that at least one term from each group was present in all abstracts. Keywords that were rarely used or could be substituted with more commonly occurring synonyms (e.g., "drawing" in place of "picture") were removed. This was followed by a structured refinement process trialled across the five databases. Although there was a marked improvement, the results continued to reflect suboptimal sensitivity and specificity.

To assist in the refinement process, a specialist librarian with expertise in database searching was consulted to review the search strategy and advise on advanced searching techniques. Independent searches were also conducted by the librarian, yielding similar unsatisfactory results. This prompted an extensive, iterative process of refinement and testing until a comprehensive search with sufficient specificity and sensitivity was achieved.

Finally, search strings were adapted for each database. Database searches were conducted from inception to September 2024, with no limits applied to ensure maximum coverage.

Eligibility Criteria (PICO Framework)

Population: Individuals diagnosed with psychiatric conditions (primary focus: bipolar disorder, major depressive disorder, schizophrenia spectrum disorders, anxiety disorders, and related conditions) or sub-threshold psychiatric symptoms assessed in clinical or research settings, including control participants for comparison.

Intervention/Exposure: Quantitative assessments of art elements (including but not limited to: colour use, space use, line quality, detail, implied energy, perseveration, rotation, and shape) using validated rating scales (e.g., FEATS, DDS, DAPA, or comparable validated scoring tools) applied to standardised directed drawing tasks (e.g., PPAT, HTP, DAP, DDS) or spontaneous drawings produced in clinical or research contexts. Assessments must utilise quantitative scoring methods.

Comparators: Cross-sectional comparisons between diagnostic groups, levels of symptom severity, or healthy controls. Longitudinal comparisons included within-person changes or repeated assessments over time in bipolar disorder or other psychiatric conditions.

Outcomes: Primary outcomes included statistical associations between art elements and psychiatric symptoms or diagnostic categories (correlation coefficients, standardised mean differences, odds ratios, regression coefficients, effect sizes, sensitivity, specificity, and diagnostic accuracy). Secondary outcomes included psychometric properties of art-based measures (inter-rater reliability coefficients, test-retest reliability, internal consistency, convergent validity, discriminant validity) and sensitivity to detect longitudinal changes in symptom severity or mood state transitions.

While eligibility criteria were structured using the PICO framework, the search strategy was developed using an adapted framework focusing on Population and Intervention terms to optimise sensitivity, consistent with current recommendations (Hausner et al., 2012; Higgins et al., 2022). Therefore, the Comparison and Outcome elements were applied during the eligibility screening phase.

Appendix C Quality Appraisal

At the time of this review, there was no existing protocol for assessing the quality of evidence in studies involving quantitative art-based measures. Therefore, in line with best-practice recommendations (Petticrew et al., 2013), a structured quality assessment checklist was created, modelled on the Mixed Methods Appraisal Tool (MMAT; Hong et al., 2018) and the JBI critical appraisal tools (Joanna Briggs Institute [JBI], 2017). The checklist was piloted using test studies identified in the initial scoping stage and refined between the three authors (CC, WM, JS). Studies were appraised in two parts.

The first section confirmed that the included studies provided adequate data for analysis, including visual examples and relevant quantitative outcomes. It consisted of six yes/no criteria to confirm eligibility and determine whether a study provided supporting material relevant to this review. Specifically, studies were required to: ( 1) involve psychiatric symptoms or recognised symptoms of mood; (2) apply objective measures of art, such as rating scales or grading criteria; (3) analyse quantitative relationships between properties of art and psychiatric symptoms or mood; (4) include examples of participant drawings; (5) incorporate visual examples within the rating criteria where applicable; and ( 6) contribute meaningful data relevant to the aims of the review.. The second section critically appraised methodological quality across 18 items. Studies were evaluated on reporting clarity, research aims, population, study design, data collection and analysis validity, management of potential biases, replication transparency, appropriateness of statistical analyses, interpretation of findings, external validity, generalisability, and acknowledgment of study limitations and future directions. Full texts of all included studies were assessed by CC, and the results were reviewed collaboratively with WM and JS. Appraisal outcomes were not used to exclude studies but were considered in the interpretation and weighting of findings during data synthesis.
