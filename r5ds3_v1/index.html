To fix Exact P-Values

What we found

Show details

Reporting p values imprecisely (e.g., p < .05) reduces transparency, reproducibility, and re-use (e.g., in p value meta-analyses). Best practice is to report exact p-values with three decimal places (e.g., p = .032) unless p values are smaller than 0.001, in which case you can use p < .001.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard), also Internal Review Board - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term); not IEC followed by a number, which is a technical standard - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee/Commission, in the singular or plural - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee, Bioethics Committee/Commission, Ethikkommission - Ethical Review Authority/Unit/Office/Agency - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Human Research Protection Office/Program, Office for Human Subjects Protection - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee(s) on/for (Medical and Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt - Ethics review that was not needed: Ethics review was not required, did not require further ethical review, exempt from formal human research ethics review, no new institutional ethics review

A template line the authors have not filled in (“[Add ethics approval … here.]”) is not a statement.

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

When a statement is expected

The module sorts each paper by where its data came from (the internal .data_source(); the signs and how the rule was tested are described in R/data-source.R): - own: the authors collected data from people or animals, for this paper, in an earlier study of their own, or (a protocol) will collect them; - existing_records: records about individual people that the authors obtained for this study, such as hospital records or a cohort; - existing_unclear: existing data about people from a source the module could not identify; - open_resource: an open resource any researcher can use, such as UK Biobank or NHANES; - none: no data about people or animals (a review, simulation, commentary, theory, tutorial, coding of published papers, or figures about groups); - uncertain: the signs were missing or pointed both ways; - no_text: the paper has no text.

A statement is expected for own, existing_records and existing_unclear. A paper without a statement in these classes, or in uncertain, is flagged. How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. A TRO uses them for each trov:TransparentResearchPerformance. * run_results$exit_status: the exit status of the process that ran the script (0 = ended normally); NA when the process did not report one (a time limit, or Docker failing to start the container). * artifacts: one row per file, with stage (deposit: as downloaded from the repository, hashed from the downloaded file; before_run: the run folder just before the first script ran, after metacheck’s own changes, such as rewritten paths and unpacked archives; after_run: only the files the scripts made (status new), changed (changed) or removed (removed), as an unchanged file is the one listed before the run), path (relative to the run folder, as valid UTF-8, a byte of another encoding written as <xx>), size (bytes), sha256, mime_type (by extension, NA when unknown), source (the file’s address in the repository, for deposit). The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). Files metacheck itself copied or made between passes (see modifications, output_copied, file_placed) show in after_run too. A TRO uses the rows as trov:ResearchArtifacts and each stage as a trov:ArtifactArrangement. * modifications$sha256_before, modifications$sha256_after: the hash of the changed script as downloaded and as it ran; NA when the script is not at the same path in both (a script made from an R Markdown file). * environment: the system that ran the code (trov:TransparentResearchSystem in a TRO): metacheck_version, started_at, ended_at, host (R version, platform, operating system), sandbox, network_isolated (TRUE only when every Docker run had --network none; the process sandbox does not stop the network), internet, timeout_per_script, total_timeout, python_local (the Python run outside Docker and its version), and docker: runs (for each kind of run, r_run, r_install, python_run, python_install, the image and the docker run arguments, such as --network none, --read-only, --cap-drop ALL, no-new-privileges, the process limit and the memory and processor limits) and images (for each image, its ID and digests from docker image inspect, its creation time and the R and Python versions its environment declares).

run_results$checked says what the run of each script shows about the code, in five groups, so that a script that does not reproduce is kept apart from one the check could not test: ran (it ran to the end); code_error (it did not reproduce: it stopped with an error of the code or the deposit, could not be read, or needs a file the deposit does not hold); dependency (a package or program was not available: a package that could not be installed or was not loaded, a program outside R and Python, a system library, or R crashing in a package’s compiled code); time (it did not finish within the time limit for one script, or was not started because the time for all scripts had run out); and not_checkable (it could not be checked automatically: it needs a person, the internet, a key or another setting of the authors’ computer, one platform such as Google Colab, Windows or OpenSAFELY, more memory than the run allows, a file that was not downloaded, a screen, or a program that runs it, or it is a file of a package of the code that Python runs only as a module of that package). It is NA for files run inside another script, files that are not an analysis, files not read, and a Docker failure. summary_table counts each group (repro_ran_n, repro_code_error_n, repro_dependency_n, repro_time_n, repro_not_checkable_n), and the report’s first sentence gives them. The traffic light reflects the static readiness signals by default; a script in code_error that stopped with an error makes it red, as a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure, and a script in one of the other three groups keeps it from being green. Calls that need a screen, which the run does not have (dev.print(), dev.copy2pdf(), dev.copy2eps(), savePlot(), windows(), win.graph(), x11(), quartz(), View()), are replaced before the run by calls that need none, which modifications records as screen_call_replaced. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

Speakers in many languages show a processing bias for consonants in word learning and recognition (C-bias). Infants learning such languages initially display an opposite bias for vowels (V-bias), switching to the C-bias through experience with native phono-lexical properties. In French-learning infants, this switch happens between 6 and 8 months. Here we asked whether early phonetic production is linked to this developmental pattern. Thirty-one French-learning 7-month-olds completed a wordform segmentation task testing consonant-vowel perceptual biases, daylong recordings (LENA system), and parental questionnaires. This procedure captured several production measures, including: a) the variety of consonants produced (human-coded); b) consistency in consonant production (Vocal Motor Schemes); c) interactivity of vocal productions in social settings (LENA's machine-coded estimate 'Conversational Turns Count', and the Vocal Reactivity Scale of the Infant Behavior Questionnaire); as well as d) the frequency of canonical babbling vocalizations (LENA's estimate 'Child Vocalization Count'). Results confirmed no group-level bias at this age, consistent with an ongoing developmental transition. Subsequent statistical analyses of individual variability revealed complex effects of phonetic production on the emergence of the C-bias, with a significant 2-way interaction between variety of consonants produced and interactivity of vocal productions. Surprisingly, this interaction revealed that more advanced production skills were negatively linked with the emergence of the C-bias in infants who do not benefit from frequent vocal interactions in their social environments. This result highlights complex relationships between early phonetic production and phono-lexical processing, showing that perception/production links can be modulated by social interaction.

Introduction

Human languages include two basic classes of speech segments, consonants and vowels, which differ markedly in their articulatory and acoustic properties. In several languages, consonants outnumber vowels and are thus more informative for differentiating lexical items (Ladefoged & Maddieson, 1996). Relatedly, adult speakers of many languages have long been found to show a perceptual bias in favor of consonants for word processing, termed the 'C-bias' (Nespor et al., 2003), relying more on the information provided by consonants as compared to vowels to memorize, mentally manipulate, and recognize lexical items (Nazzi & Cutler, 2019). However, this pattern of preferential attention to consonants versus vowels varies cross-linguistically, probably in link with the phono-lexical properties of the linguistic systems (Nazzi, 2025). In particular, the C-bias is not observed in tonal languages, in which vowels carry lexical information, and in which an opposite bias for vowels has been observed (V-bias; Gómez et al., 2018; Poltrock et al., 2018; Wiener & Turnbull, 2016). These results show that consonant-vowel perceptual biases, i.e., relative attention to consonants and vowels in lexical processing, are shaped by experience with the native language. The present study will contribute to this issue by investigating whether experience producing the sounds of the native language is linked with the emergence of the consonant bias, in French-learning infants.

Studies examining the development of these biases have shown that these preferences already emerge during infancy. In Romance languages (French, Italian, Spanish), an early V-bias, found at 5 and 6 months, switches to a C-bias at 8 months on average (Bouchon et al., 2015 (Bouchon et al., , 2022;; Hochmann et al., 2018 Hochmann et al., , 2011;; Nishibayashi & Nazzi, 2016; Von Holzen & Nazzi, 2020). In Germanic languages (English, Danish, German), no C-bias is found before 30 months of age (Floccia et al., 2014; Højen & Nazzi, 2016; Nazzi et al., 2009; Piot et al., 2025). Finally, in Mandarin and Cantonese, two languages with tonal systems, some evidence of V-bias has been found between 11 and 30 months (Chen et al., 2021; Li et al., 2026; Wewalaarachchi et al., 2017). Taken together, this work shows that consonant-vowel perceptual biases emerge through experience with the native language.

To explain these developmental effects, it has been hypothesized that auditory experience with vowels and consonants might play a role in this process (see the 'acoustic/phonetic bias hypothesis' in Nishibayashi & Nazzi, 2016). Young infants might at first devote more attention to vowels because they are familiar with these sounds. In fact, vowels are significantly more audible than consonants during uterine life (Moon et al., 2013) and convey prosodic information (information on intonation, rhythm, stress, pitch, loudness, and tempo), which constitutes a major focus in early speech processing (Gervain, 2018). However, importantly, acoustic exposure is not the only form of sensory experience that infants have with the sounds of language. Specifically, infants' experience producing speech sounds, i.e., sensorimotor experience with speech gestures and their sensory correlates, is also linked with speech processing and its development (Choi et al., 2023; Guellaï et al., 2014).

A crucial step in infants' sensorimotor learning about speech sounds is represented by babbling. Typically starting around 6 months of age, babbling is a cross-modal training serving the purpose of phonetic development. It consists in the repeated production of consonant-vowel syllable sequences, allowing infants to establish the connection between the production of speech gestures and their auditory and sensorimotor feedback. Babbling progressively evolves into more complex and lexicalized productions in the input language (Vihman et al., 2009).

Previous research has revealed that babbling is linked with advancements not only in speech production but also in speech perception. In fact, babbling is linked to word segmentation (Hoareau et al., 2019), word-form learning and recognition (Altvater-Mackensen & Fikkert, 2010; DePaolis et al., 2011; Lorenzini & Nazzi, 2022; Majorano et al., 2014 Majorano et al., , 2019;; Vihman & Cox, 2026; Marilyn M. Vihman, 2022), as well as to the ability to pair auditory and visual language stimuli (Altvater-Mackensen et al., 2016) and to the development of categorical perception, or the learning of sound categories in the native language (Vilain et al., 2019). Overall, these studies have suggested a link between the practice of the first speech sounds and enhanced perception of the practiced sounds as compared to speech sounds that are not (or not yet) actively practiced in the infant's babbling, which dovetails with other work on infant neurophysiology linking motor areas of the brain with non-native speech perception (Kuhl et al., 2014). Other studies have shown, in experimental work that manipulates infant oromotor configurations, that sensorimotor information is included in speech processing even before the onset of babbling, as early as at 3 months of age (Bruderer et al., 2015; Choi et al., 2019 Choi et al., , 2021;; Yeung & Werker, 2013). In conclusion, previous literature strongly motivates considering sensorimotor experience among the factors that influence phonetic development.

1.1.1 The present study

Sensorimotor experience may be relevant to the development of the C-bias, namely in the context of the switch from the V-to the C-bias: vowel-like sounds dominate in infants' productions during the period in which a V-bias is found (until 5-6 months of age). In turn, consonant production appears and consolidates with babbling (6-8 months of age; e.g. Blake & Boysson-Bardies, 1992; De Boysson-Bardies & Vihman, 1991; Kuhl et al., 2008) , during the period in which the switch to a Cbias starts to be observed. Thus, the V-to-C-bias switch might be favored, among other factors, by the progressive accumulation of sensorimotor knowledge on the two classes of sounds: first vowels, then consonants. We tested whether the V-to-C-bias switch is linked with phonetic production, operationalized through four measures, with full details of how these measures are calculated being described in Section 2.2.1.

First, we looked at Consonant Variety, which was defined as the inventory of different consonants produced by the infant (e.g., Robb & Bleile, 1994). We expected that producing a larger variety of consonant sounds would be linked with a higher C-bias, a prediction derived from previous research showing a link between Consonant Variety and phonetic processing of familiar words during the first year of life (Lorenzini & Nazzi, 2022).

Second, we examined Vocal Motor Schemes, abbreviated VMS, which captures the Consistency of Consonant Production. This is defined as the degree to which a particular consonant is produced repeatedly and in a stable phonetic form, thus representing an acquired motor scheme (McCune & Vihman, 2001; M. M. Vihman & Cox, 2026). While Consonant Variety measures the range of different phonetic-motor patterns that are produced by the infant, Consonant Consistency measures the stability of production of these phonetic-motor patterns (an infant may, e.g., show a range of 8 consonants produced, but only 2 of them may be stably produced in a VMS). Our prediction was that Consistency of Consonant Production would be linked with the C-bias in ways parallel to that for Consonant Variety, as prior work has suggested facilitatory effects of production consistency on novel word learning (DePaolis et al., 2011 (DePaolis et al., , 2013;; Majorano et al., 2014 Majorano et al., , 2019; M. M. Vihman & Cox, 2026).

Third, we also asked whether the infants' transition to the C-bias might be modulated by Interactivity of Vocal Productions, defined as the degree to which the infants' vocalizations are embedded in interactive contexts. Interactivity warrants consideration in research assessing perception-production links because previous research has shown that adult contingent vocal responses, compared with non-vocal or non-contingent responses, elicit increased and increasingly complex infant productions (Goldstein & Schwade, 2008; Zhang et al., 2024). Interactivity was measured both through the Vocal Reactivity Scale of the Infant Behavior Questionnaire and through Conversational Turns Count (CTC), a machine-coded measure provided by LENA algorithms that estimates the number of conversational turns between the target infant and adults (Cristia et al., 2021; Ganek & Eriks-Brophy, 2018).

Finally, we also examined the Frequency of Canonical Vocalizations produced by the infant, which is also an automated estimation provided by the LENA system, estimating the number of CV vocalizations provided by the infant (Bastianello et al., 2024; Canault et al., 2016; Ganek & Eriks-Brophy, 2018). We included this measure as a covariate to determine whether possible associations between phonetic production measures and the bias switch were dependent on this quantitative aspect or rather specifically linked to more qualitative aspects of phonetic development (i.e., the abovedescribed measures: Variety, Consistency, and Interactivity of phonetic productions). We tested the link between these four measures and looking times recorded in a wordform segmentation task. This task investigated the relative impact of consonant and vowel mispronunciations on the recognition of segmented word forms, thus measuring V-versus C-biases in the cohort (all details in 2.2.2).

2. Methods

>Methods

2.1. Participants

Thirty-one full-term, healthy 7-month-old infants (16 female and 15 male; Mage = 7 months and 5 days; range 7 months 0 days -7 months 28 days) were included in the study, thus falling in the age range in which the switch from the V-to the C-bias has been observed in French learners tested on the same task (Nishibayashi & Nazzi, 2016). All participants came from monolingual Frenchspeaking environments, defined such that parents estimated at least 90% exposure to French.

Participants had no reported family history of speech, language, or hearing disorders. Eight additional infants were tested but not included because of failure to obtain the infant's cooperation. Socioeconomic status was high in the group (with an average of four years of maternal higher education). Written informed consent to participate in this study was obtained from the participants' legal guardians. All methods were approved by the university ethics committee (CER-U Paris N° IRB: 00012021-01).

2.2. Procedure

Infants arrived at the lab and completed a perception task testing the relative weight of consonants and vowels in a wordform segmentation task using the head-turn preference procedure ('Conflict Task' from Nishibayashi & Nazzi, 2016). To further obtain our four production measures, daylong recordings of the infants' vocal productions were conducted with the LENA system (Consonant Variety, VMS, Vocalization Frequency), and the Vocal Reactivity Subscale of the Infant Behavior Questionnaire (Parade & Leerkes, 2008) was administered to parents. Methods for both the perception task and for obtaining production measures are described in more detail below.

2.2.1. Production skills: data collection and analysis Data collection

Daylong audio recordings were obtained from each participant using a LENA recorder placed in the chest pocket of a waistcoat worn by the infant (LENA Pro V3.4.0-143 r11538; Canault et al., 2016). Parents received the recorder during their visit to the lab, and were asked to record a full weekend day, within a maximum of two weeks from the lab visit, and on a day that did not include atypical and/or noisy activities. Parents received the instruction to switch the recorder on at the beginning of the day, when the infant woke up, and to leave it on until the infant went to bed at night. These instructions were provided both orally and on printed handouts.

Data analysis

To obtain Consonant Variety and Consistency of Consonant Production, we adapted prior methods (Laing & Bergelson, 2020). We selected three 30-minute segments for each participant from the three hourly time slots associated with the highest LENA Child Vocalization Counts (CVC). CVC is an automated estimate of canonical babbling based on the automatic detection of vocal productions having a syllabic structure made of one or more Consonant-Vowel (CV) sequences and not containing pauses in phonation that last more than 300 ms (Gilkerson & Richards, 2020 -note that the CVC estimation counts as one vocalization the utterance of a single CV syllable like "ba", but also equally counts as one vocalization the utterance of two or more CV syllables, like "baba", provided they are not segregated by more than 300 ms of silence). In the case of noisy recordings (30-minute segments containing more than 10 minutes of crying, feeding, noisy voice overlaps, or high-intensity noise such as tapping), we selected the second highest CVC segment available (this procedure was only needed for two participants out of 29). The selected audio segments were then used to calculate the measures of phonetic production described in section 1.1.1.

First, to calculate Consonant Variety (i.e., the number of different consonants produced), the three selected segments were listened to, and supraglottal consonant productions occurring in canonical CV syllables were manually annotated and tallied. We excluded all vegetative sounds, cries and laughs, as well as all vocalizations not having a CV structure, namely pre-babbling vocalizations not including Cs and C-like sounds not followed or preceded by a V. A consonant was included in a participant's inventory if it occurred at least twice in the analyzed sample (e.g., Ertmer et al., 2013).

Annotations were performed by two French-native independent listeners. Twenty percent of the annotations were proof-checked by an expert phonetician (the first author). Inter-coder agreement on phonetic inventories was substantial, with a mean Jaccard similarity index of 0.75 (SD = 0.17). When a sound was judged unclear by the two native French coders, it was removed from the analyses.

Second, to measure Consistency of Consonant Production, we assessed the presence of VMS following the methods in previous literature (e.g., DePaolis et al., 2011; Laing & Bergelson, 2020), i.e., we counted a consonant as a VMS if it occurred at least 50 times within one 30-minute time slot.

Only supraglottal consonants were tallied, and consonants sharing the same motor schema (i.e., differing only in the voicing parameter) were tallied in the same category (notably /p b/, /t d/, /k g/, following (McCune & Vihman, 2001). While previous studies on VMS have typically identified the development of the first VMS at 10 months of age, we decided to assess the presence of VMS in younger infants because long-form recordings are more ecological than traditional recording methods and increase external validity. Furthermore, long-form recordings allowed us to select time slots that maximize canonical vocalizations, thus enabling the measurement of the upper limits of individual production skills, whereas traditional lab-or home-based recordings involved more random sampling.

Third, Interactivity of Vocal Productions, or the degree to which the infants' vocalizations were embedded in interactive contexts, was measured by both the LENA's automatic count of the number of conversational turns (Conversational Turns Count, CTC) and a parental questionnaire: the Vocal Reactivity Scale of the Infant Behavior Questionnaire (IBQ, Parade & Leerkes, 2008). CTC estimates the number of conversational turns between the infant and the adults in the environment. It is defined as an alternation of vocal exchanges between the infant and an adult, enclosed by periods of silence (or containing sounds that are not human vocal sounds) that do not exceed the duration of 5 seconds (Ford et al., 2008). In turn, the Vocal Reactivity Scale (VR) asks parents to rate their infant's propensity to vocalize in a series of daily situations (feeding, dressing, bathing, playing, daily life activities). It quantifies how readily and intensely an infant vocalizes in response to stimulations in everyday situations. High Vocal Reactivity signals that the infant frequently and strongly vocalizes in response to events, while Low Vocal Reactivity signals that the infant tends to be quieter, less vocally responsive, and calmer in sound output. Thus, compared to the CTC, this index is specifically focused on the active role of the infant in early infant-caregiver vocal interactions. The VR scale was completed in person, during the visit to the lab.

Finally, Frequency of Canonical Vocalizations (the number of CV vocalizations) was measured by the CVC score calculated by the LENA algorithm. Specifically, for both CTC and CVC, we calculated the mean value per participant after manually tagging and subtracting all periods of infant sleep from the total duration of the recordings. Specifically, sleep periods were identified and tagged within the full recordings by two independent listeners (the first author and a research assistant whose native language is French). The duration of the sleep periods was then subtracted from the total recording duration, and the mean values of the CVC and CTC indices per participant were calculated based on the corrected duration.

2.2.2 Perception task testing the C-bias: wordform segmentation in a 'conflict situation'

To test the relative weight given to Cs and Vs, we used the Head-turn Preference Procedure (HPP) to conduct a speech segmentation experiment where participants are first familiarized with target words and then tested with consonant (C-MP) and vowel (V-MP) mispronunciations of these words (see the procedure in Nishibayashi & Nazzi, 2016; Experiment 3). This 'Conflict situation' has been consistently used for assessing consonant-vowel perceptual biases in infancy. The logic is that, if a C-bias is present, infants look longer at V-MP, being more prone to tolerate a change in V than in C, while, if a V-bias is present, infants look longer at C-MP, being more prone to tolerate a change in C than in V.

Stimuli

Thirty monosyllabic CVC target words were selected. All the words were either pseudo-words or words that are typically not included in infants' early lexicons (ex., benne, /bεn/, En. 'dumpster'; dette, /dεt/, En. 'debt'; cf. Bergelson & Swingley, 2015). For real words, word frequency was quantified using the database LEXIQUE 4.10 (New et al., 2004). The target words were chosen so that each word could be used both as a target word in the familiarization phase (ex., the pseudoword dan, /dan/ as target), or as a V-MP (dan as V-MP of the pseudoword den, /dεn/) or C-MP (dan as C-MP of the pseudoword ban /ban/). Targets and mispronunciations differed by a one-feature phonetic change. We selected four consonant contrasts (two on place and two on manner), and each was implemented in eight different conditions, using different target words and alternating the position of the contrast (word onset or coda). See Table I, Supplementary Materials for full details on the stimuli used. Since the present study aimed to assess a possible link between perception and production (babbling) in the development of the C-bias, we only used C falling in the range of consonants that are typically produced in early phonetic productions, i.e., stop and nasal consonants. Thus, the words used were not exactly the same as those in Nishibayashi & Nazzi (2016).

All but one of the 16 conditions were completed by two different participants. For each target word, a 6-sentence passage was created for use in the familiarization phase. Procedure

We used the same HPP procedure as Nishibayashi & Nazzi (2016), Experiments 3 and 4, Conflict situation). The experiment took place in a sound-attenuated booth containing a three-sided test booth made of pegboard panels. The test booth had two red lights and a loudspeaker (SONY xs-F1722) on each side and a green light at the center. All lights were at eye level for the participants.

A hidden camera was accommodated in a 5 cm hole below the center light. A PC terminal (Dell Optiplex computer), an audio amplifier (Marantz PM4000), a TV screen connected to the camera, and a three-button response box were located outside the booth. The experimenter monitored the participant's behavior on the TV screen and pressed the buttons of the response box according to the direction of the infant's head turns, thus starting and stopping the lights flashing and sound presentation. Orientation times were measured and recorded online. The experimenter and the caregiver wore earplugs and listened to music over tight-fitting headphones, preventing them from hearing the stimuli.

Infants were held on a caregiver's lap, and the caregiver was seated on a chair at the center of the test booth. Each trial began with the green light on the center panel blinking until the infant had oriented in that direction. Then, the center light was extinguished, and the red light above the loudspeaker on one of the side panels began to flash. When the infant turned the head at least 30° in the direction of the loudspeaker, the stimulus for that trial was played, while the red light continued to flash for the entire duration of the trial. Each stimulus was played to completion (i.e., when the six sentences of a given passage had been presented) or stopped immediately if the infant failed to maintain the 30° head turn for 2 seconds at least. If the infant turned away from the red light for less than 2 s, and then turned back again, the trial continued, but the time spent looking away was not included in orientation times. Thus, the maximum orientation time for a given trial was the duration of the entire speech sample. If for a trial, the infant's orientation time was shorter than 1.5 s, the trial was immediately replayed from the beginning and the initial orientation time was discarded.

Experimental sessions began with a familiarization phase during which infants heard two passages on alternating trials until they accumulated 30s of orientation times to each passage. When the infants reached the familiarization criterion for one passage, the second passage continued to be presented until its criterion was also reached. The side of the loudspeaker from which the stimuli were presented was randomly varied from trial to trial. The test phase began immediately after the familiarization criterion was reached and consisted of three test blocks, in each of which the four lists of isolated words were presented. The order of the lists within each block was randomized. Each infant was familiarized with two words embedded in passages. Then, each infant was tested with lists of isolated words containing, for each of the two familiarized words, one Vowel mispronunciation and one Consonant mispronunciation. Each infant completed one of 16 experimental conditions in which the selected consonant contrasts were implemented by using different target words and alternating the position of the contrast (word onset or coda, see Table I, Supplementary Materials).

Based on previous studies (ex., Nhisibayashi & Nazzi, 2016), we interpreted longer looking to V-MP versus C-MP in the test phase as establishing the presence of a C-bias (and vice versa).

3.1 Vocal production skills

The selected 30-minute time slots (one per infant) were extracted from hourly time slots having an average CVC index of 170 infant vocalizations per hour (range: 36 -378, SD = 73), corresponding to the highest CVC values per participant. 3.2 Relationships between consonant-vowel perceptual biases and vocal production skills

The dependent variable was Differential Looking Times, defined as the difference between looking times to V-MP and looking times to C-MP. Positive values indicate a preference for V-MPs of the target words, i.e., a C-bias; negative values show a preference for C-MPs, i.e., a V-bias (Nishibayashi & Nazzi, 2016).

The predictors were represented by our measures of phonetic production (Consonant Variety; Consistency of Consonant Production; Vocal Reactivity score; and the LENA's CTC) as well as the covariate, the LENA's CVC. Given the limited sample size (n = 31) and the high number of predictors, we performed a PCA with Varimax rotation on the four predictors of phonetic production to obtain a parsimonious representation of the predictor set and to reduce the risk of overfitting. The results revealed two main components (following the Kaiser criterion, i.e., retaining components with an eigenvalue > 1): Consonant Variety (loading = 0.88) and Consistency of Consonant Production (0.68) loaded primarily on PC1, whereas Vocal Reactivity (0.70) and CTC (0.78) loaded primarily on PC2. Together, PC1 and PC2 explained 61.9% of the variance (see Table II in the Supplementary Materials for full statistical details of the PCA). The two components reflected a grouping that is clear from a theoretical standpoint, PC1 representing a clustering of 'Early Phonetic Production Skills' (Consonant Variety and Consistency of Consonant Production) and PC2 representing a clustering of 'Interactivity of Vocal Productions' (VR scores and average CTC). We then derived composite scores based on the PCA-defined clusters by summing the mean-centered values of the component measures, thus preserving the relative contribution of each measure.

Analyses of the variable distribution showed that our covariate (CVC) was moderately rightskewed (p = .020, Shapiro-Wilk test; skewness = 1.16), with moderate kurtosis (2.74); it was therefore log-transformed prior to statistical analysis. The distribution was normal for all other variables (p > 0.05). Potential outliers were identified using the outlier labelling rule. Cook's distance was subsequently examined following Lin et al. (2026), leading to the identification of two influential points that were also outliers. Thus, the final statistical analyses included data from 29 participants.

All predictors were mean-centered before running the analyses.

Discussion

The present study asked whether early experience producing native phonetic patterns is linked to the early developmental switch from a V-to a C-bias observed in infants learning Romance languages (Bouchon et al., 2015; Nishibayashi & Nazzi, 2016; Von Holzen & Nazzi, 2020).

First, we established the absence of a generalized phonological bias -neither a V-nor a C-bias at the group level in 7-month-old infants. This is consistent with previous evidence that, at least in this task, the bias shifts between 6 and 8 months (Nishibayashi & Nazzi, 2016), with the absence of a significant bias at the group level being interpreted as high interindividual variability due to the ongoing switch.

Critically, our new finding is that Early Phonetic Production Skills or Interactivity of Vocal Productions did not predict a C-bias at the individual level, but rather, a significant interaction between the two predictors did. Importantly, this interaction (Fig. 2) was mostly driven by a significant, negative association between early phonetic production and the C-bias, exclusively for those infants who had more infrequent vocal interactions in their social environments. Conversely, infants who had frequent vocal interactions in social contexts showed a positive association between phonetic production and the C-bias. However, this association was not statistically significant.

Follow-up power analyses suggested that the lack of statistical significance here may be due to the small sample size tested.

Further analyses also revealed that the observed interaction was mainly driven by two of our measures: Variety of production and Vocal Reactivity scores. Thus, our results point to a significant negative association between the diversity of the speech motor patterns explored and the development of the C-bias, limited to the context of infrequent vocal interactions with the caregivers.

The stronger weight of Consonant Variety than Consistency of Consonant Production in the first composite score, Early Phonetic Production Skills, may be explained by the fact that infants showed little variation in Consistency (i.e., VMS number). In fact, most infants (17 of 29) had not yet developed a VMS, 11 had developed one, and only 1 had developed two. This, in turn, seems related to the early age of our participants, as the mastery of more than one VMS has been previously located towards the end of the first year of life (DePaolis et al., 2011). Hence, data obtained from our ecological, long-form recordings confirm the previously reported estimation. In turn, Vocal Reactivity had a stronger weight than CTC in the composite score representing Interactivity of Vocal Productions. Speculatively, this may be linked to the Vocal Reactivity score being a better indicator than LENA's CTC, given that the reliability of CTC against human annotation has been questioned (Cristia et al., 2021; Ramírez et al., 2021).

As to the significant negative association between Consonant Variety and the C-bias, found in infants having lower Vocal Reactivity, we interpret this result as follows. This negative relationship specifically suggests that, when Vocal Reactivity is low, exploring a large variety of production patterns (Consonant Variety) does not promote the switch to the C-bias. Vocal Reactivity, as a measure, puts a particular focus on the infant's readiness to use their vocal skills to interact. In turn, such propensity is known to be shaped by both individual factors (such as temperament, which can be measured by the IBQ test itself, of which the Vocal Reactivity scale is a subsection) and the tendency of the adults in the environment to interact vocally (Goldstein et al., 2003; Goldstein & Schwade, 2008). Vocal interactions with the caregivers provide: social reinforcement of vocalizations; online vocal feedback on the infant's own phonetic productions through reciprocal phonetic imitation (Messum & Howard, 2015); and visual-sensorimotor phonetic learning (Bastianello et al., 2022). Importantly, infants with reduced vocal interactions have fewer occasions to exploit these socially mediated phonetic learning mechanisms. The implication for our study is that, if such interactive learning pools are limited, exploring a wide variety of phonetic patterns may not ensure optimal learning of the corresponding phonological categories, due to a mismatch between active phonetic exploration and the limited availability of contingent social-vocal learning and feedback.

Vocal learning, a particular form of motor learning, specifically needs social-vocal interaction to develop and progress. Moreover, despite being driven by endogenous motivation, the infant's exploration of speech patterns is specifically modulated by social interaction; namely, it is in the social context that infants produce their more speech-like vocalizations (Long et al., 2022). Hence, if exploratory vocal behavior occurs in a context that offers limited opportunities for vocal interaction, phonetic production may not be positively associated with the consolidation of phonological representations. Specifically, infants who produce a wide range of consonant patterns but experience few vocal interactions may achieve poor entrenchment of the practiced patterns, which could account for the negative perception/production association observed here: within a limited social learning pool, focusing exploration on a more restricted range of motor patterns may allow infants to maximize phonetic learning during the relatively few interactive occasions offered by the environment.

It can also be observed that the scenario of infants exploring a wide variety of speech sounds while developing in low-interactivity environments is uncommon, following literature on language development (e.g. Tamis-LeMonda et al., 2014). This aspect of our results seems indeed to point to a possible bifurcation in early phonetic production between a purely sensorimotor endogenous aspect, i.e., babbling seen as a mechanism belonging to human species-specific motor development, and a socially dependent aspect, in which vocalizations are part of early interactions. In particular, the possible mismatch between high phonetic exploration and low vocal interaction raises the question of non-social factors contributing to early phonetic productivity, which may be endogenous, i.e., genetically linked to individual sensorimotor development, or exogenous, i.e., linked to any factor -apart from vocal interaction -that may favor infant oral motor exploration (e.g., feeding methods, patterns of pacifier use, etc.).

Conclusions

The present results add to the body of work on perception/production links in phoneticphonological development by specifically showing that perception/production associations can be modulated by social-vocal interaction. Moreover, perception/production associations can be negative if vocal interaction is scarce. To the best of the authors' knowledge, this is the first study reporting a negative perception/production link in early phonetic/phonological development, as well as to directly assess the role of social interaction in perception/production links.

A direct and general consequence of these results for nurturing practices is to highlight the importance of encouraging vocal exploration hand in hand with social opportunities for vocal interaction.
