Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard), also Internal Review Board - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term); not IEC followed by a number, which is a technical standard - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee/Commission, in the singular or plural - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee, Bioethics Committee/Commission, Ethikkommission - Ethical Review Authority/Unit/Office/Agency - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Human Research Protection Office/Program, Office for Human Subjects Protection - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee(s) on/for (Medical and Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt - Ethics review that was not needed: Ethics review was not required, did not require further ethical review, exempt from formal human research ethics review, no new institutional ethics review

A template line the authors have not filled in (“[Add ethics approval … here.]”) is not a statement.

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

When a statement is expected

The module sorts each paper by where its data came from (the internal .data_source(); the signs and how the rule was tested are described in R/data-source.R): - own: the authors collected data from people or animals, for this paper, in an earlier study of their own, or (a protocol) will collect them; - existing_records: records about individual people that the authors obtained for this study, such as hospital records or a cohort; - existing_unclear: existing data about people from a source the module could not identify; - open_resource: an open resource any researcher can use, such as UK Biobank or NHANES; - none: no data about people or animals (a review, simulation, commentary, theory, tutorial, coding of published papers, or figures about groups); - uncertain: the signs were missing or pointed both ways; - no_text: the paper has no text.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. A TRO uses them for each trov:TransparentResearchPerformance. * run_results$exit_status: the exit status of the process that ran the script (0 = ended normally); NA when the process did not report one (a time limit, or Docker failing to start the container). * artifacts: one row per file, with stage (deposit: as downloaded from the repository, hashed from the downloaded file; before_run: the run folder just before the first script ran, after metacheck’s own changes, such as rewritten paths and unpacked archives; after_run: only the files the scripts made (status new), changed (changed) or removed (removed), as an unchanged file is the one listed before the run), path (relative to the run folder, as valid UTF-8, a byte of another encoding written as <xx>), size (bytes), sha256, mime_type (by extension, NA when unknown), source (the file’s address in the repository, for deposit). The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). Files metacheck itself copied or made between passes (see modifications, output_copied, file_placed) show in after_run too. A TRO uses the rows as trov:ResearchArtifacts and each stage as a trov:ArtifactArrangement. * modifications$sha256_before, modifications$sha256_after: the hash of the changed script as downloaded and as it ran; NA when the script is not at the same path in both (a script made from an R Markdown file). * environment: the system that ran the code (trov:TransparentResearchSystem in a TRO): metacheck_version, started_at, ended_at, host (R version, platform, operating system), sandbox, network_isolated (TRUE only when every Docker run had --network none; the process sandbox does not stop the network), internet, timeout_per_script, total_timeout, python_local (the Python run outside Docker and its version), and docker: runs (for each kind of run, r_run, r_install, python_run, python_install, the image and the docker run arguments, such as --network none, --read-only, --cap-drop ALL, no-new-privileges, the process limit and the memory and processor limits) and images (for each image, its ID and digests from docker image inspect, its creation time and the R and Python versions its environment declares).

run_results$checked says what the run of each script shows about the code, in five groups, so that a script that does not reproduce is kept apart from one the check could not test: ran (it ran to the end); code_error (it did not reproduce: it stopped with an error of the code or the deposit, could not be read, or needs a file the deposit does not hold); dependency (a package or program was not available: a package that could not be installed or was not loaded, a program outside R and Python, a system library, or R crashing in a package’s compiled code); time (it did not finish within the time limit for one script, or was not started because the time for all scripts had run out); and not_checkable (it could not be checked automatically: it needs a person, the internet, a key or another setting of the authors’ computer, one platform such as Google Colab, Windows or OpenSAFELY, more memory than the run allows, a file that was not downloaded, a screen, or a program that runs it, or it is a file of a package of the code that Python runs only as a module of that package). It is NA for files run inside another script, files that are not an analysis, files not read, and a Docker failure. summary_table counts each group (repro_ran_n, repro_code_error_n, repro_dependency_n, repro_time_n, repro_not_checkable_n), and the report’s first sentence gives them. The traffic light reflects the static readiness signals by default; a script in code_error that stopped with an error makes it red, as a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure, and a script in one of the other three groups keeps it from being green. Calls that need a screen, which the run does not have (dev.print(), dev.copy2pdf(), dev.copy2eps(), savePlot(), windows(), win.graph(), x11(), quartz(), View()), are replaced before the run by calls that need none, which modifications records as screen_call_replaced. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

Background. Sustained contemplative practice confers well-documented psychological and physiological benefits, yet the experiential deepening that motivates practitioners can require many hours of practice, and mastery is not assured. Methods that accelerate training would broaden access to these benefits and could also test mechanism: the posterior cingulate cortex (PCC), caudate, and anterior insula are implicated in meditation, but only correlationally. We used low-intensity transcranial focused ultrasound (FUS) to ask whether reversibly perturbing each region shifts meditative experience in real time.

Methods. In a single-blind, sham-controlled, counterbalanced crossover design, long-term practitioners of insight meditation in the S.N. Goenka Vipassana tradition (LTMs; n = 36) and meditationnaïve novices (n = 12; an exploratory cohort; meditation experience not randomized) received 12 minutes of inhibition-biased (5% duty cycle) FUS to the PCC, bilateral caudate, or bilateral anterior insula, or sham, in separate sessions during hour-long meditation. Every ∼4 minutes, participants rated meditative depth (anchored from "extremely shallow" to "extremely deep") and intensity (relative to their normal experience, along any dimension), with autonomic, mood, and trait measures as secondary outcomes. Sham-relative changes in rating trajectories were tested with generalized linear mixed models and false discovery rate (FDR)-corrected contrasts.

Results. In LTMs, caudate stimulation produced a significantly steeper rise in depth than sham and insula stimulation; in novices, PCC stimulation produced a significantly steeper rise in depth than sham, caudate, and insula stimulation, such that the caudate-versus-PCC difference in depth slopes differed significantly between groups, reversing in sign. No target altered intensity relative to sham, insula stimulation had no sham-relative effect on meditation, and post-session mood and trait measures were unchanged. Autonomic effects were modest and did not account for the meditative effects, and expectation of benefit did not differ across conditions in LTMs.

Conclusions. FUS neuromodulation can causally and region-specifically accelerate meditative deepening, with the effective target differing by meditation experience (caudate in LTMs, PCC in novices), a dependence that may reflect practice itself or its correlates. Tempered by the single-blind protocol, the small novice cohort, and effects extending beyond sonication, these findings establish proof-of-principle for neuromodulatory facilitation of meditation and motivate individualized, closed-loop applications.

Introduction

Research has indicated that the practice of mindfulness can promote affective regulation and, in turn, ameliorate various traits associated with affective disorders, such as anxiety, anger, depression, fatigue, and confusion (Goyal et al., 2014; Speca et al., 2000; Travis et al., 2018). These improvements are complemented by attenuation of the biological manifestations of aging or deterioration often observed in those afflicted with such disorders (Creswell et al., 2012; Kang et al., 2013; Roberts et al., 2017; Schutte et al., 2020). However, successfully attaining mindful states through meditation requires consistent and prolonged practice over many years (i.e., meditative development, Galante et al., 2023); indeed, state mindfulness during meditation has been shown to improve as a function of lifetime meditation practice (Hauswald et al., 2015), and gains in well-being and mental health depend on both regular daily practice and accumulated practice experience (Bowles & Van Dam, 2025). Even then, mastery is not assured, as accumulated practice time alone does not equate to meditative expertise (Lieberman & Sacchet, 2026; Sparby et al., 2026). However, even with the help of specialized meditation-assistance techniques like mobile applications and neurofeedback (Huberty et al., 2019; Tal et al., 2026), aspiring practitioners often struggle to maintain a regular practice (Lomas et al., 2015). This is often particularly problematic for individuals who stand to benefit the most from meditation, such as those with depression (Crane & Williams, 2010), chronic pain (Hilton et al., 2017), or obsessive compulsive disorder (Lee et al., 2022), as they may find it particularly difficult to initiate and sustain a meditation practice due to a lack of motivation, difficulty concentrating, and intrusive negative thoughts.

Thus, there is a need for new methods that accelerate meditative development, enhance the effectiveness of training, and aid in the facilitation of mindful states with increasing ease so that the barriers to mindfulness training are reduced, enabling more people to experience the psychological benefits of this increasingly empirically validated practice. Direct neuromodulation of brain structures associated with facets of the meditative experience may be an effective method to facilitate the instantiation of mindful states and lead to more effective practice-mirroring the logic of extant clinical neuromodulation interventions targeting specific cognitive and/or affective processes (Cain et al., 2021a (Cain et al., , 2022;; Philip et al., 2019; Schiff, 2010). Neuromodulation also affords a complementary, mechanistic aim: whereas neuroimaging studies of meditation are correlational, transiently perturbing a candidate region and measuring the concurrent change in meditative experience can test whether that region is causally instrumental to it (Abellaneda-Pérez et al., 2024; Sacchet & Lieberman, 2026). We seek to test both notions with the use of focused ultrasound (FUS) neuromodulation, which is uniquely capable of targeting regions throughout the brain with a relatively high spatial precision (Blackmore et al., 2019) that far exceeds that of other non-invasive neuromodulation techniques, such as transcranial magnetic stimulation (TMS) and transcranial direct current stimulation (tDCS) (Bystritsky & Korb, 2015; Cain et al., 2021a). FUS is particularly relevant in this context as it can uniquely modulate the brain regions relevant for mindfulness (Brewer et al., 2011; Fox et al., 2014; Gard et al., 2015; Garrison et al., 2013 Garrison et al., , 2015;; Kral et al., 2019; Lenhart et al., 2020; Pagnoni et al., 2008; Tang et al., 2015) which include regions located below the cortical mantle and thus beyond the reach of TMS.

In this study, we recruited long-term meditators (LTMs) with extensive experience in insight meditation as well as meditation-naïve individuals (≤ 2 hours lifetime practice; hereafter, novices).

LTMs were recruited with experience in S.N. Goenka style Vipassana meditation (Hart, 2011; Zeng et al., 2014). This meditation emphasizes the systematic observation of bodily sensations. Vipassana meditation also emphasizes other individual mental phenomena to enhance present-moment awareness, encourage non-judgmental observation, and develop experience with the transience and subjectivity of mental phenomena. Practitioners employ both breath-focused awareness (anāpānasati in the liturgical language of Theravada Buddhism, from which this practice is drawn) and directed attention along consecutive points on the body (body-scan) while observing loci-specific bodily sensations as they arise and dissipate. The practice directs for non-engaging acknowledgement of thoughts and emotions as they inevitably arise. This permits meditators to resume anāpānasati or body-scanning after noticing such mind-wandering. Vipassana was specifically selected for this study due to its notable homogeneity in both practice and content, allowing exploration of impacts of modulating brain regions related to facets of both the practice and its effects.

Vipassana was also chosen because it has been shown to be relatively feasible and accessible for novices to learn and benefit from both cognitively and affectively (Chambers et al., 2008; Khoury et al., 2017; Krygier et al., 2013; Ostafin et al., 2006; Singh et al., 2024; Szekeres & Wertheim, 2015; Zeng et al., 2014).

Based on theoretical and empirical evidence, three brain regions were chosen as targets for neuromodulation of meditation: 1) posterior cingulate cortex (PCC), 2) bilateral heads of the caudate nucleus, and 3) bilateral ventral anterior insula. The PCC was chosen for its consistent association with meditative practices in terms of both its structure (Afonso et al., 2020) and function (Garrison et al., 2015). The PCC is a key component of the default mode network (DMN) and is consistently linked to self-referential thinking (Raichle, 2015), a process believed to be reduced or altered during mindfulness meditation (Bishop et al., 2004). Convergent evidence comes from psychedelic research, which, like meditation, is thought to act on self-referential processing (Sacchet et al., 2024): psilocybin acutely decreases PCC activity and medial prefrontal-PCC coupling (Carhart-Harris et al., 2012), and across studies, psychedelic-induced DMN modulation correlates with ego dissolution and improved well-being (Gattuso et al., 2023). Several canonical nodes in the DMN display reduced activity during meditation (Brewer et al., 2011; Garrison et al., 2015), while increased connectivity between DMN nodes has also been associated with prior meditation experience (Brewer et al., 2011; Jang et al., 2011). Compellingly, LTMs particularly exhibit a specific reduction in PCC activity during meditation (Brewer et al., 2011), and successful implementation of functional Magnetic Resonance Imaging (fMRI) real-time neurofeedback techniques to decrease PCC activity has yielded increased self-reports of "effortless awareness" (Garrison et al., 2013). The state of effortless awareness shares core attributes with mindfulness as it entails effortless and non-judgmental observation of experiences without striving or attempting to exert control over them. Most directly, a pilot study applying FUS to the PCC in meditation-naïve participants found within-group reductions in DMN connectivity after active, but not sham, sonication, alongside preliminary sham-relative changes in self-related experience (Lord et al., 2024). It is important to note that the goal is not merely to "turn off" the PCC but rather to develop greater flexibility and control over DMN and selfreferential activity through meditation training (Bauer et al., 2019). For instance, Kral et al. (2019) demonstrated that after 8 weeks of mindfulness-based stress reduction (MBSR), the PCC exhibited increased connectivity with the DLPFC at rest, suggesting a potentially greater ability to attend to task-related stimuli and increased control over self-referential processing and mind-wandering.

Neuroimaging studies of meditation also indicate the importance of the caudate nucleus. Grey matter volume within the caudate is reported to increase following the initial months of meditation training (Lenhart et al., 2020; Pickut et al., 2013), while long-term meditation practice is associated with increased functional connectivity between the caudate and the rest of the brain (Gard et al., 2015). The caudate nucleus is associated with a diversity of functions that are arguably involved in meditation. These functions include cognitive control, reward prediction, and motivation (Delgado et al., 2004; Grahn et al., 2008; Parro et al., 2018; Schultz, 2016). Clinical evidence supports this functional interpretation, as motivational disorders like athymhormia and abulia (a loss of initiative, spontaneous thought, and emotional responsiveness) are associated with lesions to the caudate (Bhatia & Marsden, 1994; Laplane & Dubois, 2001; Le Heron et al., 2018; Lévy & Dubois, 2006). Reduced reactivity in athymhormia parallels changes associated with the practice of mindfulness meditation, a correspondence proposed by S.Y., who likened the syndrome's conscious yet thought-free states to meditative concentration and suggested that reversible, ultrasoundinduced lesions might evoke them (Young, 2012; see also Sanguinetti & Young, 2020). Patients afflicted with athymhormia present with reduced movement, reduced motivation, and internal dialogue, but exhibit healthy behavior when presented with significant external stimuli, e.g., when motivated by a salient stimulus (Habib & Poncet, 1988; Luauté & Saladini, 2001). Also called psychic akinesia, it has been described as mental emptiness and indifferent behavior, even in the absence of cognitive impairment. This severe deficit of movement and motivated behavior presents without other, usually correlated effects, such as anxiety or pain (Bodini et al., 2004; Carota et al., 2002).

The lack of emotional and behavioral responses in athymhormia, even in situations that would typically cause distress (e.g., extreme hunger), can be readily likened to the ability to tolerate pain (Kabat-Zinn et al., 1985; Zeidan et al., 2010) and changes in motivation without affecting task performance (Hafenbrack & Vohs, 2018) that appear to be afforded to practitioners of mindfulness meditation.

Finally, the insular cortex-with regular specificity in the ventral anterior subregion-is consistently included in studies elucidating the neural correlates of meditation, reporting both functional and structural changes along multiple timescales (Davidson et al., 2003; Fox et al., 2014; Hasenkamp & Barsalou, 2012; Ives-Deliperi et al., 2011; Luders et al., 2009; Pagnoni et al., 2008; Tang et al., 2015). Indeed, a recent whole-brain meta-analysis spanning focused-attention, open-monitoring, mantra, and loving-kindness practices identified the insula among the most consistently activated regions across practice types (Baten et al., 2026), echoing an earlier meta-analysis in which the insula was the only region recruited by all four practice families examined (Fox et al., 2016). The insular cortex is a "high-order" cortical region with diverse functions, but has been robustly linked to processes typically associated with the meditative process, including emotional self-awareness (Craig, 2009) and regulation (Ghahremani et al., 2021; Kohn et al., 2014), mindfulness (Ives-Deliperi et al., 2011), and interoception (i.e., awareness of bodily sensations; Gasquoine, 2014; Pollatos et al., 2007). Vipassana meditation encourages mindfulness as well as the regulation of thoughts and emotions while also bringing attention to bodily sensations, arguably making its practitioners particularly likely to utilize such insula-supported functions, which has been supported by neuroimaging findings (Fox et al., 2014). Relatedly, it has been proposed (Sedlmeier et al., 2012) that enhancement of interoceptive processing (c.f. several studies using heartbeat perception task did not show differences between meditators and non-meditators, e.g., Khalsa et al., 2020) may in-part underlie some of the apparent clinical benefits of meditation and thus may represent an integral component of the practice (Khalsa et al., 2008; Melloni et al., 2013; Nielsen & Kaszniak, 2006; Otten et al., 2015). For example, blunted interoception, characterized by increased tolerance for aversive sensations and reduced activation of the insular cortex during attention to heartbeat sensations, has been implicated in suicidal behavior (DeVille et al., 2020)-while suicidal ideations appear ameliorated by meditation (Schmelefske et al., 2022; Wu et al., 2023).

In this single-blind, sham-controlled, crossover trial on separate groups of LTMs (n=36) and novices (n=12), we assessed the effects of neuromodulation (FUS) compared to sham, applied separately to three targets (PCC, bilateral caudate, bilateral anterior insula) during a one-hour long Vipassana meditation. The a priori sample-size determination targeted the LTM cohort (see Sampling Plan); the smaller novice cohort was included to test whether effects extend to the earliest stage of practice and is interpreted with corresponding caution. Meditative depth as well as broad changes in subjectivity (i.e., "subjective intensity") were repeatedly probed throughout the modulation period. A comprehensive assay of trait questionnaires was also administered at baseline and/or after every meditation session. These meditative and trait measures were used to assess the phenomenology of any altered subjectivity induced by modulation. This study also investigated the effects of FUS on several measures of physiological arousal during meditation: heart rate (HR), heart rate variability (HRV), respiration rate (RR), as well as Galvanic skin response (GSR, tonic and phasic). Though a 1:1 relationship between the parameters of FUS and a particular effect on neural tissue remains to be fully characterized, the parameter set we choose to employ has a low duty cycle of 5% (i.e., the percent of time during ultrasound pulsing that ultrasound is actively emitted continuously). Low duty cycles have been shown to consistently induce an inhibitory or disruptive effect on neural tissue (Dell'Italia et al., 2022; Plaksin et al., 2014 Plaksin et al., , 2016)), particularly cortical tissue, as demonstrated by studies that used a near-identical parameter set to that employed here, albeit with somewhat lower intensity (Cain et al., 2021a (Cain et al., , 2022)). These studies, alongside the inhibition of the caudate-encompassing striatum observed during thalamic sonication (Cain et al., 2022), provide strong support for the inhibitory effect of low duty cycles on neural tissue. Thus, we anticipate the FUS parameters employed here will temporarily disrupt the function of targeted structures.

Our primary research question was, "Does FUS modulation of the PCC, insula, or caudate, when compared to sham modulation, influence real-time changes in self-reported meditative depth or subjective intensity throughout a concurrent one-hour meditation in either LTMs or novices?" Based on the theory and empirical literature reviewed above, and given that we designed the FUS protocol to be inhibitory, we hypothesized differential effects across conditions such that, relative to sham, meditative depth would increase from PCC and caudate modulation, but decrease from insular modulation, whereas meditative intensity would increase from modulation of all three targets. We predicted that these effects would also replicate, although to a lesser degree, in the novice group.

The second research question was, "Does regional modulation produce sham-relative changes in mood, measured before and after each session, or differences in trait or phenomenology measured after each session?" We hypothesized a main effect of modulation condition with sham-relative effects for all outcome variables, for both the LTM group and the novice group to a lesser extent.

The third research question was, "Do individual trait characteristics moderate or influence any of the potential modulation effects on meditative depth and intensity or outcomes of mood and phenomenology?" We hypothesized that baseline trait measures would moderate modulation effects for all targets but not for the sham condition in both groups.

The fourth research question was, "Does FUS disruption of the PCC, insula, or caudate, when compared to sham modulation, influence changes in physiological measures of heart rate (HR), heart rate variability (HRV), respiration rate (RR), and galvanic skin response (GSR tonic or phasic) throughout a concurrent one-hour meditation in either LTMs or novices?" We hypothesized differential effects for the LTM Group such that, relative to sham, modulation of PCC and caudate would result in decreased HR and RR and increased HRV whereas insular modulation would produce opposite effects of increased HR and RR and decreased HRV. We predicted that these effects would also replicate, although to a lesser degree, in the novice group.

We also aimed to assess numerous interactive relations between measures from meditation (depth, intensity), physiology (HR, HRV, RR, and GSR tonic or phasic), and traits. In both groups, we hypothesized that individual traits would moderate modulation effects on physiology, other traits, and mood in both groups, and we hypothesized that physiological changes during modulation would moderate modulation effects on meditative depth and/or intensity. In the LTM Group only (due to sample size), we tested several hypotheses: 1) that physiological changes during modulation would either moderate or mediate modulation effects on trait and mood; 2) we hypothesized bidirectional influences, as moderation and/or mediation, between meditative and physiological changes during concurrent modulation and meditation; and 3) we hypothesized that meditative depth and/or intensity would moderate or mediate modulation effects on traits or mood.

Methods

Participants

Inclusion and Exclusion Criteria (LTMs).

The ability to detect phenomenological subtleties within the meditative state as mandated by this study requires subjects to have significant longterm meditation experience. To control for differences across meditation practices, we only recruited subjects over the age of 21 who selected Vipassana from a list of other meditation practices.

Specifically, participants must have reported a history of practicing Vipassana meditation for at least ten minutes a day, for at least five days a week over the last five years on their screening survey.

Inclusion and Exclusion Criteria (Novices).

To ensure participants were truly meditation-naïve, individuals were excluded if they had any prior meditation experience exceeding 2 hours total lifetime exposure. Additionally, participants were excluded if they had used any meditation applications (e.g., Insight Timer, Calm, Headspace) or engaged in mind-body practices including yoga, tai chi, Aikido, breathwork, or other contemplative movement practices. Novices thus entered the study meditation-naïve and completed approximately 3 hours of guided Vipassana practice across four standardized training sessions before testing (see Meditation Training for Novices).

Inclusion and Exclusion Criteria (All).

Since ultrasound cannot be reliably applied in certain scenarios (e.g., in the case of air gaps between the transducer and skull), individuals were excluded if they had hairstyles, such as dreadlocks, that prevent adequate parting of the hair near the site of ultrasound coupling to the head. Due to the need for magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), participants were also excluded if they had metal implants or piercings that were not MR-safe, or if they were claustrophobic. Similarly, to avoid unpredictable interactions with non-biological tissue, subjects were excluded if they had permanent implants, of any material, in regions that may reasonably receive significant ultrasound energy. Participants with a history of neurological (e.g., epilepsy or stroke) or psychiatric (e.g., bipolar or schizophrenia) disorders were excluded along with those taking any prescribed medication that affect the central nervous system (e.g., psychostimulants, antidepressants, antipsychotics). Pregnant individuals and those with hypotension were also excluded. To reduce the risk of infectious disease (e.g., sars-cov-2), all subjects were temperature checked (using an infrared forehead thermometer) upon arriving at the testing location.

Subject Confidentiality.

Upon signing up for the study, participants were assigned a unique subject number in Castor EDC. Only the Principal Investigator retained administrative access to the subject names associated with the subject numbers, requiring two-factor-authentication.

All subsequent programs used to collect imaging, bioperipherals, and task data used the subject number and no other lookup tables were created to associate the subject number with personally identifiable information. All behavioral questionnaires were collected using Castor EDC. We use Castor EDC due to its ability to store sensitive personal and research data securely. Castor EDC is compliant with 21 CFR part 11, ICH e6 GCP, GDPR and HIPAA. Castor EDC is also ISO27001 and ISO9001 certified.

Compensation. Participants were compensated $30 per hour via cash or Venmo, prorated to the nearest 15 minutes; see Supplemental Methods SM1.

Materials

Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI).

Participants scheduled an MR-safety screening with a medical doctor (author A.B.) and, upon approval were scheduled for a 30-minute MR scan (3T-Siemens), including a T1-weighted structural (MPRAGE), functional, and diffusion-weighted scans (see Supplemental Methods SM2).

Focused Ultrasound (FUS) and Neuronavigation.

FUS was administered using single-element transducers (BrainSonix Pulsar 1002, BrainSonix Corporation, Sherman Oaks, CA, USA) with three focal distances, allowing depths of 35mm (for insula target), 65mm (for PCC and caudate targets), or 80mm (alternative for PCC target dependent on individual head size and brain morphology assessed during neuronavigation). Neuronavigation (Brainsight, Rogue Research Inc., Montreal, QC, Canada) enabled accurate FUS targeting of brain regions via real-time alignment between physical and virtual (MRI) spaces. To minimize movement, participants were head-stabilized in a chair specifically designed for neuromodulation (4th Generation Subject Chair by Rogue Research; Figure 1B). Reclined with their feet extended, a chair-mounted chin rest helped maintain their head-position for the duration of sonication (Figure 1A). Brainsonix gel pads were used to ensure the transfer of ultrasound from the transducer, through 0° or 5° angled gel pads depending on trajectory, into the subject's cranium. For the sham condition, non-transmitting gel pads from Brainsonix were used, which prevent passage of ultrasound into the brain, while still retaining the soft audible buzz and light mechanical pressure associated with the non-sham conditions. Physiological Recording. Bioperipheral data were collected with the Aim II system (Cognionics, Inc.), which included electromyography (EMG) measurements of the left and right sternocleidomastoid muscles, respiration rate via bio-impedance on the left and right pectoralis major, which simultaneously collected electrocardiogram (ECG) data, and electrodermal activity (EDA) from the non-dominant palm. Prior to attaching all electrodes (Skintact Inc.), the skin was prepared with isopropyl alcohol.

Task and Behavioral Recording. Participant responses to in-task probes were recorded using a Bluetooth finger-worn input device fitted over the pointer finger of the dominant hand, on which participants made between one and five sequential button presses to answer each probe.

Auditory task elements were presented via Experiment Recorder (Intheon, La Jolla, CA, USA), and all measurement time series (bioperipheral, presentation, and response streams) were collected in temporal alignment using Lab Streaming Layer (LSL; Kothe et al., 2025). Full details of the questionnaire platform, presentation software, stimulus preparation, and recording hardware are provided in Supplemental Methods SM3.

Questionnaires.

A custom demographics questionnaire collected information about age, sex, meditation experience (frequency, duration, number of years, number of retreat days), as well as baseline sleep (quality, hours) and caffeine (cups) during the prior month. A custom preexperimental questionnaire collected information about sleep (quality, hours) and caffeine (cups) immediately prior to the session. Another in-house questionnaire (see Table 1), administered after each experimental session, probed qualities specific to theory surrounding each target region as well as questions about expectations and sham detection. Trait and/or phenomenology questionnaires included the NEO Five-Factor Inventory (NEO-FFI; Openness domain; Costa & McCrae, 1992), Five-Facet Mindfulness (FFMQ; Baer et al., 2008), Nondual Awareness Dimensional Assessment Trait Scale (NADA-T; Hanley et al., 2018), Ryff Scale of psychological well-being (Ryff, 1989), Multidimensional Assessment of Interoceptive Awareness questionnaires (MAIA; Mehling et al., 2012), Profile of Mood States (POMS; Terry et al., 2003), Body Sensations Questionnaire (BSQ; Chambless et al., 1984), Ego Dissolution Inventory (EDI; Nour et al., 2016), Toronto Mindfulness Scale (TMS; Lau et al., 2006), and the Meditation Depth Index (MEDI; Piron, 2001).

Procedure

Overview. The general experimental procedure for this within-subjects repeated-measures design involved each participant in one neuroimaging session (MRI and initial behavioral questionnaires) followed by four experimental sessions consisting of neuromodulation, meditation, and behavioral questionnaires, resulting in a total of five separate visits to the research site (∼11.5 hours). During experimental sessions, the MRI data were used to neuronavigate the positioning of ultrasound transducers on the participant's head for modulation of target brain regions while the participant was engaged in closed-eye meditation practice. Participants' self-reported ratings of meditative depth and intensity were collected during each session and synchronized with experimental events. Behavioral questionnaires were given before and after each experimental session. Within each participant, experimental sessions were scheduled to ensure the start time of each session did not vary by more than 2 hours.

Meditation Training for Novices.

Prior to the neuroimaging session, novice participants engaged in four remote meditation training sessions spanning approximately two weeks (approximately three days between sessions). Before the first session, participants received a preparatory slide deck by email outlining the experiment, session structure, practice recommendations, and compensation (Supplemental Methods SM7). The remote training phase began with an introductory 75-minute webinar conducted via Zoom (Zoom Video Communications Inc., San Jose, CA, USA), during which a research assistant introduced the study and provided basic meditation instruction, after which participants completed a 1-hour guided Vipassana meditation session (Can You Zen, 2021). Following this initial session, participants completed a 15-minute survey administered through the Castor study platform. Participants then had six days to independently complete two 30-minute guided Vipassana meditation sessions at home (Can You Zen, 2020), each followed by a 15-minute questionnaire designed to assess natural individual variations in meditation practice and confirm session completion. These independent sessions were scheduled with specific due dates, and participants received reminder notifications 24 hours prior to each deadline. Both guided recordings follow the structure of Goenka-style practice, opening with Ānāpāna (breath awareness) and proceeding to a guided, part-by-part body scan attending to changing bodily sensations, such that novices were trained in the two core techniques practiced by LTMs. Throughout training, participants were instructed to sit upright with a straight spine and eyes closed in a quiet, distraction-free location, to minimize physical movement (in preparation for the stillness required during experimental sessions), to practice at approximately the same time of day (preferably within 30 minutes of waking), and to avoid meditating immediately after eating. Failure to complete any required session during the training portion resulted in removal from the study. The remote phase concluded with a final 75-minute proctored webinar featuring the same 1-hour guided meditation session and survey completion, during which the lab manager observed participants to determine eligibility for the onsite phase; participants who showed signs of disinterest or lack of concentration, moved excessively, or appeared unable to commit to the study schedule were not advanced (full webinar protocol in Supplemental Methods SM7).

Neuroimaging session (Visit 1).

Following recruitment, informed consent, MRI safety questionnaire / medical doctor consultation, and subject screening, participants arrived at the study location where they were temperature screened before being directed to the MR suite on the first floor of study location's building to collect structural images for later stereotaxic targeting and ultrasound modelling (30 minutes; See Supplemental Methods SM2). Afterward, participants returned to the research site and completed an assay of trait behavioral questionnaires (i.e., baseline questions about sleep/coffee and meditation experience, as well as the NEO-FFI, FFMQ, NADA-T, Ryff, and MAIA; see Materials: Questionnaires section). Upon completion, participants were shown to this study's experimental room and situated in a padded chair with their head secured using a chin and headrest (Brainsight), simulating future experimental sessions. This initial familiarization facilitated subject comfort and minimized setup time for the experimental sessions.

Preparation of Brain Targets. Following the neuroimaging session, target regions -the PCC, bilateral heads of the caudate, and bilateral anterior ventral insula -were marked on each participant's T1-weighted image. Each participant's brain was registered to standard MNI space (nonlinear warp, FNIRT; Andersson et al., 2007), and the inverse transform was applied to custom MNI-space ROI masks to bring them into subject space, where they were added to the T1-weighted image as high-intensity points; anatomical accuracy was visually confirmed (J.C.). Brainsight neuronavigation configuration files were then created from these targets and anatomical reference points (see Preprocessing: MRI data and Figure 2 for the full procedure). (2) The resulting subject-to-MNI registration was inverted to yield an MNI-to-subject inverse transform. (3) Custom MNI-space regions of interest (red spheres) were propagated into subject space via this inverse transform and added to the native T1-weighted image as high-intensity points (PCC shown here as a bright sphere). Targets were then defined in Brainsight by placing a marker at the centroid of each spherical hyperintensity.

Experimental Sessions (Visits 2-5)

Questionnaire. Upon arrival, subjects completed the pre-experimental questionnaire (i.e., baseline sleep/caffeine) and the POMS questionnaire.

Registration. Next, participants' heads were registered to their T1-weighted MRI image using Brainsight. Specifically, a fiducial marker was placed on the head (central forehead) of the participant and their body was positioned such that a) they could lay comfortably in the provided chair without needing to move for the duration of the experiment and b) the fiducial marker was clearly visible to the infrared camera. Standard landmarks on the participant's head (nasion, corners of each eye, and tragus of the ear) were used to register this "participant tracker" fiducial to the participant-specific MRI. Following this, accuracy was assessed using Brainsight's validation process; if needed, more points on the skin were registered until registration accuracy was deemed to be below an error of 2 mm across 10 semi-arbitrary points which span five sides (front, back, left, right, top) of the head.

Hardware Application.

Participants were then fitted with the HID (see Figure 1D). Participants were asked once again about their comfort levels and were secured in place using chin and head supports (see Figure 1A,B). Ultrasound gel (Aquasonic, Parker Laboratories Inc., Fairfield, NJ, USA) was then applied to the pre-determined ultrasound entry point in an area readily subsuming the diameter of the transducer and was rubbed into any hair present so that no hair permeated the gel layer (see Figure 1H). This procedure minimizes air bubbles and ensures a smooth surface for coupling, meeting the assumptions of numerical ultrasound modelling used to plan these methods (see modelling below). A thin layer of ultrasound gel was applied to the surface of the appropriate transducer and bubbles were similarly smoothed from this layer (see Figure 1F,G).

The appropriate gel pad (angle, sham condition) was then selected and applied to the transducer and any air bubbles between it and the transducer surface were carefully smoothed away (see Figure 1F). The transducer was then coupled to the head with gel/gel pad filling any space between the transducer and the head (see Figure 1B,I). Next, the device was positioned such that the theoretical focus of the transducer lays on the chosen target. In the rare case where the focal depth of the chosen transducer was less appropriate given subject head morphology (visible on Brainsight), the alternative transducer (e.g., 65 mm vs. 80 mm) was substituted and the above procedure repeated. Once positioned, the transducer(s) was locked in place using the chair's mounts and their position relative to the subject's head, captured in the Brainsight software (see Figure 1E) for later modelling of ultrasound propagation through subject-specific skull-models. The chosen trajectory was maintained throughout the modulation procedure such that the real-world focal point did not deviate from the chosen target point by greater than 3 mm (see Figure 1E) for more than 5 seconds of active modulation, otherwise, the trial was terminated and repeated on another day.

Data Collection Period.

Following setup, participants were asked to close their eyes and rest for five minutes, with encouragement to "mind wander" of their own accord, making sure they are not actively engaged in their meditative practice. Afterward, they were asked to close their eyes, remain still, and engage in their standard meditation practice (prompted with a "gong" sound) for a period of 60 minutes. LTMs were instructed only to engage in their habitual Goenka-style practice, without further guidance; in this tradition, a sitting typically begins with Ānāpāna (sustained attention to the breath at the nostrils), proceeds to Vipassana proper (systematic, part-by-part observation of bodily sensations while maintaining equanimity), and may close with mettā (lovingkindness) (Hart, 2011). In a free-response description of their meditation process completed after each session, all participants confirmed that they engaged in their traditional (LTMs) or instructed (novices) style of Vipassana practice consisting of both breath-focused awareness ( Ānāpānasati) and body-scanning techniques. Subjects were provided with the information that, following minute 12 of their meditation, the ultrasound device was activated for a 12-minute period (10-second on-off blocks). In cases where modulation was bilateral (caudate and insula), modulation was performed for six minutes to each hemisphere sequentially (order counterbalanced). Throughout the one-hour meditation period, participants were presented with an auditory prompt (recording of a female voice; T.B.) every ∼four minutes (random ±30 s jitter) presenting two questions to which subjects responded by making between one and five sequential button presses on the HID: 1) "Please rate the depth of your meditation since your last response on a scale from 1-5"; 2) "Please rate the intensity of your experience since your last response on a scale from 1-5". Following each question, and a 5-second silent response period after each question, the recording played "you may now resume your meditation". Before the first experimental session, participants received written anchors for both ratings in a preparatory slide deck, which were reviewed again during setup. Depth was anchored as1 = extremely shallow,2 = very shallow,3 = neither shallow nor deep,4 = very deep, and5 = extremely deep; intensity was anchored relative to each participant's normal experience, as1 = much less intense,2 = less intense,3 = no different,4 = more intense, and5 = much more intense than normal. Participants were instructed to base both ratings on their own experience since the previous probe and, after responding, to immediately "resubmerge," attempting to attain deeper meditative states. Depth thus referred specifically to the meditative state, whereas intensity indexed the degree to which experience, along any dimension, departed from normal, irrespective of its meditative quality. Because every comparison was within participant (each participant rated all four conditions), idiosyncratic interpretation of these anchors was held constant across conditions and, absent condition-specific shifts in scale use, could add noise but not condition-specific effects.

Counterbalancing. Sessions differed only by which target was selected and whether a nontransmitting gel pad was used (sham condition). The order of Target A, Target B, Target C, or Sham (positioned either towards Target A, B, or C) were counterbalanced between participants with each non-sham modulation site appearing equally likely at each session. If data from a subject was excluded for any reason, the condition schedule assigned to that subject was reassigned to a future participant to maintain the counterbalancing schedule for final analysis; this includes enrolling additional participants if necessary.

Sham Condition and Blinding.

We used a single-blind design with a counterbalanced sham condition such that, for each participant, one of three brain targets was chosen to also be used for sham. Sham modulation involved the same modulation protocol except for the inclusion of a non-transmitting gel pad fitted onto the transducer so that no ultrasound energy could pass through.

The small audible buzzing that the transducer produces while stimulating was still audible during sham, while all other sensations associated with the ultrasound equipment (pressure, vibration) were similar between sham and real modulation. Experimenters also carefully and correctly aimed the transducer(s) during the sham condition, ensuring that this process took the same amount of time and effort as during real modulation. A key difference between modulation and sham was that the sham gel pad was opaque in contrast to the translucent, transmitting gel pads used during modulation, which was necessary in order to visually detect and remove small air bubbles from under the gel pad for optimal modulation. No translucent non-transmitting gel pads were available to use for sham, thereby precluding the possibility of a double-blind design for data collection and analysis. Though, it should be noted that none of the dependent variables readily introduce experimenter bias during data analysis and all data analysis was conducted in an entirely automated fashion independent of experimental condition. After each session, participants were asked to guess if the session was sham or not (see in-house questions in Materials section).

Data Exclusion Procedure.

In the case of technical error during a session, the session was repeated up to one additional time, so long as the error was noticed prior to completion of data collection for a given subject and the counterbalancing regime was maintained. In the case that the order of conditions was altered due to the repeating of a session, a particular condition schedule was exchanged with a future subject only if it was possible to maintain the counterbalancing regime. Technical error requiring the repetition of a session and/or disregarding data included anything that clearly reduced the validity of independent or dependent variables. For instance, if it is discovered that our ultrasound device was not on target (by at least 3mm) or was not turned on during the experiment, that would constitute grounds for removing data or repeating that session.

Similarly, if dependent variables (e.g., depth) were not collected for some reason, we may repeat a session. However, factors which only introduced moderate variability into the dataset cannot be used to justify data exclusion, for example if a subject complained about being more fatigued during a session, so long as the independent variable was administered appropriately and the dependent variables were collected.

FUS Parameters

Ultrasound was delivered at a fundamental frequency of 0.65 MHz, chosen to balance transmission through the human skull against focal spatial precision while preserving a conservative safety margin (Blackmore et al., 2019). To bias modulation toward inhibition, we used a low (5%) duty cycle -a 100 Hz pulse repetition frequency with 0.5 ms pulse widths (Figure 3) -because lower duty cycles tend to favor suppression and higher duty cycles excitation, independent of intensity (Dell'Italia et al., 2022; Plaksin et al., 2016). This exact pulse schedule and frequency have produced acute inhibitory effects during simultaneous fMRI in prior human work using the same system (Cain et al., 2021a (Cain et al., , 2021b (Cain et al., , 2022)). Modulation intensity was 30 W/cm 2 I sppa and 1.5 W/cm 2 I spta , corresponding to a mechanical index (MI = P/ √︁ f ) of 1.085. These free-field values and their estimated in-skull (in situ) counterparts, alongside FDA diagnostic limits, are shown in Table 2. Although the free-field I spta exceeds the FDA diagnostic-imaging guideline (0.72 W/cm 2 ), the estimated in situ I spta (0.12-0.19 W/cm 2 ), I sppa , and MI all fall below FDA limits. More importantly, current consensus for transcranial ultrasound stimulation does not treat I spta as a safety metric, instead defining non-significant risk by mechanical criteria (MI or transcranial MI ≤ 1.9) and thermal criteria (e.g., temperature rise ≤ 2 °C, absolute temperature ≤ 39 °C, or thermal-index-dependent exposure limits; Aubry et al., 2025; Martin et al., 2024). Our free-field MI (1.085) and estimated in situ MI (0.30-0.39) satisfy the mechanical criterion with a wide margin. For the thermal criteria, conservative analytical estimates (Supplemental Methods SM4) yielded a cranial thermal index (TIC) of 0.45-0.70 under nominal assumptions and at most 1.87 under compounded worst-case assumptions, values for which ITRUSST permits at least 80 minutes of exposure (versus our 12-minute protocol), and a peak focal temperature rise in brain of at most 0.6 °C even when neglecting perfusion. Direct evidence further supports safety at and above our exposure: MR thermometry with the same system at derated intensities up to 5.76 W/cm 2 I spta (approximately five-fold our derated exposure) detected no measurable in situ temperature rise (Schafer et al., 2021); histological examination of ex vivo human brain tissue found no injury from pulsed sonication, nor from continuous-wave sonication below 14 W/cm 2 (non-derated) I spta (Spivak et al., 2021); a pre-surgical study in patients with temporal lobe epilepsy, using 650 kHz sonication that included a 5% duty-cycle paradigm at derated intensities up to 5.76 W/cm 2 I spta , found no sonication-related histological damage in seven of eight resected specimens, with one inconclusive (Stern et al., 2021); using the same device and pulse timing at 4-9-fold higher intensities, 1-minute sonications raised skin-interface temperature by at most 1.45 °C without skin injury (Bishay et al., 2024); and animal models show no damage at intensities far exceeding ours (Dallapiazza et al., 2018; Gaur et al., 2020), consistent with a systematic review reporting no adverse effects even in studies that exceeded at least one FDA safety index (Pasquinelli et al., 2019).

Sampling Plan

Sample size was determined a priori from power analyses (G*Power; Erdfelder et al., 1996) targeting a conservative effect size midway between moderate and large (Cohen's d = 0.65; Cohen, 1988) at 95% power -a benchmark chosen so that any modulation effect would be large enough to meaningfully aid meditation relative to the sham/placebo condition (Hengartner & Plöderl, 2018; Hwang et al., 2017; Yih et al., 2019). Across our outcome families, these analyses converged on a required sample of 36 participants. This power analysis was originally motivated by our planned analysis framework of repeated-measures MANOVA with follow-up ANOVAs and one-sided t-tests; we ultimately determined that generalized linear mixed models (GLMMs) were the most appropriate approach for these data (see Statistical Methods), and we consider the original sample-size determination to remain valid in this setting, as the targeted effect size and resulting N were not specific to the planned test family and mixed-effects models generally afford comparable or greater sensitivity.

Full power-analysis derivations for each outcome family -offline questionnaire measures, the online meditative ratings, and the physiological measures -are provided in Supplemental Methods SM5.

Analysis Preprocessing

Behavioral Data. The following preprocessing procedure was applied to each session for each subject in each group. Any timestamps created by the physiological recordings starting before the meditation task onset or ending after the task offset were removed. The meditation ratings (depth and intensity), which were reported on an ordinal scale from 1 (lowest) to 5 (highest), were preprocessed by assigning "5" to any values greater than 5 (i.e., accidental duplicate button press) and by assigning "NA" to any values of 0 (i.e., no response) to be excluded from all subsequent analyses. The meditation ratings occurred approximately every four minutes (with a 30 second jitter) during each 1-hour session. Due to the jitter, some four-minute time bins contained more than one reporting period for some subjects. To account for this, each hour-long session was first discretized into six-second time bins. All six-second time bins occurring before a given report but after the previous report were assigned the value of the given report. The data were then resampled into four-minute bins, containing the average depth or intensity values across all six-second bins within that four-minute bin. This resulted in a final timecourse of 15 four-minute bins for each session which comprised the first three bins (i.e., average ratings) prior to ultrasound, three bins during ultrasound, and nine bins after ultrasound. All subsequent analyses involving depth or intensity ratings were either based on all 15 bins (i.e., time period "All") or focused on the 12 bins during and after ultrasound (i.e., time period "FUS+"). The FUS+ window included the postsonication period because the effects of low-intensity FUS on neural activity and subjective state can outlast sonication by tens of minutes in humans (Lord et al., 2024; Sanguinetti et al., 2020; Yaakub et al., 2023b) and by more than an hour in non-human primates (Verhagen et al., 2019); effects within this window are therefore attributable to FUS but not necessarily to acute, online modulation during sonication (see Limitations). Modulation effects were modeled as sham-relative differences in the rate of change over time (Condition × Time) rather than as baseline-referenced level contrasts, because meditative depth changed systematically across the session in every condition, including sham, such that the timing-matched sham condition, rather than the pre-sonication baseline, provides the appropriate reference for this within-session trajectory.

MRI data.

Masks depicting each region of interest (ROI) (bilateral caudate, bilateral ventral anterior insula, and PCC) were generated in MNI space by creating 5mm binary mask spheres over the pre-determined target voxels. The (left and right) caudate spheres represent the geometric center of the head of the caudate (Harvard-Oxford), while the same method was used to determine the position of the ventral anterior insula sphere (Faillenot et al., 2017). The PCC ROI was derived from the point of greatest statistical significance found in a previous fMRI study of changes experienced during meditation (Garrison et al., 2015). These spherical masks were created using FSL tools (Jenkinson et al., 2012) and in-house bash scripts.

T1-weighted images were first brain extracted using OptiBET (Lutkenhoff et al., 2014), which produces a non-linear conversion to MNI (2mm) space using FSL tools with optimized settings. The matrices describing this transformation are then inverted and used to move MNI-space masks into subject space over the appropriate region. The transformation from MNI space and the location of masks are visually inspected for accuracy prior to the first experimental session of any subject.

Binary masks are multiplied by the maximum intensity of the given T1-weighted image and added to the image such that a highly visible spot of hyperintensity is apparent over each region, guiding where to place markers in the Brainsight software for targeting using focused ultrasound. These markers are placed in the geometric center of each of these hyperintensities. These images are only used for targeting while the original T1-weighted images are used for any other MRI analysis steps. This procedure is depicted in Figure 2.

Physiological Data.

All signal processing was conducted with custom pipelines built in Neu-roPype Suite's Pipeline Designer program (Intheon, La Jolla, CA, USA), which allows for the construction of unique analyses using pre-designed purpose-built "nodes" built on python scripts.

The physiological measures were initially recorded at 500Hz timestamps and were preprocessed in the following steps. For cardiac metrics, the 'ECG' channel on the AIM device was extracted, downsampled to 125 Hz, bandpass-filtered between 1-30 Hz using our R peak detection based on the Pan-Tompkin algorithm with a 10 second sliding window, and then used the R peaks to calculate the heart rate (HR) as beats per minute, heart rate variability (HRV) as standard deviation of N-N intervals (SDNN), and respiration rate (RR). These computed metrics use the same timestamps as the R peaks, which is close to 1 Hz, but not exact. Next, these metrics were interpolated to the galvanic skin response (GSR) timestamps (resampled at 125 Hz) and all metrics concatenated together. Using a 1 second sliding epoch window, the values were averaged to create final output at an effective 1 Hz rate. The GSR tonic and GSR phasic metrics were prepared with a simple method used also by Biopac. The downsampled (125Hz) raw GSR data were Z-scored to normalize across subjects/sessions. The phasic component was extracted with a 2 nd order IIR butterworth high-pass filter (0.05Hz). The tonic component was extracted with a 2 nd order IIR butterworth low-pass filter (0.05Hz). Both components were then downsampled to 1Hz using same timestamps.

Next, data cleaning of the physiological measures in the 1Hz frequency was performed as follows.

For HR, HRV, and RR data, all negative values were assigned "NA" to be excluded from subsequent analyses. For these three measures, cutoff ranges were defined and applied (i.e., assigning NA to values outside of range) -HR (30-180), HRV (10-300), RR (10-60) -based on histograms to exclude extreme outliers, artifactual, or biological implausible values (Shaffer & Ginsberg, 2017).

Additionally, for HRV data, since it's derived from the same HR data, "NA" was assigned to any HRV values that had corresponding negative HR values, as well as any HRV values that had corresponding HR values outside of the cutoff range. Next, for these three measures (HR, HRV, RR), additional outliers were assigned "NA" based on a threshold of the median + 4 * median absolute deviation (MAD), separately for each session and subject. Extreme/outlier values for the Z-scored GSR phasic and tonic data were defined with cutoff ranges (GSR phasic: -0.10 to 0.10; GSR tonic: -3 to 2.5) based on visual inspection of histograms by session. Finally, the cleaned physiological data were further downsampled to match the same binning frequency as the meditation ratings (i.e., averaged within the same 4 minute intervals, 15 timestamps per 1-hour session) for all subsequent analyses, which were based on the same time periods as the behavioral data (i.e., time period "All" or "FUS+").

Statistical Methods

Below we summarize the variables, models, and analyses used; full model specifications, including complete variable lists, covariate handling, software, and diagnostics, are provided in Supplemental Methods SM6.

Independent variables.

Primary IVs were Time (either all timepoints or during FUS+ period), Condition (caudate, insula, PCC, sham), and/or Stim (FUS -the average of the three active conditions -vs. sham). Secondary analyses of outcome measures collected before (pre) or after (post) the experimental sessions were tested with an alternative Timepoint (pre, post) factor. Covariates for demographics, placebo/expectation, and prior-state measures were included where warranted (SM6).

Dependent variables.

Primary DVs were meditative depth and intensity (1-5) and the physiological measures HR, HRV (SDNN), RR, and GSR (phasic and tonic); secondary DVs were post-only trait/phenomenology measures, pre/post POMS mood measures, and blinding/placebo, in-house, and sensation items (full DV lists in SM6).

Main models.

Modulation effects over time were tested as Condition × Time and Stim × Time interactions in separate generalized linear mixed models (GLMMs; glmmTMB; Brooks et al., 2017) for each DV (meditation or physio measure) and time period (all or FUS+), with by-subject random intercepts and random slopes when feasible. Post hoc trends were estimated with emtrends (Lenth, 2025) including within-model false discovery rate correction (FDR; Benjamini & Hochberg, 1995; q = 0.05). Because depth and intensity were intended to index distinct phenomena (meditation-specific depth versus the intensity of any experience), FDR correction was applied separately for each DV and within each group rather than pooled across DVs.

Extracting slopes.

In the LTM Group, by-subject fixed-effects FUS+ slopes (i.e., rates of change) for each condition and primary DV (meditation and physio measures), based on a separate GLM (Condition*Time) within each subject, were extracted for use in subsequent moderation and mediation analyses (SM6).

Group comparisons. Experimental effects on rates of change in each primary DV (meditation and physio measures) were compared between LTM and novice groups with independent-samples t-tests of the extracted slopes (with FDR correction separately across conditions and condition contrasts, with each time period for each DV) -an approach adopted because directly modeling the group interaction was unstable in initial diagnostics (SM6).

Nonparametric tests.

Effects on placebo, in-house, and sensation measures were tested within each group using Friedman repeated-measures ANOVAs (Friedman, 1937) with Conover post hoc comparisons (Conover, 1999) and Holm correction (Holm, 1979) in JASP (JASP Team, 2024).

Moderation models.

The primary analyses testing moderation by demographic, meditationhistory, trait, and physiological variables was modeled as higher-order interactions (Condition × Time × Moderator for primary analyses, with analogous terms for post-only and pre/post measures), with post hoc tests always assessed and FDR correction applied within six predefined moderator sets separately per modulation condition; FDR-significant moderators from the LTM Group were then tested in novices (SM6). Additional analyses, within only the LTM Group, tested if the experimental changes in meditation or physiological measures moderated various outcome measures (post only, and pre/post), using the previously extracted slopes (see Extracting Slopes). See SM6 for full details.

Mediation models.

In the LTM Group only, bootstrapped within-participant mediation models (Judd et al., 2001; Montoya & Hayes, 2017) tested whether physiological and meditative changes mediated one another within each condition, or whether between-condition modulation effects on other outcomes were mediated by placebo or pre-modulation state measures or by physiological and meditative measures using the same extracted slopes, with FDR correction across DVs for each combination of predictor and mediator variables (SM6).

Model diagnostics.

All models were checked for convergence and distributional violations (DHARMa; Hartig et al., 2024); non-normal DVs were normalized (bestNormalize; Peterson, 2021) or modeled with an appropriate distribution, and continuous predictors were mean-centered (full procedure in SM6).

Discussion

Across two independent practitioner groups, an inhibitory FUS protocol produced regionally and experience-specific modulation of meditative experience. The clearest signal was an experienceby-target crossover along the depth dimension (participants' repeated online ratings of how deep their meditation was): bilateral caudate stimulation produced a steeper rise in depth in LTMs (significantly exceeding both sham and insula) but not in novices, whereas PCC stimulation showed the converse profile, producing a steeper rise in depth in novices (significantly exceeding sham, caudate, and insula) but not in LTMs, such that the caudate-versus-PCC difference in depth slopes differed significantly between groups, reversing in sign. In LTMs, the increase in subjective intensity under PCC stimulation did not differ from sham. Insular stimulation (our intended active control) produced no sham-relative change in depth or intensity in either group. Mood and trait measures were uniformly unaffected, indicating that these acute experiential effects did not propagate to post-session dispositional change.

Several second-order patterns sharpen the interpretation. First, modulation effects were moderated by baseline traits: in LTMs, higher interoceptive awareness (emotional awareness, trusting) and bliss were associated with greater depth increases under insula stimulation, and higher openness to feelings with greater PCC-related depth increases, suggesting that receptivity to modulation is trait-gated. Second, physiological change did not account for the meditative effects: in LTMs, heart rate acted, at trend level, as a suppressor of insula-related changes in depth and intensity, and lower phasic skin conductance accompanied caudate-related depth increases (a moderation effect that differed from sham), consistent with the experiential shift proceeding largely independently of, rather than downstream from, autonomic change. Third, the imperfect blinding is itself informative. LTMs, trained to attend closely to bodily sensation and doing so throughout, reported more stimulation-related sensation under every active condition than under sham, and identified sham sessions more readily than PCC sessions, despite a sham that reproduced the transducer's audible buzz and contact pressure; FUS therefore produced a perceptible change in experience. As in psychedelic trials, where salient subjective effects render full blinding unattainable (Muthukumaraswamy et al., 2021), such detection is a signature of an active manipulation, and the relevant question becomes whether expectancy or nonspecific sensation (e.g., bone-conducted auditory effects of sonication; Braun et al., 2020) can account for the outcome. Neither readily does: expectation of benefit did not differ across conditions, and the depth effect was confined to a single target (caudate) despite above-sham sensation ratings for all three. Finally, the uncorrected (pre-FDR) finding that caudate stimulation increased novices' sense of being "more present than during normal meditation" is consistent with a caudate-specific phenomenological shift rather than a generic arousal effect, though it did not survive correction.

We advance, as labelled speculation, that caudate inhibition deepens meditation by a mechanism distinct from -and potentially more phenomenologically radical than -PCC inhibition. The dorsal striatum contains head-direction (HD) cells (direct evidence : Mizumori et al., 2000), and HD coding across the broader system maintains a stable directional signal only while its anchoring computations remain intact; when allothetic and idiothetic cues are removed or corrupted, the attractor persists but its preferred-direction representation degrades and drifts (direct evidence: Asumbisa et al., 2022). A reasonable inference is that suppressing a node participating in this egocentric, spatially-anchored coding loosens the body-centered directional scaffold of the self-model; we speculate that the resulting phenomenology -a panoramic, non-located, open-monitoring or non-dual quality -is experienced as a deeper or more culminative state precisely because it departs further from ordinary egocentric perception than the quieted self-referential processing afforded by PCC down-regulation. This complements the athymhormia/auto-activation account already motivating our hypothesis (bilateral caudate compromise reducing self-generated volitional mentation): both are inhibitory routes toward experiential quieting, one motivational, one spatialrepresentational. We note honestly that this remains mechanistically open: cross-sectional imaging more often reports increased caudate engagement during meditation, so why an inhibitory protocol deepens rather than disrupts is a question we frame as a testable hypothesis (HD-degradation and auto-activation accounts) rather than a settled claim.

The PCC pattern may reflect a ceiling in LTMs and headroom in novices. Small neuroimaging studies suggest that experienced meditators show reduced PCC activity during meditation (Brewer et al., 2011; Garrison et al., 2013 Garrison et al., , 2015)), and meta-analytic evidence links focused-attention practice to PCC deactivation (Fox et al., 2016) and indicates that meditation expertise modulates the magnitude of meditation-related network activity (Ganesan et al., 2022). If LTMs already down-regulate the PCC endogenously, further exogenous inhibition may add little additional depth, consistent with the absence of a sham-relative PCC effect in this group. Novices, who have presumably not yet acquired this endogenous suppression, may retain the headroom for exogenous PCC inhibition to deepen the experience, in line with a pilot study in which PCC-targeted FUS in meditation-naïve participants was followed by reduced DMN connectivity and preliminary changes in self-related experience (Lord et al., 2024). This acute, within-session effect complements a recent randomized trial (n = 24) in which PCC-targeted FUS delivered across four sessions of a two-week mindfulness program in meditation-naïve adults decoupled the DMN, including the PCC, from the central executive network relative to sham, but did not alter the preregistered primary outcome of trait equanimity (Lord et al., 2026). Together, these findings suggest that PCC inhibition in novices perturbs network organization and in-session experience before (or without) producing measurable dispositional change, mirroring the absence of post-session trait effects here. Under this interpretation, which remains to be tested directly with concurrent neuroimaging, FUS may substitute acutely for a regulatory capacity that sustained practice renders self-sustaining.

Given the insula's centrality to interoception, its consistent activation across meditation practices (Baten et al., 2026; Fox et al., 2016), and its heavy recruitment during the body-scanning core of Vipassana, we predicted insular inhibition would disrupt depth. It did not deepen meditation (consistent with its role as an active control) but, contrary to expectation, it also failed to robustly disrupt it, producing no sham-relative change in depth in either group and only a trend-level decrease relative to its own target-matched sham in LTMs. We interpret this resilience cautiously as evidence that meditative depth is less contingent on moment-to-moment insular interoceptive signaling than predicted, or recruits redundant pathways.

The recurring experience-by-region interactions, and especially the caudate-PCC crossover, are difficult to reconcile with fixed regional effects but follow naturally from the well-established state-dependence of non-invasive neuromodulation, whereby an identical perturbation produces divergent (even sign-reversed) outcomes depending on the ongoing neural state at the moment of stimulation (Bergmann, 2018; Hanslmayr & Roux, 2017). Ultrasound here is a comparatively light perturbation; an apt analogy is that a small nudge meaningfully displaces only a system already poised at the edge of a transition. LTMs may cultivate precisely such a near-threshold state, so that caudate FUS tips them across into a deeper attractor, whereas the same dose applied far from that threshold, as in novices, has little purchase; the converse PCC pattern suggests that which transition lies within reach depends on the practitioner's stage of meditative development. Traitlevel properties may further set this susceptibility, consistent with the trait moderation observed here and with recent proposals that trait equanimity is both a mechanism of mindfulness and a tractable target for brain stimulation (Lord et al., 2025).

Viewed within the emerging "third wave" of meditation research, which focuses on advanced meditation and related phenomena (Sacchet & Lieberman, 2026), these findings offer a preliminary perturbational account of how meditative experience may scaffold access to advanced meditative states. Advanced meditation is defined not simply by accumulated practice time, but by repro-trajectory of meditative depth. It is also practically informative, identifying the post-sonication period as a window of heightened susceptibility in which practice could be scheduled, ideally after the transducer apparatus is removed so that participants can meditate unencumbered.

Future directions

The state-dependence framework motivates a closed-loop architecture. Our group's previously validated EEG decoder of meditative depth (Reggente et al., 2025) could identify transient excursions into deeper states in real time and trigger FUS at exactly those moments -delivering the perturbation when the practitioner is already at the cliff-edge, thereby exploiting rather than fighting the ongoing state. The time-delayed effects observed here are the principal obstacle to such precise temporal targeting and must be mapped before closed-loop deployment, alongside adequately powered novice cohorts. Methodologically, future replications should adopt contemporary pseudo-CT sonication-planning pipelines (e.g., BabelBrain; Pichardo, 2023; Yaakub et al., 2023a) to further refine targeting. Future work should also look to account for individual differences in response to site-specific modulation by 1) examining pre-modulation resting and structural connectomes that include the to-be-modulated region(s) and 2) leveraging voxelwise energy deposition estimates during the modulation to grade modulation-trajectory accuracy.

A second implication is that neuromodulatory facilitation is unlikely to be one-size-fits-all (Brandmeyer et al., 2023). If the target that deepens practice depends on where a practitioner sits in meditative development (here, PCC in novices and caudate in LTMs), target selection should be guided by an assessment of each practitioner's development and accessible states, ideally using phenomenologically grounded markers of meditative development (Galante et al., 2023; Sparby et al., 2026) rather than practice duration alone. Individualized functional localization offers a complementary refinement, as in a recent trial that placed each participant's PCC target at their own peak default-mode voxel (Lord et al., 2026).

Limitations

While the LTM group was sufficiently powered, the novice findings warrant restraint. Although the novice PCC effect on depth survived FDR correction, it rests on 12 participants and its magnitude may therefore be overestimated; the absence of caudate and insula effects in novices may likewise reflect limited power. Beyond sample size, novices plausibly possess coarser introspective resolution, lacking the metacognitive vocabulary to detect and report subtle gradations of depth. More broadly, although all participants received written anchors (from "extremely shallow" to "extremely deep"), depth was otherwise self-defined, and its meaning may differ across individuals, levels of experience, and contemplative lineages, a concern that our within-participant comparisons mitigate but do not eliminate. Novices were also screened for engagement during training, which may limit generalization to less motivated beginners. Finally, meditation experience was not randomized, so group differences may reflect correlates of long-term practice (e.g., age, self-selection, trait interoception) rather than practice itself; the FUS effects within each group are causal, but their dependence on experience is not established.

Most of the FUS+ window followed sonication (nine of twelve bins), so the effects reported here cannot be attributed cleanly to acute, online modulation. Figure 2.

Figure 2. Automated pipeline for marking brain targets on subject MRI for ultrasound neuronavigation. (1) Each subject's brain was extracted (OptiBET; Lutkenhoff et al., 2014) and registered to MNI space.( 2) The resulting subject-to-MNI registration was inverted to yield an MNI-to-subject inverse transform. (3) Custom MNI-space regions of interest (red spheres) were propagated into subject space via this inverse transform and added to the native T1-weighted image as high-intensity points (PCC shown here as a bright sphere). Targets were then defined in Brainsight by placing a marker at the centroid of each spherical hyperintensity.

Figure3. The ultrasound parameters used here, including pulse schedule, as well as intensity, are shown. For simplicity, the white and black blocks shown are fewer than the real number of 10-second blocks during a 12-minute period. Note that 12 minutes of ultrasound were applied to the PCC. However, for both the insula and caudate, the modulation was 6 minutes to one side (e.g., Left Insula) before switching to a transducer placed on the opposite side (e.g., Right Insula) for another 6 minutes, totaling 12 minutes of ultrasound.
