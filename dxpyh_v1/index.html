Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

What we found

We found 20 non-significant p values that should be checked for appropriate interpretation.

Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard), also Internal Review Board - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term); not IEC followed by a number, which is a technical standard - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee/Commission, in the singular or plural - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee, Bioethics Committee/Commission, Ethikkommission - Ethical Review Authority/Unit/Office/Agency - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Human Research Protection Office/Program, Office for Human Subjects Protection - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee(s) on/for (Medical and Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt - Ethics review that was not needed: Ethics review was not required, did not require further ethical review, exempt from formal human research ethics review, no new institutional ethics review

A template line the authors have not filled in (“[Add ethics approval … here.]”) is not a statement.

When a statement is expected

The module sorts each paper by where its data came from (the internal .data_source(); the signs and how the rule was tested are described in R/data-source.R): - own: the authors collected data from people or animals, for this paper, in an earlier study of their own, or (a protocol) will collect them; - existing_records: records about individual people that the authors obtained for this study, such as hospital records or a cohort; - existing_unclear: existing data about people from a source the module could not identify; - open_resource: an open resource any researcher can use, such as UK Biobank or NHANES; - none: no data about people or animals (a review, simulation, commentary, theory, tutorial, coding of published papers, or figures about groups); - uncertain: the signs were missing or pointed both ways; - no_text: the paper has no text.

A statement is expected for own, existing_records and existing_unclear. A paper without a statement in these classes, or in uncertain, is flagged. The module reads the text, and the results of power, stat_p_exact, stat_check, repo_check, data_check and prereg_check when they ran before it (run it after them); without stat_p_exact, it finds the p-values in the text.

On 199 PsyArXiv and medRxiv preprints labelled after the rule was fixed, the module decided right whether a statement is needed for 86.9% (the earlier live-data search: 83.4%). Its main errors: other people’s studies described in the words of a new study, records and specimens described only as counts, and open resources the paper does not call open.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine, Daniel Lakens and Cristian Mesquida

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download information from other preregistration sites, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Therefore 0% of true instances were missed, and 43% of detections were false positives. See https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/ for more details of the validation.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. A TRO uses them for each trov:TransparentResearchPerformance. * run_results$exit_status: the exit status of the process that ran the script (0 = ended normally); NA when the process did not report one (a time limit, or Docker failing to start the container). * artifacts: one row per file, with stage (deposit: as downloaded from the repository, hashed from the downloaded file; before_run: the run folder just before the first script ran, after metacheck’s own changes, such as rewritten paths and unpacked archives; after_run: only the files the scripts made (status new), changed (changed) or removed (removed), as an unchanged file is the one listed before the run), path (relative to the run folder, as valid UTF-8, a byte of another encoding written as <xx>), size (bytes), sha256, mime_type (by extension, NA when unknown), source (the file’s address in the repository, for deposit). The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). Files metacheck itself copied or made between passes (see modifications, output_copied, file_placed) show in after_run too. A TRO uses the rows as trov:ResearchArtifacts and each stage as a trov:ArtifactArrangement. * modifications$sha256_before, modifications$sha256_after: the hash of the changed script as downloaded and as it ran; NA when the script is not at the same path in both (a script made from an R Markdown file). * environment: the system that ran the code (trov:TransparentResearchSystem in a TRO): metacheck_version, started_at, ended_at, host (R version, platform, operating system), sandbox, network_isolated (TRUE only when every Docker run had --network none; the process sandbox does not stop the network), internet, timeout_per_script, total_timeout, python_local (the Python run outside Docker and its version), and docker: runs (for each kind of run, r_run, r_install, python_run, python_install, the image and the docker run arguments, such as --network none, --read-only, --cap-drop ALL, no-new-privileges, the process limit and the memory and processor limits) and images (for each image, its ID and digests from docker image inspect, its creation time and the R and Python versions its environment declares).

run_results$checked says what the run of each script shows about the code, in five groups, so that a script that does not reproduce is kept apart from one the check could not test: ran (it ran to the end); code_error (it did not reproduce: it stopped with an error of the code or the deposit, could not be read, or needs a file the deposit does not hold); dependency (a package or program was not available: a package that could not be installed or was not loaded, a program outside R and Python, a system library, or R crashing in a package’s compiled code); time (it did not finish within the time limit for one script, or was not started because the time for all scripts had run out); and not_checkable (it could not be checked automatically: it needs a person, the internet, a key or another setting of the authors’ computer, one platform such as Google Colab, Windows or OpenSAFELY, more memory than the run allows, a file that was not downloaded, a screen, or a program that runs it, or it is a file of a package of the code that Python runs only as a module of that package). It is NA for files run inside another script, files that are not an analysis, files not read, and a Docker failure. summary_table counts each group (repro_ran_n, repro_code_error_n, repro_dependency_n, repro_time_n, repro_not_checkable_n), and the report’s first sentence gives them. The traffic light reflects the static readiness signals by default; a script in code_error that stopped with an error makes it red, as a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure, and a script in one of the other three groups keeps it from being green. Calls that need a screen, which the run does not have (dev.print(), dev.copy2pdf(), dev.copy2eps(), savePlot(), windows(), win.graph(), x11(), quartz(), View()), are replaced before the run by calls that need none, which modifications records as screen_call_replaced. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Full text

Abstract

Exposure to natural environments has been associated with the restoration of attentional resources and improvements in affective functioning. Although these psychological benefits are well documented, less is known about whether and how environmental degradation, in the form of litter, attenuates them. In this preregistered study, we investigated the psychological effects of real-world nature exposure and whether these effects were reduced when natural environments were degraded by litter. Participants were exposed, in counterbalanced order, to a preserved forest, a littered forest, and an urban setting. Eye movements, affective state, perceived restorativeness, and mental bandwidth were assessed for each exposure. Compared with the urban setting, the forest settings were associated with lower eye-movement activity (i.e., lower fixation rates and longer fixation durations), more favorable post-exposure affect (i.e., higher positive and lower negative affect, adjusting for pre-exposure levels), and higher perceived restorativeness. The littered forest was associated with higher post-exposure negative affect compared to the preserved forest, whereas no reliable differences emerged for gaze indices, positive affect, or perceived restorativeness. Most mediation analyses were inconclusive regarding the indirect association between environment and affect or perceived restorativeness via eye-movement activity, and attention allocated to litter was neither associated with psychological outcomes nor with nature connectedness or eco-anxiety. Finally, the restorative advantage of natural over urban settings was larger among participants with higher nature connectedness. In the context of increasing urbanization and environmental degradation, these findings highlight the importance of nature preservation for psychological well-being.

1. Introduction

Contact with nature has been consistently associated with benefits for affective and cognitive functioning (Berman et al., 2008; Bratman & Gross, 2026; Rim et al., 2025). Yet, paradoxically, everyday opportunities to experience nature are steadily diminishing due to accelerating urbanization, and the remaining natural environment is increasingly exposed to littering, deforestation, and climate change. Despite this degradation, nature's benefits have largely been studied as a function of whether nature is present, with less attention to the ecological quality of the environments people encounter. The present study addresses this gap by exposing participants to real-world natural and urban environments, in which ecological degradation was experimentally manipulated through the deposition of litter. We compared the psychological benefits of these environments on affective and attentional processes using self-reported and mobile eye-tracking measures.

1.1 Proposed mechanisms underlying nature's psychological benefits

Two complementary theoretical frameworks generate predictions about the psychological mechanisms underlying nature's beneficial effects. The Attention Restoration Theory (ART; Kaplan & Kaplan, 1989) proposes that exposure to natural environments replenishes attentional resources depleted by demands on directed attention in stimulating urban settings, by engaging involuntary attention through inherently interesting stimuli, a quality Kaplan (1995) termed soft fascination. Building on ART, Basu et al. (2018) further proposed that soft fascination preserves mental bandwidth, the mental space available for processing, allowing recovery from directed attentional fatigue while leaving room for unrelated thoughts. The Stress Reduction Theory (SRT; Ulrich et al., 1991) offers a complementary account, positing that exposure to non-threatening natural environments elicits affective and physiological stress-recovery responses. Although ART and SRT emphasize different restorative pathways, attentional restoration and stress recovery remain closely interconnected, making the two theories complementary explanations of nature's restorative effects (Kaplan, 1995).

Consistent with these frameworks, nature exposure has been extensively associated with lower stress and negative affect, greater well-being and positive affect (Barnes et al., 2019; Yao et al., 2021), and improved attention and cognitive performance (Ohly et al., 2016; Stevenson et al., 2018), relative to urban environments. Yet, the cognitive processes underlying these benefits remain poorly understood (Joye & Dewitte, 2018; Joye & De Block, 2026), notably motivating the use of eye-tracking measures to capture attentional dynamics.

Eye-tracking offers a promising approach for investigating cognitive mechanisms because eye movements provide an objective, real-time index of overt visual attention (Holmqvist et al., 2011). Consistent with ART, eye-tracking studies have generally shown that viewing natural scenes is associated with lower eye-movement activity, reflected by fewer fixations and longer fixation durations, than viewing urban scenes (Berto et al., 2008; Franěk et al., 2018 Franěk et al., , 2019;; Franěk & Petružálek, 2025; Fu & Xue, 2023; Martínez-Solo et al., 2019; Valtchanov & Ellard, 2015). Previous eye-tracking studies have mostly relied on photographs presented in laboratory settings. Although such designs provide strong experimental control, they do not capture the multisensory and immersive nature of real-world experiences. Furthermore, meta-analytic evidence has revealed that exposure to actual natural environments enhances cognitive restoration relative to virtual exposures (Stevenson et al., 2018). To date, only one study, by Stevenson et al. (2019), has examined eye-movement activity during real-world exposure. They found a higher fixation rate but no difference in fixation duration in children walking in natural versus urban environments. However, the study's small eye-tracking sample, fixed exposure order, and walking procedure limit the interpretability of these findings, underscoring the need for well-controlled in situ eye-tracking studies examining attentional dynamics during nature exposure.

Another important gap in the literature, recently highlighted by Prugger & Kühn (2026), is the predominant focus of theoretical frameworks and empirical research on the restorative effects of nature, namely the recovery of depleted resources following stress or fatigue. Many restorative paradigms explicitly induce cognitive depletion or stress prior to nature exposure, reflecting an implicit assumption that nature's benefits are most detectable, or perhaps only observable, following such depletion. This emphasis has largely overlooked the instorative potential of nature, namely its capacity to enhance cognitive and emotional functioning beyond typical levels, independently of prior depletion. As a result, instoration has received little theoretical and empirical attention compared with restoration (Joye & Dewitte, 2018).

1.2 Moderators of nature's psychological benefits

Evidence supporting nature's benefits has been observed across a large range of natural settings. However, natural environments vary substantially in their ecological quality and physical characteristics, dimensions that may shape their psychological benefits (Marselle et al., 2021). A small but growing body of research suggests that the quality of our natural surroundings, mostly operationalized in terms of biodiversity, is an important predictor of mental health and well-being (Knight et al., 2022; Wyles et al., 2019). Yet, another key dimension of ecological quality is the degree to which natural environments have been degraded by human activities (e.g., littering). This distinction raises an important yet largely unexplored question: does environmental degradation attenuate the psychological benefits typically associated with exposure to nature? The ART (Kaplan & Kaplan, 1989) proposes that restorative environments should capture attention effortlessly through soft fascination, possess sufficient extent and coherence, create a sense of being away from daily concerns, and be compatible with an individual's purposes. However, environmental degradation may compromise these restorative properties by introducing visually unpleasant stimuli, disrupting the coherence of the environment, evoking environmental concerns caused by human activities, and thereby diminishing the motivation to engage with the environment. This prediction is supported by a small but growing body of evidence examining whether litter attenuates the affective benefits and perceived restorativeness of nature exposure. Wyles et al. (2016) found that littered beach scenes were rated as less restorative and elicited more negative emotions than clean scenes, particularly for public litter (e.g., cans, bottles). Converging evidence links exposure to littered environments with lower positive affect, higher negative affect, and reduced perceived restorativeness (Bielinis et al., 2022 (Bielinis et al., , 2024;; Clayton, 2021; Menzel et al., 2024). However, these studies differ in how environmental degradation has been manipulated and experienced. Most relied on photographs, videos, or imagined environments (Clayton, 2021; Menzel et al., 2024; Wyles et al., 2016), offering experimental control but limited ecological validity. Some also used extreme forms of degradation such as burnt-out vehicles (Pretty et al., 2005), leaving unclear whether subtler, more ecologically realistic signs of degradation are sufficient to alter the benefits of nature exposure. Conversely, studies conducted in real environments offered greater ecological validity but less experimental control. For example, Bielinis et al. (2024) compared a preserved forest with one containing an open waste dump, but environments differed in other characteristics and participants were explicitly instructed to pay attention to the litter. Menzel et al. (2024) included a real-world walking condition, but participants selected their own route, leaving litter and other environmental features uncontrolled.

The psychological benefits of nature may also vary with individuals' sense of nature connectedness. Indeed, higher nature connectedness has been associated with perceiving natural environments as more restorative (Wilkie & Stavridou, 2013) and with greater affective benefits from nature exposure, including increased positive affect and capacity for reflection (McMahan et al., 2018). Eye-tracking evidence, though limited, suggests that nature-relatedness also shapes visual engagement with natural versus urban environments, including greater visual exploration and attention to natural elements (Batool et al., 2022; Chen et al., 2022). Clayton (2021) further suggested that affective responses to environmental degradation may depend on environmental identity, with individuals strongly connected to nature being more affected by littered natural scenes. Other individual differences may also modulate responses to environmental degradation. Eco-anxiety, defined as anxiety related to the global environmental crisis including climate change, biodiversity loss, pollution, and deforestation (Hogg et al., 2021), may shape sensitivity to visible signs of environmental degradation. Furthermore, an increased attentional focus on these visible signs may amplify their detrimental impact on nature's benefits. However, whether ecoanxiety and attention allocation respectively moderate the psychological impact of exposure to degraded natural environments has not yet been examined.

1.3 Aims and Hypotheses

The present study aimed to advance our understanding of the psychological benefits of nature exposure in four complementary ways. First, we examined these benefits in an ecologically valid, in situ setting, while maintaining experimental control through a withinsubjects design. Second, we used mobile eye-tracking to capture attentional dynamics during exposure, providing continuous indices of overt visual attention. Third, we examined psychological responses to nature exposure in the absence of an explicit pre-exposure depletion induction to consider whether benefits may extend beyond conventional restoration paradigms. Finally, we examined the role of environmental quality by comparing a preserved forest, a littered forest, and an urban environment, to investigate whether environmental degradation attenuates the psychological benefits of nature exposure.

Our first research question examined whether environmental quality influences attentional, affective and perceived restorative outcomes. We hypothesized that natural environments would be associated with lower eye-movement activity (indexed by lower fixation rates and longer fixation durations; H1a), more favorable post-exposure affective states, controlling for pre-exposure positive and negative affect (H1b), and higher perceived restorativeness and mental bandwidth (H1c) compared with urban environments. We further expected these benefits to be attenuated in degraded relative to preserved natural environments. Our second question examined whether attentional processes mediate the effects of environmental conditions on affective and perceived restorative outcomes. Given ART and SRT's complementary predictions, natural environments should engage less effortful involuntary attention, which may contribute to the affective and restorative benefits of nature exposure. We hypothesized that reduced eye-movement activity in natural (versus urban) environments would be associated with greater affective improvements (H2a) and higher perceived restorativeness and mental bandwidth (H2b), with these indirect effects attenuated in degraded (versus preserved) natural environments. Finally, we investigated whether the effects of environmental conditions on psychological outcomes varied as a function of individuals' nature connectedness (H3), eco-anxiety (H4) and attention allocation on litter (H5). We further hypothesized that higher eco-anxiety and nature connectedness would be associated with greater attention allocation to litter (H6). The procedure, hypotheses and analyses were preregistered (https://osf.io/6pt8q).

2. Methods

>Methods

2.1 Ethics

The study protocol followed the ethical standards established by the Declaration of Helsinki for experiments involving humans and was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Psychological Sciences Research Institute (UCLouvain; Projet2025-114).

2.2 Design

We used a within-subjects design comprising three conditions administered in a single session: a preserved natural environment (forest without litter), a degraded natural environment (forest with litter), and an urban environment (the front of a university sports complex). Participants were exposed to each condition in situ, in counterbalanced order (Figure 1).

2.3 Participants

Sample size was determined via simulation-based power analysis (1000 simulations, SIMR package; Green & MacLeod, 2016) using parameters from a pilot study (N = 8) with the same design, for mixed-effects models testing the effect of the environment-related conditions (natural-versus-urban and preserved-versus-degraded contrasts) on fixation rate, positive affect and perceived restorativeness. Results indicated that N = 40 provided at least 90% power for all natural-versus-urban contrasts, detecting effects equal to or up to 20% smaller than the pilot's effects. For the preserved-versus-degraded contrast, power was insufficient for fixation rate and positive affect but reached 100% for perceived restorativeness (Figures S1-S3 in the supplementary materials section). As preregistered, we aimed for N = 40 and collected data from 45 participants to account for potential exclusions.

We recruited 45 participants (aged 18 to 45 years) via social media and flyers.

Exclusion criteria were severe myopia (≤ -5 diopters) or hypermetropia (≥ +3 diopters) without the possibility of wearing contact lenses, astigmatism greater than +1 diopter, cataract, use of bifocal or progressive lenses, history of eye surgery, strabismus or other oculomotor disorders. We also excluded participants reporting allergies to tree pollen or grasses, attention deficit/hyperactivity disorder, and heavy smoking or vaping to minimize potential order-related confounding related to the prolonged passive testing, discomfort or nicotine abstinence. Participants were asked to refrain from wearing eye make-up for the experiment session and those requiring vision correction were asked to wear contact lenses.

All participants provided informed written consent before taking part in the study and were unaware of the hypotheses tested. As compensation, they received 20 euros for participating in the study.

2.4 Procedure

The two natural environments were located at the entrance of a wooded area, 20 m apart, while the urban environment was 300 meters away, in front of an athletic indoor center facilities (Figure 2). The natural sites were physically highly similar, and the assignment of the degraded condition to one of them was counterbalanced across participants to control for location-specific effects. In the degraded condition, two pieces of litter (a transparent plastic bag and a white plastic cup) were placed at a fixed position, three meters apart near the center of the visual field. All sessions took place in the afternoon to standardize time of day and limit diurnal variability and were postponed in case of rain. Participants were visually shielded by a screen or wall, so that their field of view was directed exclusively toward the target environment. The observed area was free of human activity, and experimenters remained out of sight during exposure.

Each condition followed four steps. First, participants sat on a chair, put on the mobile eye-tracker and completed a single-point calibration, holding a white card with a circular black target at arm's length and eye level while fixating on its center. Second, affective state was assessed via a smartphone survey. Third, participants freely observed their surroundings for 8 minutes (duration preregistered based on pilot testing) while minimizing head or body movements. Fourth, affective state was reassessed via smartphone, together with perceived restorativeness and mental bandwidth. Participants then walked, accompanied by an experimenter, to the next condition and repeated these steps. At the end of the session, they completed questionnaires assessing eco-anxiety, nature connectedness, and demographics. As a manipulation check, they indicated whether they had noticed the litter in the forest (40 out of 45 participants, 89%) and, if so, to what extent it had bothered them (M = 2.17, SD = 0.64, on a 0-3 Likert scale).

2.5 Apparatus

Eye movements were recorded using Tobii Pro Glasses 3 (video-based pupil-center corneal reflection, 100 Hz, average accuracy of 0.6°, binocular). Fixations were identified with Tobii I-VT (Velocity-Threshold) Fixation filter. Raw gaze samples were smoothed with a moving median noise reduction (window of 3 samples), and velocity was computed using a 20ms-window. Samples with angular velocity below 30°/s were classified as fixations.

Adjacent fixations separated by less than 75ms and 0.5° were merged, and fixations shorter than 60ms were discarded. Only the 8-minute exposure period for each condition was analyzed. High-resolution reference snapshots were taken with an external device positioned to match the participant's viewpoint. Gaze data were mapped onto these snapshots using the Assisted Mapping function in Tobii Pro Lab, which matches scene-camera frames to the snapshot via feature-matching algorithms. Mapped points with low similarity scores were manually verified and corrected where necessary. Rectangular areas of interest (AOIs) were then manually drawn around each litter item, centered on the object and extending beyond it by the object's own width and height, to capture fixations on litter while accounting for spatial uncertainty in gaze estimation.

2.6 Measures

2.6.1 Eye movements

Four eye-movement variables were computed from fixation data recorded during the 8-minute exposure period. Fixation rate was the total number of fixations divided by the exposure duration (480 s). Mean fixation duration was the total fixation duration (ms) divided by the total number of fixations. Relative dwell time on litter, computed only for the degraded environment condition, indexed the proportion of visual attention allocated to the litter and was the summed duration of fixations within the two litter AOIs divided by the total fixation duration. Number of visits on litter indexed how often participants oriented their gaze toward the litter and was the number of distinct AOI visits, a visit being a sequence of one or more consecutive fixations within an AOI, bounded by fixations outside it.

2.6.2 Questionnaires

The Positive and Negative Affect Schedule (PANAS; Watson et al., 1988) assessed positive and negative affect, each scored by summing 10 items rated on a 1-5 scale (range 10-50; ω = .84-.91 for positive and .77-.87 for negative affect across conditions and time points). The Perceived Restorativeness Scale (Pasini et al., 2014) assessed the perceived restorative quality of an environment along four dimensions drawn from ART (Kaplan, 1995): fascination, being away, coherence and scope. The score was the sum of 11 items rated on a 0-10 scale (ω = .79-.85). The Mental Bandwidth Scale (Basu et al., 2018) assessed the mental processing capacity available to participants during exposure. The score was the sum of 7 items rated on a 0-6 scale (ω = .51-.65). The Hogg Eco-Anxiety Scale (Hogg et al., 2021; French validation by Mathé et al., 2023) assessed psychological responses to climate change and other ecological issues (e.g., environmental degradation, species extinction) along four dimensions: affective symptoms, rumination, behavioral symptoms, and anxiety about personal impact. The score was the sum of 13 items rated on a 1-4 scale (ω = .87).

The Connectedness to Nature Scale (Mayer & Frantz, 2004; French validation by Navarro et al., 2017) assessed the emotional and experiential sense of connection with, and belonging to, the natural world. The score was the sum of 14 items rated on a 0-5 scale (ω = .81).

2.7 Data Analysis

2.7.1 Data Pre-Processing

A preregistered data reduction procedure was performed to ensure good data quality.

We excluded eye-tracking data for each condition when more than 50% of the eyemovement recording was invalid (due to calibration failure or signal loss), which resulted in the exclusion of seven recording sessions. All participants provided valid eye-movement data for at least two conditions. However, we deviated from preregistration by removing five participants who declared not having noticed the litter in the degraded natural condition. This resulted in a final sample of 40 participants. For transparency, all analyses were repeated using the full sample and are fully reported in Supplementary Materials. Continuous variables were inspected for skewness and outliers prior to analysis. Variables showing high skewness (i.e., mean fixation duration, pre-and post-negative affect, relative dwell time on litter) were log-transformed to improve normality. Results proved robust to winsorization and are therefore reported on non-winsorized data. Model residuals were inspected and met assumptions of normality and homoscedasticity. All analyses were conducted using R (R Core Team, 2026). The data and analysis code are made publicly available on Dataverse (https://doi.org/10.14428/DVN/WUOLRP).

2.7.2 Main analyses

To compare the restorative effects of the environmental conditions (H1), we ran linear mixed-effects models (using the lmer function from the lme4 package) with condition as the main independent variable and random intercepts for participants to account for the repeated-measures designfoot_0 . Following recommendations for a priori planned comparisons (Granziol et al., 2025), we tested two a priori contrasts reflecting our theoretical questions: (1) a nature-versus-urban contrast, comparing the average of preserved and degraded natural environments to the urban environment; (2) a degradation contrast, comparing preserved-versus-degraded natural environments.

The order of condition was included as a covariate. Separate models were fitted for each dependent variable. To compare the effects of environmental conditions on attentional outcomes (H1a), we fitted two separate models with fixation rate (M1a.1) and mean fixation duration (M1a.2) as the dependent variable. To compare the effects of environmental conditions on affective states (H1b), we fitted two separate models with post-positive affect (M1b.1) and post-negative affect (M1b.2) as the dependent variable. We included in both models the corresponding pre-exposure affect as a covariate. To compare the effects of environmental conditions on perceived restorative outcomes (H1c), we fitted two separate models with perceived restorativeness (M1c.1) and mental bandwidth (M1c.2) as the dependent variable. For all analyses testing H1, the Holm-Bonferroni correction was used to adjust the p-values for four comparisons within each family of dependent variables (i.e. attentional, affective and perceived restorative measures), whereas the confidence intervals were not adjusted.

To examine whether attentional activity mediates the effects of environmental conditions on outcome variables (H2), we conducted multilevel mediation analyses with random intercepts for participants. Condition effects were tested with the two planned contrasts described above, and condition order was included as a covariate. Each attentional metric (fixation rate, mean fixation duration) was tested separately as a mediator of each outcome (post-positive affect, post-negative affect, perceived restorativeness, mental bandwidth). For affective outcomes, pre-exposure affect was also included as a covariate. To improve model convergence, mediators and outcomes were z-standardized across all observations. Indirect effects were computed as the product of the path a (condition → mediator) and path b (mediator → outcome) coefficients, and their 95% confidence intervals were estimated with 5000 participant-level bootstrapped replicates. Indirect effects were considered significant when the interval excluded zero.

2.7.3 Secondary analyses

To investigate whether the differential effects of the environmental conditions were moderated by individual factors (H3-H5), we replicated the structure of the mixed-effects models used to test H1 (M1a.1-M1c.2), adding in each model an interaction term between condition and the moderator of interest (nature connectedness, eco-anxiety, relative dwell time and number of visits on litter). Models were restricted to dependent variables yielding significant results in the main analyses. For all analyses testing H3-H5, the false discovery rate procedure (Benjamini-Hochberg) was used to adjust the p-values given the larger number of tests conducted, whereas the confidence intervals were not adjusted. All significant interactions were examined using simple slopes analyses and visualized using estimated marginal means. To examine whether higher levels of eco-anxiety and nature connectedness predict greater attention allocation toward litter (H6), we fitted two linear models, with relative dwell time on litter (M6a) and number of visits on litter (M6b) as dependent variables, and eco-anxiety and nature connectedness as simultaneous predictors.

If multicollinearity was detected between predictors, they were entered into separate models.

Finally, as a complementary analysis, we tested an additional degraded-versus-urban contrast in mixed-effects models to examine whether the degraded natural environment retained greater attentional, affective and perceived restorative benefits than the urban environment.

3. Results

>Results

3.1 Descriptive Statistics

Our final sample consisted of 24 women and 16 men, with a mean age of 23 years (SD = 3.4). Participants reported a mean nature connectedness score of 44.4 (SD = 8.96, range = 25-61) and a mean eco-anxiety score of 21.5 (SD = 5.50, range = 13-38).

3.2 Main Analyses

3.2.1 Impact of environmental conditions on restoration (H1; Figure 3)

Regarding attentional outcomes, M1a.1 revealed that participants showed a lower fixation rate when exposed to natural environments compared to the urban environment (b = -0.16, p-Holm = .018, 95% CI [-0.28, -0.05]), whereas no significant difference emerged between preserved and degraded natural environments (b = -0.00, p-Holm = .999, 95% CI [-0.09, 0.10]). Consistent with this pattern, M1a.2 showed longer fixation durations when participants were exposed to natural environments compared to the urban environment (b = 0.22, p-Holm < .001, 95% CI [0.15, 0.29]), whereas no difference was found between preserved and degraded natural environments (b = -0.01, p-Holm = .999, 95% CI [-0.07, 0.04]).

Regarding affective outcomes, M1b.1 revealed that participants reported higher positive affect after being exposed to natural environments compared to the urban environment, after adjusting for pre-exposure positive affect (b = 2.16, p-Holm < .001, 95% CI [1.29, 3.04]), whereas no significant difference emerged between preserved and degraded natural environments (b = 0.39, p-Holm = .305, 95% CI [-0.36, 1.15]). M1b.2 revealed lower negative affect after exposure to natural environments compared to the urban environment, after adjusting for pre-exposure negative affect (b = -0.07, p-Holm = .007, 95% CI [-0.12, -0.03]), and after exposure to the preserved natural environment compared to the degraded one (b = -0.05, p-Holm = .025, 95% CI [-0.09, -0.01]).

Regarding perceived restorative outcomes, M1c.1 revealed that participants perceived the natural environments as much more restorative than the urban environment (b = 25.53, p-Holm < .001, 95% CI [22.73, 28.32]), whereas no significant difference in perceived restorativeness emerged between preserved and degraded natural environments (b = 1.25, p-Holm = .615, 95% CI [-1.16, 3.65]). M1c.2 showed no significant difference in mental bandwidth between natural environments and the urban environment (b = 1.15, p-Holm = .069, 95% CI [0.17, 2.13]), nor between preserved and degraded natural environments (b = 0.37, p-Holm = .615, 95% CI [-0.48, 1.22]).

3.2.2 Mediation by attentional activity (H2)

Of the 16 multilevel mediation analyses, one indirect effect was significant: the indirect effect of nature-versus-urban contrast on mental bandwidth via fixation rate (indirect effect = 0.08, 95% CI [0.02, 0.16]). Natural environments were associated with lower fixation rates (a = -0.29, 95% CI [-0.48, -0.12]), which in turn were associated with greater mental bandwidth (b = -0.28, 95% CI [-0.45, -0.09]); the direct effect was not significant (c' = 0.07, 95% CI [-0.10, 0.26]). As m1c.2 testing the main effect of nature-versus-urban contrast on mental bandwidth did not survive Holm correction, this result should be interpreted with caution. All other indirect effects had confidence intervals including zero (see Table S1).

3.3 Secondary Analyses

3.3.1 Moderation by nature connectedness (H3)

A significant interaction was found between nature connectedness and the natureversus-urban contrast on perceived restorativeness (b = 0.36, FDR-adjusted p = .047, 95% CI [0.05, 0.66]), indicating that the restorative difference between natural and urban environments increased with participants' connectedness to nature. Simple slopes analyses indicated that natural environments were perceived as significantly more restorative than urban environments at low (-1 SD; b = 22.35, SE = 1.95, p < .001), mean (b = 25.52, SE = 1.38, p < .001), and high (+1 SD; b = 28.70, SE = 1.95, p < .001) levels of nature connectedness. No significant interaction emerged for the preserved-versus-degraded contrast on perceived restorativeness (FDR-adjusted p > .05).

No significant interaction emerged for any contrast on fixation rate, mean fixation duration, post-positive and post-negative affect (all FDR-adjusted p-values > .05), showing no evidence that nature connectedness influenced the effect of environmental conditions on attention patterns and affective outcomes.

3.3.2 Moderation by eco-anxiety (H4)

The interaction between eco-anxiety and the preserved-versus-degraded contrast on post-exposure positive affect was not significant after correction for multiple testing (b = 0.17, unadjusted p = .014, FDR-adjusted p = .062, 95% CI [0.04, 0.31]). Although the unadjusted estimate suggested that the difference in post-positive affect between preserved and degraded natural environments increased with eco-anxiety, this pattern should be regarded as exploratory. Simple slopes analyses indicated that the preserved natural environment elicited more positive affect than the degraded natural environment among participants with high eco-anxiety (+1 SD; b = 1.34, SE = 0.53, p = .01), whereas no significant difference emerged at low (-1 SD; b = -0.54, SE = 0.52, p = .31) and mean (b = 0.40, SE = 0.37, p = .29) levels of eco-anxiety. No significant interaction emerged for the nature-versus-urban contrast on post-positive affect (FDR-adjusted p > .05).

No significant interaction emerged for any contrast on fixation rate, mean fixation duration, post-negative affect and perceived restorativeness (all FDR-adjusted p-values > .05), showing no evidence that eco-anxiety influenced the effect of environmental conditions on attentional patterns, negative affective states and perceived restorativeness.

3.3.3 Moderation by attention allocation toward litter (H5)

No significant interaction emerged for any contrast on fixation rate, mean fixation duration, post-positive, post-negative affect and perceived restorativeness (all FDRadjusted p-values > .05), showing no evidence that attention allocation toward litter influenced the effect of environmental conditions on attentional patterns, affective states and perceived restorativeness.

3.3.4 Predictors of attention allocation toward litter (H6)

Neither nature connectedness nor eco-anxiety predicted the relative dwell time spent on litter (CNS: b = -0.00, p = .970, 95% CI [-0.05, 0.05]; eco-anxiety: b = -0.00, p = .936, 95% CI [-0.08, 0.07]) or the number of visits made on litter (CNS: b = 0.33, p = .607, 95% CI [-0.96, 1.62]; eco-anxiety: b = 0.13, p = .902, 95% CI [-1.97, 2.23]).

3.3.5 Exploratory comparison of the degraded natural and urban conditions

When exposed to the degraded natural environment compared to the urban environment, participants showed a lower fixation rate (b = -0.16, p < .001, 95% CI [-0.23, -0.10]), longer fixation durations (b = 0.16, p < .001, 95% CI [0.10, 0.23]), a stronger increase in positive affect (b = 1.42, p = .001, 95% CI [0.61, 2.24]) and a higher perceived restorativeness (b = 18.49, p < .001, 95% CI [14.85, 22.13]). However, no significant difference emerged for post-negative affect (b = -0.03, p = .191, 95% CI [-0.07, 0.01]) and mental bandwidth (b = 0.68, p = .125, 95% CI [-0.19, 1.54]).

4. Discussion

This study aimed to provide novel insights into the psychological benefits of real-world nature exposure and the potential detrimental impact of environmental degradation. Using a counterbalanced in situ design, we examined attentional and psychological responses to a preserved forest, a littered forest, and an urban environment. Nature exposure was associated with lower eye-movement activity, more favorable affective states and substantially greater perceived restorativeness, whereas subtle litter selectively altered negative affect in the primary analysis.

4.1 The psychological benefits of nature (versus urban) exposure

When comparing the benefits of exposure to natural versus urban environments, our findings provided partial support for our first hypothesis that real-world nature exposure had beneficial effects on attentional (H1a), affective (H1b) and perceived restorative (H1c) outcomes. First, natural environments were associated with reduced eye-movement activity, indexed by longer and less frequent fixations during exposure. This aligns with previous laboratory-based eye-tracking studies, which similarly observed fewer and longer fixations when participants viewed natural scenes compared to urban scenes (Berto et al., 2008; Franěk et al., 2018 Franěk et al., , 2019;; Franěk & Petružálek, 2025; Fu & Xue, 2023; Martínez-Solo et al., 2019; Valtchanov & Ellard, 2015). These gaze patterns are consistent with ART-derived predictions that natural environments impose lower demands on directed attention than urban environments (Kaplan, 1995), though fixation dynamics do not offer a process-pure measure of such demands.

Second, our findings showed that nature exposure also benefited affective states, reflected in higher positive affect and lower negative affect following exposure to the natural environments compared to the urban environment. These results echo a substantial body of research documenting the beneficial effects of nature exposure on mood, stress and subjective well-being (for reviews, see Barnes et al., 2019; Bratman et al., 2021; McMahan & Estes, 2015), and are consistent with SRT-derived predictions that nature can foster more favorable affective states (Ulrich et al., 1983). It should be noted, however, that unlike most previous ART-and SRT-based studies, the present study did not measure the recovery of depleted resources following stress or fatigue. Instead, we examined responses to nature in the absence of an explicit depletion induction and assessed pre-to-post changes in affective state. Our findings therefore suggest that affective benefits of nature can emerge even in the absence of an explicit depletion induction, a pattern consistent with the possibility of instorative as well as restorative effects.

Third, participants rated both forest settings as possessing substantially greater perceived restorative qualities than the urban setting. By contrast, the nature-urban difference in mental bandwidth was small and did not survive correction for multiple comparisons. This dissociation may partly reflect what each scale captures. The PRS assesses an evaluation of the environment's restorative qualities (fascination, being away, coherence, and scope; Pasini et al., 2014), whereas the MBS assesses participants' own momentary cognitive state, that is, the extent to which their mind was free to wander into self-generated thought rather than remaining tied to the immediate surroundings (Basu et al., 2018). Within ART, these are distinct: an environment may lack restorative qualities without preventing the mind from wandering. Our urban setting, a quiet building façade without traffic or crowds, was likely low in fascination and extent but also low in attentional demands, which would reduce perceived restorativeness without significantly reducing mental bandwidth.

Moreover, because no depletion was induced before exposure, participants may have had ample processing capacity in all conditions, leaving little room for differences in mental bandwidth to emerge, whereas evaluations of environmental qualities do not depend on prior depletion. Evaluative judgments may also be more sensitive to shared beliefs about the restorative value of nature and to direct comparison across conditions in a within-subjects design.

4.1 The detrimental impact of environmental degradation

When investigating the specific effect of environmental degradation on these benefits, we found a detrimental impact on affective outcomes (H1b), but no evidence of an effect on attentional (H1a) and perceived restorative (H1c) outcomes. Specifically, post-exposure negative affect (adjusted for pre-exposure levels) was higher in the littered than in the clean forest. Descriptively, this difference appears to reflect an attenuated decrease rather than an increase: negative affect tended to decrease from baseline in the clean forest but remained close to baseline in the littered forest (Figure 3). No evidence was, however, found for an effect of environmental degradation on positive affect. This dissociation underscores the importance of measuring positive and negative affect as independent dimensions rather than opposite poles of a single continuum, notably when studying nature's benefits (Bratman & Gross, 2025; Walle & Dukes, 2023). One possible explanation could account for both the detrimental effect of degradation on negative affect and the absence of effect on the other outcomes. With natural elements (trees, sounds, vegetation) remaining intact, the two pieces of litter constituted only a localized, background element within a much broader natural scene. This subtle degradation may have elicited irritation, disgust, or concern, by reminding participants of humans' disregard for the environment. However, it may have left intact the positive affect (e.g., sense of interest or inspiration) elicited by the natural scene as a whole and not disrupt the broader perceptual and attentional processes sustaining perceived restorativeness, mental bandwidth and effortless visual engagement with nature. This interpretation is also consistent with the exploratory comparison of the degraded forest with the urban environment, which converged with the pattern of results observed for nature in general except for negative affect. These mixed findings align with prior research, which has consistently reported an effect of environmental degradation on negative affect, whereas effects on positive affect and perceived restorativeness have been found in some studies (Bielinis et al., 2024; Wyles et al., 2016) but not others (Menzel et al., 2024). This discrepancy may reflect methodological differences. The detrimental effects of litter on positive affect and perceived restorativeness tend to emerge when litter is abundant (e.g., an illegal open dump) and placed in the foreground of participants' field of vision (Bielinis et al., 2024; Wyles et al., 2016). By contrast, we opted for more subtle traces of litter to prevent participants from deducing our hypotheses, and to ensure that our degradation closely resembled what they might actually encounter in the forest. Considered alongside previous studies, our findings raise the possibility that the magnitude and form of environmental degradation determine which psychological benefits are disrupted.

4.2 Mediation of nature's benefits by eye-movement activity

In addition to the finding that litter was associated with negative affect, whereas we found no evidence of corresponding differences in eye-movement activity, mediation analyses provided little evidence that gaze indices accounted for condition-related differences in affect or perceived restorativeness (H2). These findings should not, however, be interpreted as evidence that the affective and cognitive benefits of nature operate through distinct pathways. During free viewing without a task, fixation rate and duration primarily reflect visual exploration and are influenced by low-level scene properties, such as visual complexity (Franěk & Petružálek, 2025; Valtchanov & Ellard, 2015), rather than directly indexing attention restoration. By contrast, cognitive benefits of nature have been most consistently demonstrated in tasks taxing executive functions, including working memory, cognitive flexibility, and attentional control (Ohly et al., 2016; Stevenson et al., 2018). Gaze behavior during passive viewing may therefore be insufficient to capture the processes underlying these benefits. Rim et al. (2025) proposed that affective and cognitive benefits, although often co-occurring, may arise through separate mechanisms, with affective benefits more closely tied to environmental preference (Meidenbauer et al., 2020; Stenfors et al., 2019). The selective association between litter and negative affect is consistent with this account, as litter reduces preference for natural settings (Wyles et al., 2016), but testing this hypothesis would require combining in situ exposure with executive function tasks and preference measures. Yet one indirect effect emerged between nature-versus-urban exposure and mental bandwidth through fixation rate: Although consistent with ART, which proposes that nature engages attention through soft fascination, allowing attention to be sustained with relatively little effort and thereby preserving mental resources (Basu et al., 2018; Kaplan, 1995), this effect was the only significant (non-zero) indirect effect across 16 mediation models. However, the effect size was small and the direct effect of nature-versusurban exposure on mental bandwidth was not significant. Moreover, the internal consistency of the Mental Bandwidth Scale was modest. This finding should therefore be considered exploratory and requires replication.

4.3 Moderators of nature's benefits and degradation impact

Beyond the main effects of nature exposure on psychological outcomes, our findings also revealed that higher nature connectedness was associated with a larger perceived restorative advantage of natural over urban environments (H3c). Specifically, the perceived restorative advantage of natural over urban environments increased with individuals' level of nature connectedness, such that those with higher nature connectedness reported a greater difference in perceived restorativeness between the two environments. This finding is consistent with Wilkie and Stavridou (2013), who similarly found that individuals higher in nature connectedness perceived natural environments as more restorative. However, contrary to previous findings linking nature connectedness to greater affective benefits from nature exposure (McMahan et al., 2018) and differences in visual engagement with natural environments (Batool et al., 2022; Chen et al., 2022), we found no evidence of moderation on affective (H3b) or attentional (H3a) outcomes. Moreover, consistent with Wyles et al. (2016), we found no evidence that nature connectedness moderate responses to degraded versus preserved natural environments. This suggests that nature connectedness may primarily enhance the perceived restorative benefits associated with being in nature, while not necessarily translating into greater sensitivity to differences in ecological quality within natural environments, though this effect warrants replication in a larger sample.

Although the moderating effect of eco-anxiety on positive affect following nature exposures (H4b) did not remain significant after correction, the observed pattern was consistent with our hypothesis. Participants with higher eco-anxiety reported greater positive affect following exposure to the preserved compared to the degraded natural environment, whereas no such difference emerged among participants with lower or average eco-anxiety.

The relatively subtle nature of our degradation manipulation may have contributed to the overall limited magnitude of this effect, which was further reduced when participants who did not notice the litter were included. Although these findings should be considered exploratory, the observed pattern raises the possibility that environmental degradation may attenuate reductions in negative affect and, among individuals reporting higher eco-anxiety, may also attenuate gains in positive affect.

We also examined whether the amount of attention participants allocated to litter, indexed by relative dwell time and number of visits, was related to how strongly litter affected their responses (H5). We reasoned that participants who looked at the litter more would show larger differences between the clean and littered forests, but we found no evidence for such an association. One speculative explanation is that the psychological impact of litter may not require direct, foveal fixation. Once detected, litter may remain perceptually available even when participants do not overtly attend to it, as it can remain within their peripheral visual field. Our eye-tracking measures captured overt visual attention through gaze position but did not allow us to determine whether litter was processed peripherally in the absence of direct fixation. Thus, awareness of the presence of litter, or its mere presence within the visual field, may be sufficient to influence affective responses. Finally, the proportion of attention participants allocated toward litter was not associated with either nature connectedness or eco-anxiety (H6). Thus, it remains unclear what drives individuals to attend to environmental degradation, and, in turn, how such attentional capture shapes their subjective experience of nature exposure.

4.4 Limitations of the present study

Our conclusions are subject to several limitations. First, the sample was relatively small and predominantly young, and statistical power was limited for some of the preservedversus-degraded contrasts. Second, environmental degradation was operationalized using only two pieces of litter in a single forest context; the findings therefore should not be generalized to more extensive or qualitatively different forms of environmental degradation.

Third, the urban comparator was deliberately quiet and visually simple, meaning that differences in gaze behavior may partly reflect lower-level visual and structural properties of the scenes rather than naturalness alone. Fourth, although the in situ design increased ecological validity relative to picture-based paradigms, participants remained seated and were instructed to minimize head and body movements. Fifth, fixation dynamics provide indices of overt gaze behavior but do not uniquely index directed-attention effort, and no preexposure cognitive measure was available to establish attentional change from baseline.

Accordingly, the eye-tracking findings should be interpreted as being consistent with, rather than as direct evidence for, ART-derived predictions. Finally, internal consistency of the Mental Bandwidth Scale was modest in the present sample, which may have reduced sensitivity and warrants caution when interpreting findings involving this measure.

5. Conclusion

This study provides further insights into the psychological benefits of real-world nature exposure. Using an in situ within-subject design, we found that exposure to a forest, compared with an urban environment, resulted in lower attentional activity, improved affective states, and greater perceived restorativeness. The affective benefits observed in the absence of an explicit depletion induction are consistent with the possibility that nature exposure can produce instorative as well as restorative effects. Importantly, most nature-urban differences remained detectable even in the littered forest, though litter was associated with a less favorable negative-affect response. These findings underscore the psychological benefits of nature exposure and the importance of maintaining the quality of natural environments to preserve these benefits, with implications for the design and maintenance of settings used in nature-based interventions.
