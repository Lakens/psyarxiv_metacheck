Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine, Daniel Lakens and Cristian Mesquida

Overall, among all instances flagged as power statements, 90.6% were correct (positive predictive value).

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). Statcheck might still miss one-sided tests that are described in another sentence, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on three hand-coded sets of papers (scienceverse/validation: 249 Psychological Science, 250 Economics and 250 MDPI papers). Of 1,783 non-significant p-values in the ground truth, version 0.4.4.3 found 1,719 (3.6% missed), and 94.8% of its 1,814 detections were in the ground truth. How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. Abstract

Humans are always facing new tasks, but they are not forced to solve them from scratch, since they are almost always able to leverage previous experience to scaffold current learning. We propose that what connects past to present is task-general representations: abstract knowledge of the structure shared across tasks that serves as a foundation for new learning. Treating tasks performed in the past as samples from an unknown task distribution, we review how properties of the observed samples shape task general representations. We apply the same lens to two different timescales; learning over the course of minutes to days and development over the course of months to years. We then consider how people exert control over their own task distributions. We propose that exploration, by diversifying experience, keeps task-general representations adaptive because people choose their experiences using the very representations they are learning, errors in those representations can become self-sustaining. Specifically, we highlight directed and value-free random exploration as two distinct exploration strategies that correct different distortions in task-general representation.

[div-01]

Picture a student starting their first semester of university. Within a few weeks, they need to figure out how to juggle and perform well in a variety of college-level classes. None of these courses have been previously taken, yet most students are able to complete them reasonably well. This is achieved by generalizing from past experiences to the novel task at hand. Learning a class in one semester follows a gradual process, and attending office hours is usually conducive to understanding STEM class materials while course participation matters less. The reuse of knowledge not only helps people become proficient at a task quickly, but also has a lasting impact over longer timescales. For example, the interpersonal skills people learn throughout education help them when entering the workforce even if they have never interacted with people in a work environment before.

Representation learning, the process of mapping the raw observations into internal state representation, is helpful for discovering which aspects of the environment are relevant for solving the current task (1) (2) (3). However, knowing what to learn and generalize from past tasks to a novel task is still difficult. Each task is unique, and there lacks a clear signal indicating which lesson apply broadly and which were specific to one situation. A student who did well in a course cannot easily tell whether they should attribute getting a good grade to office hours, a particularly helpful instructor, or the subject itself. If they decide to follow one feature-outcome link e.g., taking all classes provided by the same instructor, they may end up taking all the classes from the same instructor which keeps them from trying otherwise, and they may never discover that the relationship is not the ground truth. One way to connect past to current is to learn about what has held true across many of them i.e., a task-general representation. The representation includes the features that are important for solving the task family as well as how important each feature is. Formally, it can be described as a prior over feature-outcome weights, learned across tasks and used to initialize learning in a new one.

A task-general representation differs from a task-specific representation in three ways. First, it retains only features that matter across tasks. Features such as completing an extra problem set may increase final grade in one class, yet across all the courses a student has taken, it bears little relation to outcome, therefore receiving negligible weight. Second, features of a taskgeneral representation are more abstract than task-specific representations because they are extracted across tasks. In the course learning example, the feature of course materials could encompass homework, readings, and lectures. Third, the task-general representation carries uncertainty aggregated across tasks that is influenced by both the amount of experience and the consistency across past experiences. For instance, a senior who has found office hours helpful in nearly every course can rely on them in a new class, whereas a first-year student with only a semester of experience cannot yet tell whether the same is true. Even for the senior, features whose value has varied across courses, such as group projects that helped in some classes but not others, remain uncertain and must be reevaluated in each new course.

We view the formation of a task-general representation as a meta-learning problem with nested inner and outer loops (4, 5). At the same time of learning to solve a single task (inner loop), the learner extracts a template shared across a family of tasks (outer loop) and uses it as an adaptive prior for future learning. This framing speaks to the central question in developmental research, that is, how early experiences shapes later learning. Because early experiences typically span a range of tasks, studying how task-general representations are learned and how they change offers a common framework for understanding how previous experiences influence learning across timescales, ranging from one experiment session to different developmental stages.

The properties of a task-general representation depend on the set of tasks from which it was learned. When only one task is examined, it is difficult to tease apart task-specific and taskgeneral representation. Identifying factors influencing task-general representation, therefore, requires a multi-task setup where transfer among tasks can be measured (6). A learner's history can be described as a finite, ordered sample of tasks drawn from an unknown task distribution, each experienced through noisy data (7). Such a sample is characterized by which tasks it contains, how tasks relate to each other, the number of tasks, and the order in which they arrive. These properties have counterparts in Bayesian accounts of concept learning, where generalization depends on the coverage, size, ordering, and exemplar features observed (Xu & Tenenbaum, 2007). In this review, we synthesize how these factors influence the learning of task-general representation.

Learners are not passive recipients of experience. They act on the world to gather new information and adjust the internal representation through which experience is interpreted (9). Externally, by exploring different task dimensions (e.g., first time discussing problem set with classmates), people can learn about the task structure and introducing new information into the evolving task-general representation (10). Exploration is particularly valuable for learning a taskgeneral rather than a task-specific representation because its benefits accrue over the long term. The value of the information about shared structure can inform every future task in which that structure recurs (Baram et al., 2026). Internally, on the other hand, learners can weaken, reset, or switch the representation when it no longer fits. (12) (13) (14). Theories of generalization suggest that people can compare the current experience with previous instances and generalize based on task similarity, and people's generalization is best described as a hybrid model combining rule-based i.e., knowledge about shared structure, and similarity-based processes (15).

These ideas draw on literatures that have developed largely in parallel. Research on cognitive science has examined how task representations support generalization in situations where the tasks are well trained within a short time period such as one experimental session (16, 17). Developmental psychology has shown, on longer timescales, that early life experiences impact people's ability to learn later in life (5), but lack an overarching framework to unify research and derive principled way to make predictions. Recent advances in machine learning have formalized the problem of multi-task learning and identified failure modes from the perspective of algorithm design for artificial agents, while pay little attention to how humans continually learn in a multi-task environment (18). Here we bring these threads together to answer two questions: what are the factors influencing task-general representation learning, and how to learn a good task-general representation?

Glossary

Generalization

Applying what was learned in past experiences to new situations.

Reuse of knowledge

Drawing on knowledge acquired from past experiences to guide learning and behavior in a new task

Representation learning

The process of mapping raw observations onto an internal representation that captures the aspects of the environment relevant for prediction and action.

Task-general representation

Shared knowledge, abstracted across tasks, of which features matter, which can be ignored, and how important each feature is.

Task-specific representation

Knowledge of which features matter and how they relate to outcomes within a single task.

Meta-learning

The process of learning to learn.

Transfer

The effect of learning one task on learning or performance in another.

Transfer is positive when prior learning helps and negative when it interferes.

Learning trap

A situation in which choices guided by a mistaken belief prevent the learner from gathering the evidence that would correct it, allowing the error to persist.

Directed exploration

Choosing options in proportion to how much information they are expected to provide, typically modeled as an information bonus that scales with uncertainty and is added to an option's expected value.

Random exploration

Injecting variability into choice. In its value-free form (e.g., ε-greedy), options are chosen at random regardless of their estimated value or uncertainty.

What shapes a task-general representation?

Ideally, the sample of previously experienced tasks would approximate the underlying task distribution so that the learned representation is a good proxy of the latent structure of the task distribution. Because the task distribution is unknown, sampling tasks that differ from one another i.e., creating a diverse task portfolio, is a useful heuristic (19). Features that remain predictive across varied tasks are likely to be shared structure, whereas features that differ massively in importance task to task are likely to be task-specific. Consider two students in a flipped-classroom course. One has taken only lecture-based courses while the other has taken both lecture-based and seminar courses. When being asked to participate in class, the first one will have a harder time seeing student speaking up in class as a kind of course than the second one, and may be slower in applying the learned weight on course materials to the student presentation.

The positive effect of diversity on generalization has been empirically tested across timescales. In concept learning, variable exemplars support broader generalization (20) with the spread of examples determining the level of abstraction at which a new word is generalized (Xu & Tenenbaum, 2007). Over longer timescale, diverse experience has been linked to an enhanced capacity to learn new tasks (21). For example, bilingual children are more flexible in learning speech structure, which is attributed to higher diversity in language input than monolingual children (22). Machine learning literature formalizes the same intuition as well, showing that transfer is guaranteed only when training tasks are diverse enough that span the dimensions on which new tasks depend (19). Conversely, a task set with little variation leaves the task-general representation underspecified in two ways. First, features that never mattered across past tasks are not included in the learned representation, which slows later learning that depends on them. We term this distortion as a blind spot. In one study on category learning, participants initially learned a category determined by two of three stimulus dimensions. They were slower to learn a category that depended on the previously irrelevant dimension than a category that depended on one of the previously relevant dimensions (Kruschke, 1996). Second, features may covary in the task set (e.g., the exam always takes place on Wednesday afternoon), in which case the learners cannot determine which feature drives the outcome. This can lead to learned spurious feature-outcome relationship (e.g., always prepare for exam on Wednesday morning) in both humans and machines (24).

In addition to diversity, higher-order environmental statistics across tasks influences the learned task-general representation as well. Compare the following two learning histories. In the first case, every class rubric states that 5% of the final grade relies on class participation, whereas in the second set the weight on class participation varies between 0% to 10% across classes, roughly composing a uniform distribution. Although the average weight of class participation can be equated in both cases, the second case leads to more uncertainty over the learned weight (Fig. 2). This example reflects the distinction between the controllability and predictability of the environment (25), both of which has been shown to impact learning (26). In our framework, a low controllability task set leads to a learned certain small weight on actions initiated by the learner themselves. Based on the prior knowledge that exerting effort matters less than other components (e.g., the overall rate of getting an A), the learner would be less willing to exert effort, developing behavior patterns resembling learned helplessness. This is in line with existing proposal that people actively infer controllability in the environment and use this to guide future behavior (27, 28). If the learner senses lower level of control, they will rely more on the learned value of the stimulus independent of their action. Unpredictability, on the other hand, leads to high uncertainty in learned feature-outcome association. Since the previous learning history is uncertain about the specific relationship, the learner is going to rely more on the incoming information, which could manifest as a higher learning rate (29). The two statistics thus produce different distortions. Unpredictability leaves weights uncertain, whereas uncontrollability leaves weights on one's own actions confidently low.

On a longer timescale, the environmental statistics during early life have a profound impact on later learning in life. Classic work in rodents showed that variation in maternal care alters stress reactivity in adulthood, suggesting that early caregiving calibrates development (30, 31). Later work showed that in addition to the amount of caregiving, its temporal pattern matters. When maternal care is fragmented and inconsistent, offspring show deficits in reward processing (Baram et al., 2012; Molet et al., 2016). Developmental psychology research has also highlighted the influence of early life unpredictability (ELU), separate from other dimensions of early life adversity, on cognitive and emotion development (34) (35) (36) (37). Different from the definition in our framework, ELU refers to variability in the signals a child receives from the environment, such as inconsistent caregiver responses or frequent changes in residence (38). From the learner's perspective, the same variability in observed outcomes can arise from either unpredictability, uncontrollability, or both. Current measures of ELU, which index the variability of the environment rather than its dependence on the child's actions, likely capture a mixture of both. Since our framework predicts that uncontrollability and unpredictability have distinct influence on task-general representation, distinguishing them would be important to develop interventions for learning better representation, which we discuss in the next section.

A longer learning history generally improves the learned representation, but not without limit. Early research show that both monkey and children's few-shot transfer learning ability increases monotonically as a function of the number of tasks that they have been trained on in one experiment session, up to 56 tasks for children and 355 for monkeys (39). Yet prolonged accumulated experience can also entrench a representation, the phenomenon of which has been described as plasticity loss in artificial neural network (40). Trained on a long sequence of tasks (up to 2000 tasks), standard deep network initially benefits from prior training but gradually lose the ability to learn new ones, eventually performing no better than a shallow network.

Over the human life span, where a considerable number of tasks are experienced, a related pattern may emerge. As experience accumulates, the uncertainty of the task-general representation shrinks, making it hard to incorporate information from the current task to flexibly adjust behavior. Consistent with this proposal, older adults underweight uncertainty when learning in changing environment, adjusting their learning rate less than young adults (41, 42). More broadly, age-related changes in decision-making have been formalized as an increased reliance on prior knowledge over information in the current task, and some cognitive decline may reflect the cost of accumulated learning (43). Notably, the developmental trajectory is not monotonic. In a changepoint task administered across the lifespan, children and older adults both perseverated more and updated less than adolescents and younger adults (44), so that flexible adaptation peaked between childhood and old age. Within our framework, the two ends of this pattern may arise for opposite reasons. Older adults possess an entrenched task-general representation that resists new evidence, whereas children have yet to accumulate one, therefore lacking a well-calibrated prior about when change is likely and how much to update. Sustained learning across tasks thus requires mechanisms that counteract entrenchment and preserve representational flexibility.

Given a fixed set of tasks, how the tasks are sequentially organized, i.e., the curriculum, influences task-general representation as well. Artificial networks trained sequentially on tasks suffer catastrophic forgetting, overwriting earlier tasks unless training is interleaved (45). Humans, in contrast, can benefit from blocked training, which promotes learning factorized task representations that resist interference (46). Across domains, the benefit of interleaving tend to be most prominent in motor learning, whereas blocked training is usually advantageous when learning perceptual and cognitive tasks (13). Over longer timescales, interleaved practice improves academic performance in an STEM education setting than its blocked counterpart (47). The optimal curriculum thus depends on both the task domain and the timescale.

Early tasks do not merely precede later ones but set the prior expectations through which later experience is interpreted. Order effects -the sequence in which experiment conditions are presented influences how participants respond -has long been documented in psychology research, alluding to an asymmetry in the impact of early task on later learning (48). A recent study illustrates this asymmetry in learning the temporal structure of the environment (49). Participants learned two temporal structures through a predictive-inference task in counterbalanced orders. In a changepoint environment, large prediction errors (PE) signal real change and requires rapid updating. In an oddball environment, large PE reflect one-off outliers and should be ignored. Participants successfully learned the appropriate policy for whichever environment they encountered first, but those who experienced changepoint first persisted in responding to large PE in the subsequent oddball environment. This result suggests that it is more difficult to modify the learned positive link between update and PE than a learned discounted relationship. On a longer timescale, the impact of task order on learning has long been highlighted in the domain of education. Practitioners adopt the strategy of decompose complex material into simpler components that are mastered first i.e., divide and conquer strategy (50) so that early experiences establishes the structure which scaffolds later learning.

Tasks that are poorly sequenced will lead to interference in task-general representation, where learning a new task can overwrite or be blocked by what was learned before.

How to learn a good task-general representation?

A task-general representation helps solve the problem of continual learning. With an appropriate shared representation (e.g., learning a STEM class), people can adapt to a stream of new tasks (e.g., learning applied math electives) without starting over from scratch each time or overwriting what has been learned. A representation that misrepresents the task distribution may impede learning. A student who learned in high school that completing homework is sufficient may keep reviewing notes for college courses, where grades instead relate to class participation. Since the alternative (i.e., asking a question during class) is never tried, the mistaken weight is never corrected. This failure mode is termed as learning trap: people make choices guided by a mistaken belief which prevents them from gathering the evidence that would revise it (51). A good task-general representation, therefore, is evaluated by whether it can approximate and identifying the shared structure in the task distribution, which subsequently facilitates new task learning.

We argue that exploration, the act of choosing options that are not currently believed to be the most rewarding (52), plays a key role in learning a good task-general representation. The distortions reviewed in the previous section share a property that makes them self sustaining, that is, learners choose their experiences based on the representation that is distorted. Exploration, therefore, breaks the confirmatory loop that leads to learning trap and happens at both withintask and across task level. After finishing a semester, a student can decide what class they want to take next. Within a course, a student can choose how they want to allocate time among activities such as reviewing course material, going to office hour, and attending study group. Specifically, exploration matters more for learning task-general than task-specific representation. Within a task, the value of exploration pays off only over the remaining choices, and a learner can reasonably stop exploring once performance is satisfactory. For a task-general representation, in contrast, the information gathered pays off across all future tasks that shares the structure.

Existing research on exploration, mostly conducted in a single-task setting, has identified two general strategies that are effective in maximizing long-term reward (53-56): directed and random exploration (Fig. 2). Below we describe these two families of strategies and discuss the distortions each is more suited to correct (Table 1).

Directed exploration resolves uncertainty

Directed exploration biases choices towards the options whose outcomes are uncertain, adding an information bonus proportional to the uncertainty estimate to their expected value (57). Across tasks, it drives learners to disengage from the current task that they are already familiar with and explore towards novel tasks, thereby increasing experiential diversity (58). The preference for information and novelty emerges early in development (59, 60), and artificial agents that explore based on novelty learn representations that transfer better to new tasks (61), corroborating the effectiveness of directed exploration on generalization. . Directed exploration is closely related to the process of hypothesis testing. Conditioned on the current hypothesis about the world, people direct their choices towards the option that provide information that supports or falsify the hypothesis. In causal learning tasks, people seek information to understand the causal structure of the machine, with the efficiency with which they sample to reduce uncertainty about the hypotheses improves through development (62). This hypothesis testing behavior is also observed during value-based decision-making, where participants combine hypothesis testing and feature learning at the same time (63, 64). Compared to incrementally learning the values associated with the features, hypothesis testing enables rapid change in the dimension being attended to, increasing the flexibility of the learning process. It is particularly suited to finding blind spot and break spurious links. By varying one (or more) features while holding others fixed, the learner can directly test feature-outcome relationships that were ambiguous due to consistent co-occurrence of features across previous experience.

Exploration is especially important in sequential settings, where the effect of a feature can be temporally extended: an action shapes later states and outcomes, not just immediate reward (52). Identifying such effects requires solving a temporal credit assignment problem (65) and usually a single choice is insufficient to identify the relationship between feature and outcome. In this setting, directed exploration can take the form of repetition rather than a switch from previous choices. One recent study has found empirical support of this argument.

Participants learned a set of options that vary in the magnitude of short and long-term reward. Those who repeated the same option back-to-back learned the long-term consequences of activities much better than those who frequently switched (Bruinsma et al., 2024.). This suggests that perseveration, often regarded as a simple or maladaptive policy, may reflect a form of directed exploration that utilizes the structure of the environment to isolate and learn the delayed effects of an action. More broadly, it shows that the learner can determine the order of their own experiences, therefore counteract the distortions that arise from poorly sequenced past tasks.

Value-free random exploration revisits what has been dismissed

Random exploration increases the overall stochasticity of behavior and takes on different forms based on how randomness is injected (56). One simple implementation is value-free random exploration, which injects a constant level of noise to people's choice thereby increasing the possibility of trying out all options except for the current best one (52). It is computationally inexpensive because it does not require tracking value or uncertainty in the environment, and has been shown to facilitate transfer learning in a multi-task setting (Xu et al., 2024). One recent study compared the effectiveness of different strategies in scientific discovery, and random chosen experiments can best approximate the underlying distribution than other sampling strategies that conditioned on what has been learned from the sampled data (68). In this case, its simplicity of not relying on existing knowledge also prevent it from being biased. For the same reason, value-free exploration can reach exactly the options that a distorted representation has ruled out, such as features that are deemed not important from a high uncontrollability environment.

More sophisticated forms of random exploration, therefore, are not necessarily better for learning a task-general representation. One recent study investigates the social representation people learned after interacting with social partners that generate reward from the same distribution. During learning, people used Thompson sampling, a random exploration algorithm that matches the probability of action to the probability of its being the optimal choice (69). After learning, the participants formed arbitrary preference over partners (e.g., prefer A over other partners) while all partners are identical i.e., they formed inaccurate representation (70). This is because a rewarding enough initial arbitrary interaction discourages people from investigating others that would be equal or better, which is actually amplified by Thompson sampling. In contrast, if the participant receive information generated using a value-free random exploration strategy, they formed less biased impressions (70, 71).

Promoting exploration

Given the importance of exploration in learning a good task-general representation, how can it be promoted? Directed exploration is driven by the information gained during interaction, therefore can be promoted by raising the value of information relative to reward. This can be achieved by emphasizing information and/or downplaying reward. Concretely, Bai and colleagues (2025) have tried three different interventions including adding monetary bonus proportional to uncertainty, making a subset of partners temporarily unavailable to be selected to maintain high uncertainty, and reducing the overall reward magnitude. All three manipulations are proven effective in reducing the learned inaccurate social representation, though the least biased representation was obtained when information was sampled using value-free random exploration. Following the logic of emphasizing information over reward, another possible intervention is to decouple exploration from reward entirely. Although interventions of this kind has not been tested yet, recent work has corroborated the link between reward-free context and increased exploration, showing that people engage in more directed exploration during planning than execution (72).

In addition to altering the incentive structure in the environment, it is also possible to nudge directed exploration by changing people's internal state such as their attitude towards uncertainty. A more positive attitude towards uncertainty could function similar to amplifying the information bonus, thereby encouraging directed exploration. Consistent with this argument, negative affective states that heighten aversion to uncertainty, such as anxiety and worry, have been associated with reduced directed exploration (73, 74). Importantly, there is evidence showing that manipulating the internal affective state could lead to a change in directed exploration. People explore less after an aversive interoceptive state induction that increases state anxiety (75), and engage in more directed exploration after a brief psychotherapy-inspired intervention that reduced worry (76). Interventions can also target people's view on uncertainty directly. After an intervention framing uncertainty as an opportunity, the extent to which participants endorsed this view predicted their directed exploration (77). It is worth noting that this relationship was absent in participants who did not receive the intervention, suggesting that making uncertainty salient is a prerequisite to allows people's beliefs about uncertainty to shape how they explore.

People also engage in random exploration, or selection of options independent of their value or associated uncertainty, and random exploration shows different dynamics and dependencies than directed exploration. Random exploration is sensitive to the decision context, showing an increase when facing a longer decision horizon (78). Alternatively, as shown in Bai et al (2022, 2025), value-independent samples can be imposed externally through forced choices so that learners encounter information that their current policy would not lead them to seek. (70, 71). From the perspective of changing the learner's internal state, previous work has shown that propranolol reduced value-free exploration while leaving directed exploration intact (79), suggesting that manipulating noradrenaline could also change people's tendency to engage in value-free random exploration.

The remedy offered through exploration should match the distortion in task-general representation (Table 1). We have proposed that unpredictability leaves high uncertainty around the learned feature-outcome relationships. In this case, promoting directed exploration is likely to be helpful to lead the learner to engage in actions to gather more information about the feature weight. On the other hand, directed exploration is unlikely to reverse the effects of uncontrollability. We theorized that uncontrollability leads to consistently small weight, which carries little information bonus. Instead, promoting value-free exploration is helpful to generate evidence about the controllability of the current environment.

Beyond one's own exploration

Diverse experience serves as a proxy for the distribution of future tasks (80). People do not need to acquire every experience themselves and could instead benefit from the learning of others (81, 82). Observation learning -one form of social learning -is not influenced by the physical constraint of the individual learner, therefore has the potential of providing high novelty (e.g., college life in a different country). It is also of low cost, which could promote the learner to seek out social information in an exploratory way and further increase the experience diversity. Finally, humans learn from others not merely by tracking which action leads to reward, but by inferring the goals behind them (83), which helps task-general representation above and beyond the social data itself. The advantage of social learning is also corroborated in machine learning research, where artificial agents trained with a mixture of social and solo experience perform better in new tasks than those that only have access to information obtained through their own interactions with the environment (84).

Whereas observation learning provides samples from which rules can be inferred, instructed learning can transmits rules themselves across timescales (81). When teaching others, people convey complex concepts more effectively through language than through demonstration, because language can refer directly to abstract features rather than to specific instances (85). It can also be used to relay abstract relationships, which helps learners avoid spurious correlation (86).

Update internal representation directly

Exploration changes the incoming sample from the external world. Internally, learners can change how the existing representation is used. When the current representation repeatedly fails, it may be more adaptive to weaken its influence than to keep refining it. Machine learning literature has offered a direct analogy: resetting a subset of network weights restore plasticity (40) and reduces the impact of early experiences (87).

Up until this point, we have assumed a single task-general representation underlying all tasks. In reality, the picture is likely to be more complicated. Tasks are naturally clustered by content (e.g., taking classes vs. making friends), and learners could maintain multiple representations and infer which one applies to the current situation (13). If behaviors based on one representation keep generating large prediction errors, it may suggest that instead of adjusting the current representation, the learner should load a different one. Indeed, forming and using different representations when encountering high prediction errors has been proposed as one solution to avoid catastrophic forgetting (88, 89). Information about different tasks is stored in distinct representations, preventing them from interfering with one another. The challenge for the learner shifts from refining one representation to deciding when to update the current representation, when to retrieve a previous one, and when to create a new one, a problem that is under active investigation in both cognitive science (12, (90) (91) (92) and machine learning (93, 94).

Concluding Remarks

Life is a never-ending learning experience. People constantly encounter new tasks and rely on what they already know to facilitate new task learning. Task-general representations make this possible, allowing learners to carry knowledge from past experience into new situations instead of starting anew. We argued that the task-general representations reflect the learner's learning history across timescales, at the same time influenced by the composition and the learning order of the past task set. Because exploiting a representation yields mostly confirmatory evidence, exploration is essential to keep it adaptive. Specifically, we propose that different kinds of exploration are suitable for correct different distortions, with directed exploration targeting the uncertain parts in the representation and value-free random exploration increasing the overall choice stochasticity. Beyond one's own exploration, social learning and language widen experience further. From an internal state perspective, when a representation no longer fits, internal signals may help learners update it, retrieve a better one, or create a new one.

This perspective opens up a whole stream of new questions. First, it would be interesting to understand the frequency and magnitude of the task-general representation update. Does it get updated continuously during a task or primarily between tasks? Second, the uncertainty in the task is compound from the within task level to the across task level. How do people integrate the uncertainty across levels into the task-general representation? Third, in addition to early life adversity, our framework can potentially be extended to examine and understand decision pathology in mental health and potentially develop corresponding intervention strategy to help learn more appropriate task-general representations. Addressing these questions will require multi-task, multi-timescale designs that track how representations form and change.
