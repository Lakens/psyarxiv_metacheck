Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard), also Internal Review Board - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term); not IEC followed by a number, which is a technical standard - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee/Commission, in the singular or plural - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee, Bioethics Committee/Commission, Ethikkommission - Ethical Review Authority/Unit/Office/Agency - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Human Research Protection Office/Program, Office for Human Subjects Protection - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee(s) on/for (Medical and Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt - Ethics review that was not needed: Ethics review was not required, did not require further ethical review, exempt from formal human research ethics review, no new institutional ethics review

A template line the authors have not filled in (“[Add ethics approval … here.]”) is not a statement.

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

When a statement is expected

The module sorts each paper by where its data came from (the internal .data_source(); the signs and how the rule was tested are described in R/data-source.R): - own: the authors collected data from people or animals, for this paper, in an earlier study of their own, or (a protocol) will collect them; - existing_records: records about individual people that the authors obtained for this study, such as hospital records or a cohort; - existing_unclear: existing data about people from a source the module could not identify; - open_resource: an open resource any researcher can use, such as UK Biobank or NHANES; - none: no data about people or animals (a review, simulation, commentary, theory, tutorial, coding of published papers, or figures about groups); - uncertain: the signs were missing or pointed both ways; - no_text: the paper has no text.

A statement is expected for own, existing_records and existing_unclear. A paper without a statement in these classes, or in uncertain, is flagged. The module reads the text, and the results of power, stat_p_exact, stat_check, repo_check, data_check and prereg_check when they ran before it (run it after them); without stat_p_exact, it finds the p-values in the text.

On 199 PsyArXiv and medRxiv preprints labelled after the rule was fixed, the module decided right whether a statement is needed for 86.9% (the earlier live-data search: 83.4%). Its main errors: other people’s studies described in the words of a new study, records and specimens described only as counts, and open resources the paper does not call open.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 685 tests with 34 instances of inconsistent reporting, Statcheck correctly detected 34 of them, and incorrectly identified 26. Therefore 0% of true instances were missed, and 43% of detections were false positives. See https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/ for more details of the validation.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on 226 Psychological Science papers with 5,019 hand-coded p-values, 438 of them imprecise (scienceverse/validation, branch stat_p_exact). Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). Of the misses, 13 are starred table notes (“*p < .05”) that the module leaves out on purpose and 11 are p-values it did not find; of the wrong reports, 20 are p-values the ground truth does not contain (such as “ps < .05”) and 6 use ≤ or ≥. These figures measure agreement with the hand-coding, which has known errors in both directions (scienceverse/validation#48).

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on three hand-coded sets of papers (scienceverse/validation: 249 Psychological Science, 250 Economics and 250 MDPI papers). Of 1,783 non-significant p-values in the ground truth, version 0.4.4.3 found 1,719 (3.6% missed), and 94.8% of its 1,814 detections were in the ground truth. Some differences come from the ground truth: it lacks p-values with a correction name (“p Bonf = .061”), and counts partial eta squared written with its subscript after it (“η 2 p = .05”), “p = .05”, and some table notes and figure legends (“† p < .10, *p < .05”), which this module leaves out, as non-significant p-values. How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Abstract

Cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT) is widely regarded as the most scientifically grounded form of psychotherapy, owing to the extensive evidence from randomized controlled trials and meta-analyses. This article argues that this reputation increasingly rests on borrowed credibility. CBT was originally founded on a coherent theoretical framework rooted in learning theory and experimental psychology.

It has since become an umbrella label for a heterogeneous set of protocols and techniques, from Beck's cognitive therapy to mindfulness-and acceptance-based approaches, schema therapy and EMDR, that share neither theoretical assumptions nor a common research base and that differ markedly in empirical support. Under this label, newer approaches inherit the scientific prestige earned by wellvalidated protocols without having to demonstrate their own connection to basic psychological research. I contend that CBT, as a unified framework, is no longer a scientific theory: individual protocols may be scientific, but only insofar as they combine empirical validation with theoretical coherence and a link to basic research. I examine the epistemological inconsistencies among the main strands of CBT, the limits of the trial evidence on which its status rests, and the conditions a psychotherapy must meet to be considered scientific. To prevent further fragmentation, I propose reconnecting psychotherapy with general psychology and learning theory, grounding new protocols 1 in explicit theory, complementing randomized trials with modern single-case experimental designs, and ultimately moving toward a psychotherapy without adjectives.

Introduction

Cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT) is generally considered an evidence-based psychotherapy, because numerous randomized controlled trials (RCTs) and meta-analyses attest to its efficacy, and many of its protocols are classified as empirically supported treatments (ESTs). This has contributed to its spread, both in private practice and in national health services. Currently, however, CBT is going through a profound epistemological and methodological crisis that raises more than one question about its scientific validity.

CBT derives from two psychotherapeutic traditions: behavior therapy and cognitive therapy. One of the founding texts of behavior therapy was Wolpe's Psychotherapy by reciprocal inhibition (Wolpe, 1958). Behavior therapy was essentially based on modifying patients' behaviors, whereas cognitive therapy was based on changing the cognitions underlying those behaviors. There are two main traditions in cognitive psychotherapy. The first is based on the work of Albert Ellis (Rational Emotive Behavior Therapy, REBT; Ellis, 1962) and Aaron T. Beck (cognitive therapy; Beck et al., 1979), although Judith Beck, Aaron T. Beck's daughter, prefers to call her father's approach "cognitive-behavior therapy" rather than "cognitive therapy" (see J. S. Beck, 1995). The second is the so-called constructivist approach, based on George Kelly's personal construct psychology (Kelly, 1955) and on Bowlby and Ainsworth's attachment theory (Bowlby, 1979). The first approach (Ellis and Beck) is more rationalist and focused on symptom eradication, whereas the second is considered more complex and dynamically oriented. Today, the umbrella term "cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT)" is used to indicate all these different approaches, even though some authors prefer to define their approach as "cognitive", "behavioral", "radical behavioral", or otherwise. These approaches are now represented by distinct international societies, from the Association for Behavior Analysis International to the World Confederation of Cognitive and Behavioural Therapies and dedicated societies for schema therapy and mindfulness. Medicine, too, offers competing treatments for the same condition, such as surgery, radiotherapy, and pharmacotherapy in oncology. What distinguishes it is that these alternatives rest on shared basic sciences, such as physiology, biochemistry, and pharmacology, against which they can be compared and explained. It is precisely this shared foundation in basic science that psychotherapy, including CBT, lacks.

This fragmentation poses a serious challenge to the identity of CBT, one that can no longer be postponed. CBT is no longer a unified therapy, but rather a set of different therapies under a common name. My central claim is that CBT now functions as an umbrella label that confers borrowed credibility: approaches grouped under it inherit the scientific prestige earned by well-validated, learning-based protocols without having to demonstrate their own theoretical and empirical grounding. In this sense, CBT as a unified framework is no longer a scientific theory, although many of its individual protocols are well supported. The aims of this paper are twofold: to evaluate the current scientific status of CBT and to propose a path toward greater scientific and epistemological coherence by reconnecting CBT with its original experimental basis.

Scientific foundations of CBT

Behavior therapy emerged at a time when the main psychotherapies were psychodynamic and were based on the theory of the unconscious and on free association. Behavior therapy therefore had to fight hard to be accepted, as the two approaches were radically different. Psychodynamic therapy rested on claims that could not be demonstrated, whereas behavior therapy was based on experimental psychology and learning theory. The emergence of behavior therapy thus represented an epistemological breakthrough in psychotherapy, both because of its eminently scientific approach and because of the importance it gave to the empirical verification of treatment outcomes. Behavior therapy had epistemological coherence and a solid experimental basis, as its principles derived from general psychology and learning theory (Pavlov's classical conditioning and Skinner's operant conditioning). CBT subsequently continued to use the techniques of behavior analysis while combining them with techniques aimed at modifying cognition. That the first cognitive behavioral approaches were not so different from behavior analysis is shown by the fact that Beck's well-known book on depression (Beck et al., 1979) not only stressed the importance of behavioral techniques but also stated that treatment should begin with these techniques and only then move on to cognitive restructuring. Indeed, according to Beck et al. (1979), the more severe the depression, the greater the need for behavioral techniques.

Today, CBT is considered the leading psychotherapy, both for its efficacy and for its scientific evidence, and it dominates the field of empirically supported treatments (ESTs). This dominance is demonstrated by the fact that an entire national psychotherapy service, the British one, is essentially 3. Desired behavior change may be affected through cognitive change." I would only add that, if cognitive activity affects behavior, the opposite is also true: changes in behavior affect cognitive activity.

The aim of CBT is the modification of both overt and covert behavior. The term "behavior modification" therefore differs from "cognitive behavioral therapy", because behavior modification aims to change only overt behaviors, whereas CBT aims to modify both overt and covert behaviors (at least in theory; in practice, the situation is somewhat different). Dozois et al. (2019, pp. 6-7) state, for example, that using a classical conditioning approach with a child with autism spectrum disorder falls within the realm of "behavior analysis" or "applied behavior analysis" and therefore lies outside CBT, because only psychotherapies that rely on cognitive mediation can be considered cognitive behavioral therapies. According to these authors, the same applies to purely cognitive therapies, because they lack the behavioral component. I disagree. All these models should be considered forms of CBT, differing only in their emphasis on overt (behavior analysis) or covert (cognitive therapies) behaviors, for the simple reason that overt and covert behaviors are closely interrelated.

According to Dozois et al. (2019), four historical factors led to the development of CBT from behavior therapy by the end of the 1960s: 1.

It had become evident that behavior analysis could not account for all human behavior; 2. Some problems (e.g., obsessional thinking) were difficult to conceptualize without also taking cognition into account; 3. Psychology was moving toward the cognitive revolution, represented by the informationprocessing model; 4. Research studies suggested that cognitive behavioral interventions were at least as effective as purely behavioral ones (Dozois et al., 2019), although this conclusion was contested (e.g., Ledwidge, 1978).

Both behavior analysis and early CBT (I am referring to Beck's model) shared a strong scientific basis, and the debate between the various strands of CBT could be summarized in a single question: which comes first, behavior or cognition? Which of the two determines the other? The two approaches therefore shared a scientific coherence deeply rooted in the psychology of learning. Subsequently, the situation changed, and this framework of epistemological coherence and grounding in experimental psychology came under increasing strain.

The progressive fragmentation of CBT Today, CBT is merely an umbrella for very different therapies, with different theories, different techniques, and very different levels of empirical support. In this case, the plural "therapies" is therefore more accurate than the singular "therapy".

We are witnessing a proliferation of CBT psychotherapies, including newer approaches such as mindfulness-based cognitive therapy (Segal et al., 2002), schema therapy (Young et al., 2003), acceptance and commitment therapy (ACT; Hayes et al., 1999), dialectical behavior therapy (Linehan, 1993), metacognitive therapy (Wells, 2009), eye movement desensitization and reprocessing (EMDR; Shapiro, 1989 Shapiro, , 1995)), and compassion-focused therapy (CFT; Gilbert, 2009).

On the one hand, these psychotherapies may have enriched therapists' clinical skills; on the other hand, they have introduced constructs (such as mindfulness, acceptance, and metacognition) that have undermined the common epistemological basis of CBT, as well as its connection with a common experimental model. Some of these approaches, such as metacognitive therapy, are derived from explicit theoretical models with experimental foundations (Wells & Matthews, 1994); these models, however, are largely independent of both learning theory and Beck's cognitive model.

These psychotherapies are based on different theoretical models. To give just a few examples, schema therapy draws, among other sources, on attachment theory, an ethological and evolutionary theory with a broad empirical basis. It is an integrative approach that combines elements of gestalt therapy, attachment theory, and classic CBT. Mindfulness has its roots in Buddhism and is based on shifting attention to the present moment. Both its theory and its techniques differ so markedly from those of first-and second-generation CBT that Segal et al. (2004) recall the epistemological crisis they experienced when, as CBT researchers, they became interested in mindfulness. This is because of the large differences between the theoretical model of CBT and that of mindfulness. While classic CBT seeks to change the content of dysfunctional thoughts, mindfulness-based approaches seek to change the person's relationship to those thoughts, accepting rather than disputing them. Proponents argue that accepting internal experience and changing overt behavior operate at different levels and are therefore compatible; whether this is the case, however, is an empirical question that has rarely been tested directly. EMDR was initially developed for the treatment of posttraumatic stress disorder (PTSD). It is based on linking traumatic memories with adaptive memories so that the latter replace the former. I include EMDR within CBT because its active ingredient appears to be exposure to traumatic memories: dismantling studies suggest that the eye movements add little to the effect of exposure (Davidson & Parker, 2001). EMDR thus illustrates the thesis of this paper: a technique whose efficacy can be explained by learning theory, accompanied by an independent theoretical model (adaptive information processing; Shapiro, 1995) that is not needed to account for its results.

Compassion-focused therapy uses compassion to relieve patients' suffering and helps them develop a compassionate self. Some of these interventions are considered psychotherapies and others merely techniques (such as mindfulness),foot_0 although this classification varies across authors.

Not only do we now have a proliferation of CBT psychotherapies based on different theoretical models, but their empirical support is often limited to a few disorders or a few trials, and their theoretical identity is unclear. For example, schema therapy, despite encouraging randomized trials for borderline personality disorder (Giesen-Bloo et al., 2006), is largely an integration of techniques drawn from various psychotherapies that do not appear to be well matched from a theoretical viewpoint. Schema therapy takes an empirical approach ("let's use what works") and seeks to unify these techniques within a single theory based on schemas. However, this theory does not account for everything that therapists of this orientation do in clinical practice. Mindfulness-based approaches, in turn, rest on acceptance, a principle that is at least prima facie in tension with classic CBT. Acceptance and commitment therapy is a partial exception: it is grounded in relational frame theory, an operant account of language and cognition (Hayes et al., 2001), and thus retains an explicit link with learning theory. Yet its central clinical constructs, such as acceptance, defusion, and values, are defined at a level only loosely connected to that basic research, and the link between relational frame theory and the clinical efficacy of ACT has received little direct testing. Constructivist and cognitive-evolutionary approaches (Guidano & Liotti, 1983; Liotti, 2001) rely on constructs, such as tacit meaning structures and internal working models, that are inferred rather than observed and are closer to psychodynamic theorizing than to experimental psychology; moreover, to my knowledge, the post-rationalist and cognitive-evolutionary models have never been evaluated in randomized controlled trials.

It follows from the above that CBT cannot be considered a single psychotherapy, nor does it have a common epistemological or research basis. Instead, it comprises different techniques and interventions that often have little in common.

Weaknesses of CBT

The greatest strength of CBT is that it is supported by randomized controlled trials (RCTs).

But is all that glitters gold?

In 1995, the Division 12 Task Force of the American Psychological Association (APA) published its first report on psychological interventions, which distinguished ESTs from less structured interventions, clearly favoring the former (Task Force on Promotion and Dissemination of Psychological Procedures, 1995). According to Westen et al. (2004), ESTs share several features: 1) treatments are mostly designed for a single disorder, and patients are selected on the basis of diagnostic homogeneity, thus excluding those with comorbidities in order to reduce variability in treatment response (as a result, such samples differ from the patients clinicians encounter in everyday practice); 2) treatments are manualized and have a fixed, predetermined duration in order to reduce withingroup variability, even though this, too, departs from everyday clinical practice; 3) outcomes are evaluated essentially in terms of symptom reduction; 4) patients' symptoms are assumed to be independent of personality dispositions and processes, although this is not always the case. Westen et al. (2004) also argue that RCTs rest on other, more implicit, assumptions.

The most important of these are the following.

The first assumption is that psychological processes are highly malleable. ESTs usually range from six to about 16 sessions, which implies an assumption of malleability. Indeed, longer treatments increase variability within experimental conditions, which in turn reduces the possibility of identifying causal relationships. However, many studies (e.g., Howard et al., 1986; Hollingbery et al., 2026) have shown a dose-response relationship whereby the longer the therapy, the greater its effectiveness. Shorter therapies, such as those used in ESTs, may therefore underestimate the benefits of longer treatment and may be associated with higher relapse rates, although the dose-response relationship is not linear and patients appear to stop when they reach a good-enough level of improvement (Barkham et al., 2006).

A second typical assumption of ESTs is that each patient has only one clinical problem and that, in the case of multiple clinically relevant disorders, a separate treatment manual can be used for each disorder. Unfortunately, reality is more complex. Psychological disorders very often occur in comorbidity. Furthermore, it is very difficult for a therapist to learn multiple treatment manuals for different diagnoses. A potentially useful solution is offered by transdiagnostic treatment protocols, such as Barlow's unified protocol (Allen et al., 2008; see also Carlucci et al., 2021, for a recent metaanalysis), which allow a variety of diagnoses to be treated with a single manual.

A third assumption is that treatment protocols always represent the best choice for treating patients, despite the problems listed above. In fact, treatment protocols present various problems; to name just one, they lack ecological validity, as they exclude, for example, patients with comorbidities. Furthermore, we cannot assume that treatments that have not been subjected to experimental research do not work; we must honestly admit that we do not know whether they work or not. Obviously, until we know whether they work (and whether they cause harm), we should not use them.

What, then, are the most significant problems of randomized controlled trials (see, for example, Westen et al., 2004)? A first problem concerns what we mean by "recovered": are we referring to patients who have fully recovered, or to those who have merely improved? And after how long do we consider them recovered or improved: if they maintain their gains at 6 months, at 1 year, or beyond? The percentages of patients who have recovered and improved at follow-up are therefore essential. Which changes can we truly consider clinically significant, rather than the result of, for example, statistical artifacts?

A second problem is that exclusion criteria are too rigid compared with clinical reality. For example, does it make sense to keep excluding patients who have attempted suicide from RCTs on depression? In my opinion, it does not. Exclusion criteria should therefore be reduced to the indispensable minimum, limited to those that would also be applied in everyday clinical practice. Furthermore, RCTs should report, at each stage of the research, the number of excluded patients and the reasons for their exclusion, so that readers can judge what has been done. Kirsch et al. (2008) found that the difference between antidepressants and placebo is small and reaches clinical significance only in severe depression. Can we be sure that the same is not true of psychotherapies, which, having psychological effects, should in theory be even more susceptible to placebo effects?

This question would open a long discussion that goes beyond the scope of this paper.

A third problem is that, given the increasingly frequent presence of comorbidities, it may no longer make sense to exclude comorbid patients from RCTs. In addition, an assessment of patients' personality would help researchers interpret their results more fully. For example, can introversionextraversion influence treatment outcomes? Logic suggests that it can, but this remains to be demonstrated.

It is, however, important to mention the critical response of Crits-Christoph et al. (2005) to the arguments put forward by Westen et al. (2004). These authors maintain that ESTs remain indispensable for assessing the clinical validity of a treatment.

What it means for psychotherapy to be scientific

To be considered scientific, a new psychotherapeutic technique (or set of techniques) must meet a series of requirements. First, its efficacy must be demonstrated through randomized controlled trials and meta-analyses. Second, it must be theoretically coherent, both internally and with the body of basic psychological research, particularly learning theory and general psychology, on which CBT was originally founded. In other words, it must demonstrate both its efficacy, through validated protocols, and its connection to a nomological network grounded in basic research.

Empirical validation cannot be divorced from the relevant nomological network, not only in psychotherapy but in any scientific field. Doing so leads to practices that appear to work but fail to contribute to the genuine accumulation of scientific knowledge; ultimately, because they are disconnected from a nomological network, they prevent continuous progress, even from a clinical standpoint.

Therefore, when discussing the scientific method, it is crucial to remember that empirical validation alone is insufficient unless it is embedded within a theoretical framework that supports and legitimizes it. Research findings must either modify or confirm the underlying theory. Consequently, each new empirical study should advance our understanding of the phenomenon in question while strengthening our theoretical knowledge. Relying exclusively on empirical results inevitably leads to problems, because the scientific method comprises not only empirical observation but also theoretical conceptualization and a dynamic interplay between the two. The problems I am referring to are precisely those that CBT encountered when it became disconnected from its theoretical roots and lost interest in its theoretical framework, limiting itself to a single, narrow question: does it work or not?

Every confirmed hypothesis, or series of confirmed hypotheses, should lead to the formulation of a new theory or the confirmation of the initial one; conversely, a disconfirmed hypothesis should lead to the revision, or even the replacement, of the initial theory.

Furthermore, theories themselves often present problems. As Meehl stated in a famous article (Meehl, 1978, p. 806), "most so-called 'theories' in the soft areas of psychology (clinical, counseling, social, personality, community, and school psychology) are scientifically unimpressive and technologically worthless." He immediately adds (Meehl, 1978, pp. 806-807): […] Most of them suffer the fate that General MacArthur ascribed to old generals-They never die, they just slowly fade away.

In the developed sciences, theories tend either to become widely accepted and built into the larger edifice of well-tested human knowledge or else they suffer destruction in the face of recalcitrant facts and are abandoned, perhaps regretfully as a "nice try." But in fields like personology and social psychology [and, I would add, in psychotherapy in general and CBT in particular], this seems not to happen. There is a period of enthusiasm about a new theory, a period of attempted application to several fact domains, a period of disillusionment as the negative data come in, a growing bafflement about inconsistent and unreplicable empirical results, multiple resort to ad hoc excuses, and then finally people just sort of lose interest in the thing and pursue other endeavors. Feynman (1974) levels a further criticism at psychology, arguing that psychological research merely imitates the forms of genuine scientific inquiry without possessing its substance, and that it lacks methodological rigor and intellectual honesty. Nearly 50 years after Meehl's (1978) seminal article and Feynman's (1974) critique, and despite the reforms prompted by the replication crisis discussed below, many of these problems persist in psychology and, by extension, in psychotherapy. Dalal (2018) has offered one of the most significant critiques of CBT in recent years. In my view, however, he has merely scratched the surface of the problem, primarily because his criticisms stem less from a scientific perspective than from a psychoanalytic, political, and social one. He states (Dalal, 2018, p. 141): […] the logical positivists created a version of science that was fundamentalist, and it is exactly here that we find the roots of the hyper-rationality with which our world is infected.

The fundamentalist and hyper-rationalist vision of science has come to be the dominant perspective not only in scientific research, but in all areas of public life from social policy to the fashion industry.

This statement, however, is fundamentally incorrect. The real issue facing CBT today is not the fundamentalist, hyper-rationalist perspective that Dalal (2018) attributes to it, but rather its predominantly empiricist orientation: the accumulation of validated protocols without a shared theory. Such an orientation is necessary but not sufficient for science, for science is not merely empiricism. While the empirical method is crucial to science, theoretical foundations and the relationship between theory and empirical verification are equally important. Ioannidis (2005) has argued that most published research findings are false. In psychology, this concern was confirmed by the Open Science Collaboration (2015), in which 270 researchers attempted to replicate 100 studies published in 2008 in three leading psychology journals. The results were striking: while 97% of the original studies had reported significant findings, only 36% of the replications yielded significant results. Moreover, the effect sizes of the replications were, on average, about half those of the original studies. In psychotherapy research specifically, estimates of efficacy are further inflated by researcher allegiance (Munder et al., 2013) and publication bias (Cuijpers et al., 2010).

At this point, it is necessary to discuss the scientific method with reference to two of the major philosophers of science of the last century: Karl Popper and Thomas Kuhn. According to Popper's critical rationalism (1935 Popper's critical rationalism ( /1959)), a theory remains valid only as long as it withstands attempts at refutation. The scientific method therefore does not aim to establish absolute truths, but rather provisional truths subject to empirical falsification. Theories are never verified; they are only provisionally corroborated, for as long as they survive attempts at falsification.

For Kuhn (1962), by contrast, science consists of paradigms shared by the scientific community that shape its development. Scientific progress is thus not linear; instead, it occurs through scientific revolutions, that is, through paradigm shifts.

In its standard hypothetico-deductive form (see, e.g., Hempel, 1966), the scientific method can be summarized in the following steps: 1.

Theory: start from the reference theory concerning the phenomenon under study.

2.

Observation: identify a phenomenon within the reference theory.

3. Question: ask "how" or "why" about the phenomenon under study.

4. Hypothesis: formulate a provisional, logical explanation derived from the reference theory.

5. Experiment: test the hypothesis under controlled and replicable conditions.

6. Data analysis: verify quantitatively whether the results support or refute the hypothesis.

7. Conclusion: retain the hypothesis (confirming a law or theory) or discard it in favor of a new one.

CBT should adhere to this logical progression of hypothesis testing, yet it does not.

Typically, Steps 1 and 2 are ignored, and Step 7 is only partially considered. Which new psychotherapeutic techniques or CBT approaches fully respect the scientific method? Among recent validations of CBT techniques, hardly any do. For instance, the Mindfulness-Based Cognitive Therapy (MBCT) protocol (Segal et al., 2002) was derived from a cognitive theory of depressive relapse (Teasdale et al., 1995).

That theory, based on the differential activation hypothesis and the Interacting Cognitive Subsystems model, is distinct from, and in some respects incompatible with, Beck's cognitive model; yet the success of MBCT did not lead to any revision of the broader CBT framework. MBCT and Beck's cognitive therapy thus coexist without either theory being tested against the other. More generally, empirical validation that is not framed within a theory can rarely be used to confirm or modify that theory, simply because its sole objective is to answer a question that may be of practical importance but lacks scientific value from the standpoint of modern epistemology. In medicine, too, we sometimes discover drugs that work without knowing why, as happened, to cite just one example, when Fleming discovered penicillin. However, research was subsequently conducted to understand why penicillin works; without this further step, medical progress would have been minimal. It is no coincidence that both mindfulness and the MBCT protocol remain separate techniques, not fully integrated into cognitive behavioral theory. The following example illustrates the importance of theory in clinical practice. If we teach a patient with major depressive disorder to recognize and change their irrational thoughts, following Beck's methodology, while simultaneously using the MBCT protocol, we must realize that we are presenting them with two opposing approaches to their problems: Beck's approach, based on challenging and modifying irrational thoughts, and the mindfulness approach, based on acceptance. How can a patient carry out two tasks that are so diametrically opposed as to be mutually exclusive? Could such a conflict not compromise the effectiveness of the intervention? In my view, it could. Whether combining these two strategies compromises effectiveness is, however, an empirical question that, to my knowledge, has not been directly tested; dismantling and sequencing studies could address it.

Existing proposals and how this one differs

Several proposals have been put forward that align with the direction advocated in this article.

The first is process-based therapy (Hayes & Hofmann, 2017; Hofmann & Hayes, 2019). Its proponents propose moving beyond the current model, based on specific protocols for individual diagnoses, to focus instead on processes of change grounded in an evolutionary theoretical metamodel. Process-based therapy shares several features with the proposal outlined in this article, including the move away from the labels of different schools of psychotherapy. However, my approach differs in several respects. I advocate a psychotherapy grounded in a clearly defined scientific foundation, consisting primarily of general psychology and learning theory. Process-based therapy, by contrast, risks fostering a new form of eclecticism and theoretical confusion by incorporating models of diverse origins. The fundamental difference, however, is that I propose comparing theories directly to test their validity, whereas process-based therapy, while calling for the empirical testing of individual processes of change, is less explicit about the need to test the underlying theories against one another.

The second proposal is the inhibitory learning approach to exposure (Bouton, 2004; Craske et al., 2014). Exposure does not necessarily eliminate the original association between a stimulus and a feared outcome; rather, it fosters the learning of a new, competing association: that the stimulus does not inevitably predict the aversive outcome. Consequently, fear may re-emerge over time, in different contexts, or after new aversive experiences. This approach moves beyond earlier models, which posited that exposure works through habituation and the modification of the fear structure (Foa & Kozak, 1986). Indeed, experimental and neurobiological research has shown that exposure does not erase prior excitatory learning, that is, the fear memory, but rather creates a new safety memory (corresponding to inhibitory learning) that competes with the original memory and inhibits its expression (Bouton, 2004; Craske et al., 2014).

The research conducted by Craske et al. (2014) and Bouton (2004) is firmly grounded in general psychology and learning theory. This shows that it is possible to keep innovating in psychotherapy without abandoning a scientific framework, and that behaviorism is not merely of historical interest. On the contrary, it continues to yield new findings and, as in this case, to drive concrete changes in clinical exposure procedures.

Furthermore, it is crucial to demonstrate not only that treatments are effective but also why they are effective (Kazdin, 2007). This is often a limitation of evidence-based treatments: while we can document their efficacy, we do not precisely understand the mechanisms that produce their results. Goldfried (1980) was among the first to propose identifying common principles that transcend individual schools of thought; however, this approach does not solve the problem unless we can explain how these principles operate and interact.

Rebuilding CBT

What must be done to rebuild CBT? In my view, the steps to be taken are as follows: 1) reconnect CBT with basic psychological research, specifically with general psychology and learning theory; 2) propose a theoretical integration based not merely on a collection of empirically validated clinical techniques or practices, but on a clear explanatory model that accounts for why CBT works and how its various techniques fit together; 3) ensure that the protocols, techniques, and procedures to be validated are grounded in theory: while they may certainly arise from clinical needs, these needs must be integrated into a guiding theoretical framework; 4) take the final step from "CBT psychotherapy" to psychotherapy without adjectives.

The first, and essential, step is to reconnect psychotherapy with basic research in psychology (and probably beyond). The period in which psychotherapy came closest to the ideal of a science was the era of behavior therapy (so-called first-generation CBT), when it was grounded in general psychology research, particularly on learning. Even then, the link with learning theory was contested (Breger & McGaugh, 1965; London, 1972), but the aspiration to derive clinical procedures from basic research was explicit and shared. That initial period saw a productive exchange between theory, research, and clinical practice that strengthened all three. Subsequently, the so-called second-and especially third-generation psychotherapies (I am generalizing here, though the statement holds true on average) began to diverge from this approach. At best, they increasingly focused on validating techniques without integrating them into a guiding theoretical framework. To cite just one example from basic research: we now know that long-term memory is not static but undergoes reconsolidation (Nader et al., 2000). Our brains continuously modify, rewrite, and reconsolidate our memories. On the one hand, this process updates information; on the other, it can create false memories. Both aspects must be taken into account in all areas of psychotherapy, a practice that is already being adopted, at least in part (see Ecker et al., 2012).

Similarly, many individuals with depression or anxiety struggle to recall specific details of past events, a phenomenon known as overgeneral autobiographical memory. Research on this phenomenon has led to the development of Memory Specificity Training (MEST; Raes et al., 2009; Neshat-Doost et al., 2013), a brief cognitive training intervention that restores the ability to retrieve specific memories.

Finally, dismantling studies suggest that behavioral components, when applied on their own, can yield outcomes comparable to, or even better than, those of cognitive therapies. In a study of 150 patients with major depression, Jacobson et al. (1996) found no significant advantage of full cognitive behavioral therapy over behavioral activation (BA) alone, or over BA combined with techniques targeting automatic thoughts. Furthermore, Dimidjian et al. (2006) showed that, among more severely depressed patients, BA was as effective as antidepressant medication and that both outperformed cognitive therapy. With regard to anxiety disorders, Adams et al. (2015) observed large effect sizes for both CBT and behavior therapy, with no additional benefit from adding cognitive techniques.

Similarly, Longmore and Worrell (2007) concluded that the evidence supporting the inclusion of cognitive components is limited. Overall, while further research is needed, these findings support a renewed interest in behavioral foundations and their specific contribution to therapeutic efficacy.

With regard to the second point, theoretical integration is essential to the scientific method.

Without theoretical integration, there is no science, merely an accumulation of knowledge. It would be a mistake to believe that the only thing that matters is whether techniques work, because theoretical conflict causes considerable harm. Therefore, if we must choose between two new, incompatible therapeutic practices, it is far better to integrate the one that fits best with the established theoretical framework. Theoretical coherence is not a luxury in science but a necessity. A single theory containing an internal contradiction loses its predictive power, since anything can be derived from a contradiction (ex falso quodlibet). The CBT family, however, is not a single theory but a collection of rival theories, and the coexistence of rival theories is normal in science; indeed, competition among theories is one of the engines of scientific progress (Kuhn, 1962). The problem with CBT is not that incompatible theories coexist, but that they are rarely tested against one another. Instead of crucial experiments, dismantling studies, and research on mediators that could decide between competing accounts, rival models coexist under a common label, each validated only against control conditions.

In Lakatos's (1978) terms, a research programme is progressive when its theoretical changes predict novel facts that are then corroborated, and degenerating when it merely accommodates results through ad hoc adjustments. A field in which new constructs are added to meet clinical needs, without generating novel and tested predictions, shows the signs of a degenerating programme. Finally, the logical empiricist tradition (Nagel, 1961) holds that scientific explanation requires the explanatory principles of a discipline to form a deductively connected system, a condition that CBT, as a whole, does not currently meet.

Consequently (turning to the third point), the validation of new techniques and therapeutic protocols cannot, and must not, proceed without first analyzing the underlying theoretical framework.

Such validation must follow the steps outlined above and address the problems of randomized controlled trials identified by Westen et al. (2004). This also raises the challenge of developing more effective and efficient research methods, and single-case designs could serve this purpose. Single-case experimental designs (SCEDs) have rightly been criticized because, compared with RCTs, they have lower external validity, a definite drawback. Furthermore, their interpretation traditionally relied on visual inspection, a method notoriously prone to subjectivity. They were also considered to lack sufficient experimental control, owing to the absence of a control group against which to compare treated participants. However, the SCEDs used today differ from those originally developed by early behaviorists, as they have evolved considerably. First, international guidelines have been established 2011) have given these designs a quantitative foundation they previously lacked, although Tau-U has been criticized on psychometric grounds (Tarlow, 2017). Furthermore, SCED data can now be metaanalyzed (Manolov et al., 2023). These methods are well suited to evaluating the efficacy of personalized psychotherapeutic treatments (Scholten et al., 2024).

In sum, RCTs remain superior to SCEDs when the research goal is large-scale statistical generalizability, whereas SCEDs should be used when the primary objectives are ecological validity and treatment individualization.

Paradoxically, the final step in saving CBT lies in the death of CBT. Let me explain. In contrast to other forms of psychotherapy, CBT originated as a psychotherapy with a solid scientific basis and proven efficacy. Today, CBT is increasingly merging with other psychotherapies. For instance, the proponents of process-based therapy (Hayes & Hofmann, 2018) argue that techniques from different forms of psychotherapy can be used, provided that they target theory-based, empirically supported processes of change (see the section on existing proposals). Although their fundamental reference point remains CBT, these authors are effectively already operating beyond its confines.

This is by no means a negative development. Throughout its history, medicine, too, was characterized by different schools that did not always agree with one another. Today, there is essentially only one medicine, characterized by its adherence to the scientific method. It therefore seems clear to me that psychotherapy, too, should shed its qualifying adjectives (such as "analytic" or "cognitive-behavioral"), so that it can simply be called "psychotherapy". However, to reach the position that medicine enjoys today, psychotherapy must also fully adhere to the scientific method. In this scenario, CBT would not so much integrate with other forms of psychotherapy; rather, all psychotherapies would converge by adhering to the principles of the scientific method. This would represent a fundamental historical milestone, not a diminution of CBT.

Objections and replies

Four main objections can be raised against the arguments developed in this paper.

The first is that, since "CBT" is an umbrella term encompassing various evidence-based treatments, its scientific validity must be assessed for each individual treatment; on this view, the label itself matters little. My argument is that the label can lead to the assumption that all the treatments it covers are valid, even though not all of them have been empirically validated. In other words, the label confers borrowed credibility. My critique therefore concerns the sometimes inappropriate use of the label, not the validity of the individual treatments. One may state that "behavioral activation for major depressive disorder is evidence-based", but one cannot simply claim that "CBT is evidence-based".

The second objection is that theoretical pluralism is a standard feature of all sciences. In response, I would argue that genuine science pits competing theories against one another in order to achieve a better theoretical understanding and, ultimately, a unified theory. In the field of CBT, however, different theories coexist independently of one another, because each theory, and the clinical practice derived from it, is validated only against control conditions rather than tested against competing theories.

The third objection is that theory is irrelevant if what truly matters are the common factors shared by different psychotherapies (Wampold & Imel, 2015). The "Dodo bird verdict" (Rosenzweig, 1936), according to which all forms of psychotherapy are equally effective because common components (such as the therapeutic relationship) contribute more to the success of an intervention than specific techniques, stems precisely from a lack of theory-driven research. Even if common factors accounted for a large part of the efficacy of psychotherapy, these factors would still need to be explained through basic research, which is precisely what I am proposing here.

A fourth objection is that the "third wave" is already behavioral (Hayes, 2004). However, this is only partially true, specifically in the case of Hayes's ACT, and does not apply to other third-wave therapies. In this regard, it is worth recalling the "new wave or old hat?" debate (Hofmann & Asmundson, 2008), as well as Öst's (2008) meta-analysis of third-wave therapies, which concluded that, at the time, none of them met the criteria for empirically supported treatments. Although the evidence has since grown for some of them, notably ACT (A-Tjak et al., 2015), this growth has not been accompanied by a corresponding theoretical integration.

Discussion and conclusions

The analysis presented in this article suggests that the current epistemological fragmentation of CBT poses a significant challenge to its scientific identity. Under the CBT label, newer approaches inherit the scientific credibility earned by well-validated, learning-based protocols without having to demonstrate their own theoretical and empirical grounding. In this sense, CBT as a unified framework is no longer a scientific theory, even though many of its individual protocols are well supported. While evidence of efficacy is a necessary condition for a therapy to be considered scientific, it is not sufficient; a therapy must also possess theoretical coherence. Indeed, scientific paradigms are characterized by both empirical validation and theoretical coherence. Although individual protocols may work, a unified scientific framework is lacking. It is therefore essential to rebuild the epistemological foundations of CBT. This fragmentation stems in part from the proliferation of techniques and protocols, all nominally classified as CBT, yet distinct from one another, particularly in their underlying theoretical frameworks. Mindfulness-and acceptance-based protocols are a prime example: they bear little resemblance to first-generation (behavioral) or second-generation (Ellis and Beck) CBT.

The absence of a shared theoretical framework also has clinical implications. Clinical applications lacking epistemological coherence may have clinical costs, a hypothesis that deserves direct empirical testing. Consider, for instance, a patient with major depressive disorder treated simultaneously, or in immediate succession, with behavioral activation and mindfulness-based cognitive therapy. Such a patient might become confused, or worse, given that the former asks them to act against the pull of avoidance, while the latter encourages them to accept and observe their internal experiences. A clinician acting in this way applies techniques without fully grasping their nature or implications. Theoretical and epistemological coherence supports informed clinical decision-making.

Reconnecting CBT with its scientific and epistemological roots helps clinicians select the interventions best suited to the individual patient, while maintaining an overarching perspective that is both scientific and coherent. It also entails changing the practice of clinical supervision, which currently focuses primarily on adherence to protocols and far less, if at all, on a conceptual understanding of the techniques and protocols employed. The fact that very different interventions are subsumed under the broad acronym "CBT" certainly does not help.

In conclusion, CBT has lost the methodological and epistemological coherence with its roots that once distinguished it from other forms of psychotherapy. That coherence was grounded in its strong connection to basic psychological research. It is therefore essential to re-establish this link, so that CBT (which will probably simply be called "psychotherapy" in the future) can continue to grow and thrive.
