Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

Metacheck does not yet analyze automatically whether sentences which include non-significant p-values are correct, but we recommend manually checking the sentences below for possible misinterpreted non-significant p values.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

Background: Teachers and students share the same classroom, but they may perceive teacher behavior differently. Understanding these perceptual differences is important because students' experiences of teaching may be linked to their well-being.

Aims: This study examined teacher-student agreement on teachers' social-emotional and instructional characteristics, tested whether student-rated characteristics were associated with student well-being, and investigated whether teacher-student alignment predicted well-being beyond either perspective alone.

Participants were 1,994 students and 143 secondary school teachers from Belgium.

Methods: Teachers and students completed parallel ratings of 35 classroom-relevant teacher behaviors using a Too Little/Too Much response format, indicating whether behaviors were perceived as insufficient, optimal, or excessive. Analyses included teacher-student agreement correlations, multilevel models testing linear and quadratic associations with student wellbeing, and response surface analyses examining teacher-student alignment.

Results: Teacher-student agreement was modest overall, with an average correlation of r = .18. Teachers generally rated their behavior as closer to the optimal level than students did.

Several student-rated characteristics showed curvilinear associations with well-being, including teaching pace, openness to different opinions, patience, and emotional self-control, indicating that both perceived deficits and excesses were associated with lower well-being.

Teacher-student misalignment predicted lower student well-being beyond teacher and student ratings considered separately for several characteristics, including support for mentally struggling students and instructional pacing.

Conclusions: Students provide a distinct and consequential perspective on teaching. Effective teaching may depend not only on whether desirable behaviors are present, but also on whether they are perceived as appropriately calibrated.

Introduction

Teachers and students share the same classroom yet often experience it through different lenses (Elen & Lowyck, 1998). This matters because key classroom processes -how instruction is structured, how disruptions are handled, how supportive the learning climate feels -are not only defined by what teachers do, but by how students perceive those behaviors. In this study, we focus on teachers' social-emotional and instructional (SEI) characteristics: a broad set of teacher behaviors spanning relational support, emotion regulation, instructional communication, classroom management, and innovative teaching practices (Scheirlinckx et al., 2026). These characteristics are foundational to classroom functioning. According to the prosocial classroom model (Jennings & Greenberg, 2009), teachers' social and emotional competence underlies their capacity to build supportive relationships, manage classroom dynamics effectively, and foster a climate conducive to students' academic and emotional development. SEI characteristics thus represent the behavioral mechanisms through which teachers shape not only how much students learn, but how students experience school.

Why Multiple Perspectives Are Needed

Despite the relevance of teachers' SEI characteristics, many studies rely on a single informant, often the teacher, to report on instructional practices and social-emotional attributes (Lauermann & Hagen 2021). However, evidence consistently suggests that students and teachers often disagree in their perceptions of what happens in the classroom, particularly regarding classroom management and emotional support (den Brok et al., 2006; Scherzinger & Wettstein, 2018). Importantly, these discrepancies are not trivial-external observers often align more closely with students' perceptions than with teachers' self-reports (De Jong & Westerhof, 2001; Scherzinger & Wettstein, 2018), suggesting that student reports capture meaningful variation in classroom processes that may be underestimated when relying solely on teachers' perspectives.

Building on this multi-perspective view, the present study assesses teachers' SEI characteristics using both teacher self-ratings and student ratings of the same teacher behaviors. Methodologically, combining perspectives from both informants reduces sharedmethod bias and strengthens construct validity (Kankaraš & Suarez-Alvarez, 2019).

Substantively, it allows us to test whether student-rated SEI characteristics are predictive of student outcomes and what agreement between teachers and students means for students' well-being.

Why SEI Characteristics Matter for Student Outcomes

Teachers are widely recognized as the most influential in-school factor in shaping students' academic achievement and well-being (Hanushek & Rivkin, 2010; López-Martín et al., 2023). They do far more than deliver course content. They model behavior, regulate classroom climate, and form relationships that significantly affect students' development (Blazar & Kraft, 2017; Emilia et al., 2023). Accordingly, teaching quality is increasingly conceptualized as a combination of a) instructional competencies (e.g., providing structure, clarity, cognitively challenging learning opportunities) and b) social-emotional competencies (e.g., emotion regulation, perspective-taking, classroom management, adaptive and innovative practices; Klusmann et al., 2022). In this paper, we refer to this broader set of teacher and teaching qualities as teacher social-emotional and instructional (SEI) characteristics. This integrated view aligns with the prosocial classroom model by Jennings and Greenberg (2009), which posits that teachers' social and emotional competence is foundational for building supportive teacher-student relationships and effective classroom management. These capacities, in turn, foster a classroom climate conducive to students' academic and emotional functioning. SEI characteristics matter precisely because they shape day-to-day classroom interactions and learning opportunities, with consequences for students' longer-term academic and emotional development (e.g., Zins et al., 2007).

For example, teachers' emotion regulation is associated with more effective management of disruptions and higher student engagement (Rimm-Kaufman & Hamre, 2010), and positive teacher-student relationships are associated with greater school engagement and fewer behavioral problems (Marzano & Marzano, 2003; Pianta et al., 2003; Quin, 2017). Metaanalytic evidence also indicates that classroom management interventions or strategies show small but reliable associations with students' academic, behavioral, and social-emotional outcomes (Korpershoek et al., 2016). Complementing this work, research suggests that teachers influence both students' cognitive skills and their social-emotional development (Kraft, 2019).

Taken together, these findings position SEI characteristics as plausible mechanisms through which teachers shape not only how much students learn, but also how students experience school.

At the same time, evidence is not uniformly strong nor linear across SEI domains and outcomes. Some associations appear to operate through intermediate classroom processes (e.g., classroom climate, management, or support), and effects can vary depending on measurement source, operationalization, and context (e.g., Fauth et al., 2019). This motivates a more finegrained approach that (a) differentiates among SEI characteristics, (b) incorporates multiple perspectives, and (c) allows for nonlinear assessment of teachers' competencies.

Student Evaluations and the Possibility of Non-linear Effects

Student ratings are sometimes criticized as weak proxies for "true" teaching effectiveness or learning outcomes, particularly when effectiveness is defined narrowly in terms of grade-based achievement (Van der Lans & Maulana, 2018). However, students are also closerange observers of teachers' day-to-day behavior, especially instructional, relational, and emotional practices that are most salient during lessons. Research suggests that students are sensitive to teachers' interpersonal behaviors (e.g., empathy, communication), and these perceptions can shape how students experience the classroom (Ryan & Patrick, 2001). In line with this, research on classroom environments indicates that students' perceptions of psychosocial classroom characteristics shape students' well-being. For example, in Belgian secondary education, students reported higher well-being when they perceived their teacher's interpersonal behavior as tolerant instead of authoritarian (Van Petegem et al., 2007 , 2008). In other words, student ratings may not merely reflect bias, but rather a substantively important perspective on classroom processes, especially for SEI characteristics. This is consistent with evidence that students' perceptions of classroom interactions are systematically related to their engagement in school. In a multilevel study of Norwegian students, perceived emotional support of teachers was associated with higher emotional and behavioral engagement of students (Havik & Westergård, 2020). Alternatively, Collie (2021) found that teachers' instructional support was not directly related to students' emotional well-being outcomes but indirectly related via students' perceived competence.

Student reports are especially informative when teaching behaviors have an "optimal zone" because students can indicate not only whether a behavior is present, but whether it feels insufficient, appropriate, or excessive. For some teaching behaviors, both insufficient and overly excessive levels may be associated with poorer outcomes, implying curvilinear (optimality) relationships rather than strictly linear ones (Vergauwe et al., 2017). For instance, caring for students is typically beneficial, but when support becomes overly intense or intrusive (e.g., crossing boundaries, monitoring), it can undermine students' autonomy and relate to less adaptive functioning.

A similar pattern may apply to teacher humor. A study found that moderate use of humor increased student enjoyment and reduced boredom, yet excessive humor unrelated to the course content was perceived as distracting and was linked to lower enjoyment and higher boredom (Bieg et al., 2017). They also found that, when taken too far, aggressive humor and sarcasm (e.g., when the teacher makes fun of the students) were associated with lower enjoyment and heightened anxiety.

Evidence for such nonlinear patterns also exists in the instructional domain: inquirybased teaching (i.e., a student-centered teaching approach that reverses traditional instruction by starting with questions and problems, rather than presenting facts) has been shown to be related to student achievement in a curvilinear way, with moderate levels sometimes being most beneficial (Teig et al., 2018). Capturing these patterns requires measures that can capture deviations in both directions.

Too Much of a Good Thing

Many studies assess teaching characteristics using traditional Likert-type agreement scales (e.g., from "strongly disagree" to "strongly agree"). Although widely used, this format has well-known limitations when the construct of interest is optimality rather than amount. This limitation is especially relevant for SEI characteristics, which may follow a "too much of a good thing" logic: beyond some point, additional increases can become unhelpful or even counterproductive (Pierce & Aguinis, 2013). Likert responses are shaped by a process in which respondents interpret an item relative to their own frame of reference (Ogden & Lo, 2012). As a result, Likert ratings often obscure qualitatively different perceptions. For example, disagreeing with the item "The teacher provides sufficient structure" does not reveal whether students experience too little structure (chaotic, unclear) or too much structure (rigid, controlling). This ambiguity becomes especially problematic for SEI behaviors where both deficits and excesses can have consequences for students.

A second limitation is that Likert scales can be relatively insensitive at the extremes.

When response options run from "strongly disagree" to "strongly agree," it is often unclear what the endpoints actually represent in practice, especially for behaviors that vary in frequency or intensity (Schwartz et al., 1991). For example, two students might both select "strongly agree" to "The teacher is involved in students' personal lives", with one student experiencing the teacher as supportive without crossing boundaries, whereas the other experiences the same teacher as overly involved; the classic Likert-scale does not allow to distinguish "high involvement but still appropriate" from "too much involvement". Conversely, two students could choose different options (e.g., "agree" vs. "strongly agree") while still sharing the same underlying judgment that the teacher shows the right amount of involvement.

To address these issues, we use the Too Little/Too Much (TLTM) response format, introduced in leadership research and recently used in personality research to capture deviations from an ideal level (Dupré et al., 2022; Kaplan & Kaiser, 2006). TLTM frames each behavior on a bidirectional continuum anchored around "the right amount", typically ranging from "much too little", "about right" to "much too much." This design allows for clearer interpretation of responses: a rating communicates not only whether a behavior is present, but whether it is perceived as insufficient, optimal, or excessive.

TLTM is particularly valuable for SEI characteristics because many teaching behaviors likely follow an optimality ("more is not always better") logic: too little enthusiasm can feel disengaged, whereas too much can feel overwhelming. Importantly, evidence for the added value of TLTM comes primarily from leadership and individual-differences research, where "too little/too much" formats have been shown to capture curvilinear associations and improve interpretability compared with unidirectional Likert ratings (Kaplan & Kaiser, 2006). Building on this methodological work, we apply TLTM to teaching-specific SEI behaviors to test whether similar optimality patterns emerge in classroom settings.

Overall, TLTM measurement aligns with the idea that effective teaching involves calibration and balance rather than simple maximization of teaching characteristics.

Importantly, however, the "right amount" is not necessarily the same for every student. Students differ in their needs for structure, autonomy, emotional closeness, and stimulation, meaning that a behavior experienced as supportive by one student may feel intrusive, rigid, or overwhelming to another. This is a further reason to rely on student ratings and to use TLTM specifically. TLTM does not assume one normative "best" level of a behavior; instead, it records whether each student experiences the teacher's behavior as insufficient, appropriate, or excessive.

The Present Study

Most work on teaching effectiveness targets a narrow slice of classroom functioning (e.g., classroom management or emotion regulation) and often relies on a single informant to capture this information (Schulz et al., 2014). Building on calls for broader, multi-perspective assessments, the present study examines teachers' SEI characteristics using (a) a comprehensive taxonomy of classroom-relevant teacher behaviors, (b) parallel teacher self-ratings and student ratings of the same characteristics, and (c) a TLTM response format that captures perceived deviations from an optimal level. This design allows us to move beyond the question of whether SEI characteristics matter, and to address how they are perceived (and whether those perceptions converge) in everyday classroom life.

First, we assess teacher-student agreement across a broad set of teachers' SEI characteristics, quantifying overall alignment and identifying systematic gaps between self-and student perceptions. Second, we examine whether student-rated SEI characteristics relate to student well-being and academic performance, because students' reports may capture classroom-relevant variation not fully reflected in teachers' self-reports. Third, we test whether alignment versus misalignment in SEI ratings predicts well-being and performance beyond teacher and student ratings considered separately, including whether outcomes are strongest when both perspectives converge at optimal levels and whether specific discrepancy patterns (e.g., teachers rating themselves more optimally than students, or vice versa) are linked to poorer outcomes.

Across these aims, we assess a broad set of SEI characteristics spanning bonding and connecting characteristics (e.g., connecting and caring, empathic awareness, trusting students, responsiveness to diversity, growth mindset), instruction and communication (e.g., clarity, humor, pacing), organization (e.g., planning, student engagement management), and innovative practice (e.g., flexibility, creative teaching, reflective practice, appreciating diversity), alongside teachers' emotional self-regulation and stress resilience. By integrating dual perspectives with a comprehensive set of SEI characteristics, this study contributes to a more fine-grained and ecologically grounded understanding of how teacher SEI characteristics relate to student outcomes.

Method Participants

Teachers

A total of 143 secondary education teachers from 41 schools participated, each rating one class with which they had the most weekly contact, resulting in 143 unique teacher-class combinations. Their mean age was 42.15 years (SD = 10.82, range: 23-63). The gender distribution closely reflected the broader Flemish teaching workforce, with 65% identifying as women (Flemish Ministry of Education and Training, 2023). On average, teachers had 17.83 years of teaching experience (SD = 10.36) and taught 19 hours per week (SD = 4).

Students

A total of 2,031 students completed the questionnaire. After excluding cases with missing key variables or indications of poor data quality (e.g., identical responses across all items; Gosling et al., 2004), the final sample consisted of 1,994 students. Of these, 60.4% identified as girls, 38.4% as boys, and 1.2% as non-binary. Students' mean age was 15.41 years (SD = 1.93, range: 11-20), spanning all six years of Flemish secondary education. The broad age range reflects the full scope of the secondary school system and is acknowledged as a source of heterogeneity; student age was included as a covariate in all analyses to partially account for this variation.

Procedure

Teachers were recruited via university student networks and professional contacts across Flanders. Teachers from special education and those teaching the participating class exclusively in physical education were excluded, as the distinct educational context of these settings would affect the interpretability of SEI ratings. All exclusion criteria, research hypotheses, and primary analysis plans were preregistered on AsPredicted before data collection; analyses not specified in the preregistration are treated as exploratory and are identified as such in the results.

Each participating teacher selected one class for inclusion. Students in these classes completed a short questionnaire (approximately 10 minutes) rating their teacher's SEI characteristics and reporting on their own well-being and academic performance, either on paper or online. Teachers completed a separate online survey (approximately 40 minutes) assessing their own SEI characteristics and demographic information. Teacher and student data collection occurred within the same one-week period per class to minimize temporal mismatch between perspectives. Written informed consent was obtained from all teachers and students; parental consent was additionally obtained for students under 18. The study was approved by the institutional ethics committee.

Measures

Teachers' SEI Characteristics SEI characteristics were assessed using a 35-item instrument covering 17 characteristics based on a validated framework of classroom-relevant teacher behaviors spanning relational support, emotion regulation, instructional communication, classroom management, and innovative teaching practices (Scheirlinckx et al., 2025 (Scheirlinckx et al., , 2026)). A full description of the taxonomy and its 17 scales is provided in the supplementary materials.

Teachers and students completed parallel versions of the same items: teachers provided selfratings of their own classroom behavior, and students rated the same behaviors for their teacher.

Both versions used the Too Little/Too Much (TLTM) response format (Kaplan & Kaiser, 2006), in which respondents indicate whether the teacher showed much too little (-2), too little (-1), the right amount (0), too much (+1), or much too much (+2) of each behavior.

For two items assessing speaking and teaching pace, anchors were adapted to "too slow"

versus "too fast." This format is explicitly designed to capture deviations from an optimal level in both directions, making it well-suited for detecting curvilinear associations in which both deficits and excesses may relate to poorer outcomes. Items were phrased positively and descriptively, avoiding evaluative language, as the TLTM scale itself conveys evaluation. Full item wording and adaptation guidelines are provided in the supplementary materials.

Student Well-Being

Student well-being was assessed using the Student Subjective Well-being Questionnaire (SSWQ; Renshaw, 2020), a 16-item scale covering four dimensions: Joy of Learning, School Connectedness, Educational Purpose, and Academic Efficacy. Items are rated on a four-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (almost never) to 4 (almost always).

Following prior applications of this instrument, scores were aggregated into a single composite well-being score. Internal consistencies were good, with McDonald's omega for the total well-being scale being ω = .85, ω = .76 for Joy of Learning; ω =.77 for School Connectedness; ω = .74 for Educational Purpose; and ω = .87 for Academic Efficacy.

Data Analysis

Because students were nested within teachers, all analyses used two-level hierarchical linear models with students at Level 1 and teachers at Level 2. To evaluate whether a third school level was warranted, we estimated unconditional three-level models; likelihood ratio tests indicated that the school level did not significantly improve model fit (p = .15), and all subsequent analyses used two-level models. To evaluate within-class consistency of student ratings and justify the use of aggregated student scores in RQ1 and RQ3, we computed ICC1 and ICC2 values for all 35 SEI items. ICC1 values ranged from .04 to .31 (M = .14), indicating that on average 14% of the variance in student ratings was attributable to the teacher level. ICC2 values ranged from .35 to .86 (M = .66), supporting the reliability of class-mean aggregates for use in subsequent analyses. Full ICC values per item are reported in Table 1.

All multilevel models included the age and gender of teachers and students as covariates. These variables were selected as known confounds in research on teacher-student perceptions and student outcomes (Scherzinger & Wettstein, 2018; Wisniewski et al., 2022) and to partially account for the heterogeneity introduced by the broad student age range.

Analyses were conducted at the individual item level rather than using latent scale scores or summed composites. This decision was theoretically motivated by the TLTM response format: aggregating items into scale scores risks masking opposing deviations that cancel each other out -for instance, a teacher perceived as "too little" on one item and "too much" on another could yield an apparently optimal mean score despite being perceived as suboptimal on both (McNeish & Wolf, 2020). Item-level analysis preserves the directional information that TLTM is specifically designed to capture. To manage the risk of inflated Type I error across 35 simultaneous models, all p-values were adjusted using the Benjamini-Hochberg false discovery rate procedure (Benjamini & Hochberg, 1995).

A small proportion of data was missing across student-reported variables (range: 0.53-6.8%) and teacher self-evaluations (1.15%); missing values were handled via multiple imputation. Full details of the missing data analysis, imputation procedure, and diagnostics are reported in Supplementary Materials S1. Measurement invariance of the TLTM items across teacher and student rater groups was examined using multi-group confirmatory factor analysis with WLSMV estimation; results supported approximate scalar invariance and are fully reported in Supplementary Materials S2.

Results

Descriptive Statistics

Table 1 presents item-level descriptive statistics for both teacher self-ratings and student ratings, including the percentage of respondents endorsing too little, the right amount, and too much for each SEI item, alongside ICC1 and ICC2 values. Overall, responses clustered strongly around the midpoint, with the most frequently selected category being "right amount" for both teachers (M = 65%) and students (M = 63%). Despite this generally positive pattern, several SEI characteristics were more often perceived as insufficient than optimal by both raters. The largest "too little" endorsements appeared for adapting lessons to the level of each student (teachers: 67.4%; students: 50.0%), gauging how students experience the lesson (teachers: 65.9%; students: 49.5%), and experimenting with new teaching methods (teachers: 58.7%; students: 56.2%), suggesting these behaviors are systematically perceived as underused. Endorsements of "too much" were less common overall but emerged for a small set of items, most notably trusting that students are not cheating (teachers: 36.2%) and speaking pace (students: 22.1% too fast). Agreement between teacher selfratings and class-mean student ratings was consistently small across all 35 SEI items (average r = .18; ρ = .17; full results in Supplementary Table S4), indicating that teacher self-ratings and student ratings capture meaningfully different aspects of classroom functioning.

Agreement was strongest for observable instructional behaviors -using personal anecdotes in class (ρ = .41) and following a lesson plan (ρ = .36) -whereas items reflecting internal beliefs or less visible relational processes showed near-zero associations, such as believing that every student can learn (ρ = .00) and being open to different opinions (ρ = .09). This pattern is consistent with the interpersonal perception model (Kenny, 1994), whereby behaviorally salient behaviors yield higher agreement than beliefs or intentions.

Beyond rank-order agreement, teachers generally perceived their own behavior as closer to the optimal level than their students did. The largest gaps appeared for adapting when a lesson goes differently than expected (dz = -.53), starting the lesson optimistically (dz = -.50), and using humor in class (dz = -.48). For a smaller set of items, students rated teachers as more optimal than teachers rated themselves, most notably for wanting to improve oneself as a teacher (dz = .39) and addressing diversity issues (dz = .38). Together, these findings suggest that teacher self-reports and student perceptions are complementary rather than interchangeable sources of information.

SEI Characteristics Predicting Student Well-Being

Multilevel models were estimated for each SEI item predicting student well-being, including both linear and quadratic terms to test for curvilinear associations consistent with the TLTM optimality logic. Items were grand mean-centered before computing the squared term. Across items, the pattern of results more consistently supported curvilinear rather than purely linear relations. Teacher-Student (Mis)Alignment and Student Well-Being

To test whether teacher-student (mis)alignment predicts student well-being beyond either perspective alone, we compared baseline polynomial models including teacher ratings, class-mean student ratings, and their squared terms against alignment models additionally including the teacher × student mean interaction term. Significant interaction effects were interpreted using response surface analysis (RSA) parameters for the line of congruence (LOC; a1: slope, a2: curvature) and line of incongruence (LOIC; a3: directional asymmetry, a4: mismatch penalty).

Adding the interaction term significantly improved model fit for seven SEI items: supporting students who are mentally struggling, being open to different opinions, actively engaging students, distancing oneself from stressful situations, having patience, speaking pace, and teaching pace (full RSA parameters reported in Supplementary Materials Table S5).

Across all seven significant effects, the curvature of the line of incongruence (a4) was consistently negative (range: -.19 to -.69), indicating a mismatch penalty: student well-being was highest when teacher and student ratings were similar and decreased as discrepancies grew in either direction. The slope of the line of incongruence (a3) was consistently small (range: -.11 to .01), suggesting little directional asymmetry -the well-being cost of disagreement did not differ substantially depending on whether teachers rated themselves more optimally than students or vice versa. In other words, the magnitude of the mismatch mattered more than its direction. Discussion

Do teachers and students experience the classroom in the same way? Does student well-being improve when students rate their teacher more favorably? And does alignment between teacher and student perspectives matter beyond either perspective alone? The present study examined these questions by assessing teachers' social-emotional and instructional (SEI) characteristics from dual perspectives and linking these perceptions to student wellbeing. Three main findings emerged. First, teacher-student agreement on SEI characteristics was consistently small, indicating that teachers and students often perceive classroom behavior differently. Second, several student-rated SEI characteristics were associated with student well-being in curvilinear ways, with both too little and too much related to lower wellbeing. Third, for a subset of SEI characteristics, teacher-student misalignment was associated with poorer well-being beyond either perspective alone.

Limited Teacher-Student Convergence

Rank-order agreement between teacher self-ratings and class-mean student ratings averaged ρ = .17, indicating weak overall convergence. This aligns with prior research documenting discrepancies between teacher and student perceptions across instructional behavior, emotional support, and classroom management (den Brok et al., 2006; Scherzinger & Wettstein, 2018). Critically, these discrepancies should not be dismissed as measurement error: external observers tend to align more closely with student perceptions than with teacher self-reports (De Jong & Westerhof, 2001; Scherzinger & Wettstein, 2018), suggesting that student ratings capture meaningful classroom variation that teachers may overlook.

The pattern of agreement in our data is consistent with the interpersonal perception model (Kenny, 1994): agreement was strongest for behaviorally salient and observable items such as using personal anecdotes and following a lesson plan, whereas internal beliefs and subtle relational processes showed near-zero correlations. Interestingly, agreement was also low for teaching pace and making learning materials creative and attractive, despite these being relatively observable behaviors. This suggests that observability alone does not guarantee convergence when a behavior is inherently subjective -teaching pace depends partly on each perceiver's processing capacity and prior knowledge (Reilly, 1998), and judgments of creativity likely reflect different aesthetic standards between teachers and students.

Beyond rank-order agreement, teachers generally perceived their own behavior as closer to optimal than their students did, consistent with findings on self-serving bias in instructional quality ratings (Wisniewski et al., 2022). This pattern mirrors evidence from research on teacher-student relationships, where teachers tend to report more positive perceptions of relational quality than students do (Wagner et al., 2016; Zee & Koomen, 2017).

For a smaller set of items -wanting to improve as a teacher, addressing diversity issuesteachers evaluated themselves as less optimal than students did, possibly reflecting stricter self-evaluation benchmarks for reflective and diversity-oriented practice. Together, these findings reinforce the argument that teacher self-reports and student ratings are complementary rather than interchangeable sources of information (Kankaraš & Suarez-Alvarez, 2019; Lauermann & Hagen, 2021).

SEI Characteristics and Student Well-Being

Several SEI items showed inverted-U associations with student well-being, providing empirical support for the optimality logic embedded in the TLTM format and the "too much of a good thing" principle (Pierce & Aguinis, 2013; Vergauwe et al., 2017). These curvilinear patterns emerged across relational and social-emotional domains (trusting students, patience, emotional self-control), instructional pacing, growth mindset beliefs, and teachers' selfimprovement orientation. Items related to connecting with students, empathic awareness, humor, behavioral management, and epistemic curiosity showed no significant associations with well-being, suggesting that optimality effects are domain-specific rather than universal.

Notably, teaching and speaking pace showed both curvilinear and negative linear components, indicating directional asymmetry: being perceived as too fast was more detrimental than being perceived as too slow. This is consistent with cognitive load theory (Sweller, 2011) and evidence that accelerated speech degrades comprehension once input rates exceed processing limits (Peelle & Davis, 2012). More broadly, these findings challenge the implicit "more is better" assumption in much educational research and suggest that effective teaching involves calibration rather than maximization.

Alignment Effects: Mismatch Is Associated with Lower Well-Being

Adding the teacher × student interaction term significantly improved model fit for seven SEI characteristics, indicating that perceptual alignment carries incremental associative value for student well-being beyond either perspective alone. Across all seven effects, the curvature of the line of incongruence (a4) was consistently negative, consistent with a mismatch penalty: well-being was highest when teacher and student ratings converged and lower as discrepancies grew, regardless of direction.

One interpretation is that perceptual discrepancies undermine students' sense of being understood and effectively taught. When teachers believe they are providing adequate support for struggling students, while students do not share this perception, students may feel invisible or misunderstood -with potential consequences for trust and engagement (Pianta et al., 2003; Quin, 2017). Discrepancies in perceived pacing similarly suggest failures of instructional calibration. This interpretation is consistent with research on teacher-student relationship quality showing that incongruent perceptions are associated with less positive student outcomes (Liu et al., 2023), and with evidence from organizational research that perceptual congruence between leaders and employees constitutes a resource in itself -when both parties share the same reading of a situation, interactions feel more predictable and expectations clearer (Černe et al., 2013; Gibson et al., 2009). Although the power dynamics in classrooms differ from those in organisations, the underlying mechanism -that shared understanding reduces ambiguity and supports functioning -appears to translate across contexts.

Strengths and Limitations

A key strength of this study is the comprehensive assessment of teachers' SEI characteristics, spanning bonding with students, emotion regulation, behavioral management, instructional competencies, and innovative characteristics (e.g., creative teaching, openness to diversity). This broad coverage is based on the taxonomy of Scheirlinckx and colleagues (2026) and enables a more fine-grained test of which SEI characteristic is most strongly linked to students' well-being. By looking at a multitude of teacher characteristics, we reduce the risk that conclusions hinge on a narrow operationalization of "effective" teaching behaviors.

A second strength is the use of the TLTM response format-and the broader "calibration" perspective it embodies-which treats SEI characteristics not as simple "more is better" quantities but as behaviors that can be underused, well-calibrated, or overused depending on the classroom context and the student's needs. This approach likely mitigates several drawbacks of traditional Likert-type ratings, including acquiescent responding and response-style artifacts (e.g., systematic avoidance of endpoints or midpoint preferences) that can distort between-person comparisons and attenuate associations with outcomes (Van Vaerenbergh & Thomas, 2013). At the same time, complementing TLTM ratings with nonsurvey methods (e.g., systematic observations, experience sampling, or open-ended student feedback) would provide richer insight into the behavioral manifestations of SEI characteristics and could help explain why some SEI domains show robust well-being associations whereas others do not. Several limitations warrant consideration. First, the cross-sectional design precludes causal inference. Although SEI characteristics are modeled as predictors, it is equally plausible that students' well-being shapes their perceptions of teacher behavior, or that unmeasured variables such as school climate influence both. The association between misalignment and well-being is particularly susceptible to reverse causality: students with lower well-being may perceive their teachers' behavior more negatively, artificially inflating apparent discrepancies. Longitudinal and intervention designs are needed to establish temporal precedence.

Second, the assumption that the TLTM scale midpoint represents the true optimum for all behaviors remains untested. For some characteristics, the empirically observed optimum may deviate from the midpoint, and anchoring optimal levels against external criteria -such as observer ratings or student outcome benchmarks -would strengthen interpretability.

Third, the significant effects represent a minority of tested items, and effect sizes are modest. While this is partly expected given the item-level analytical approach and the conservative multiple testing correction, readers should interpret the findings with this base rate in mind. Statistically significant associations do not automatically imply practical significance, and effect sizes should be considered alongside theoretical plausibility.

Finally, despite efforts to protect anonymity (e.g., omitting personal identifiers on the paper surveys), students' responses on well-being and evaluations of their teacher may have been affected by social survey completion in the classroom, which may have introduced social desirability bias, particularly for adolescent students for whom belonging concerns are salient (King, 2024). Future work could strengthen anonymity procedures or collect data outside class time.

Implications for Research and Practice

These findings carry several implications for educational research and practice. First, they reinforce calls for multi-informant assessment of teaching quality (Fauth et al., 2019; Lauermann & Hagen, 2021). Teacher self-reports alone appear insufficient for capturing classroom-relevant SEI variation, and student perspectives offer a complementary lens on relational and emotional dynamics that students are uniquely positioned to observe.

Practically, this supports embedding structured student feedback cycles -short surveys, reflection, and goal-setting -into school-level professional development, building on evidence that student feedback can be implemented feasibly and contribute to improvements in practice (Finefter-Rosenbluh, 2021).

Second, the prevalence of curvilinear associations challenges the maximisation logic implicit in much educational intervention research. Effective teaching appears to involve calibration and balance rather than simply doing more of what works. Intervention designs should target optimal ranges of key behaviors rather than promoting uniformly higher levels, and help teachers recognise when initially beneficial practices begin to cross into excess (Pierce & Aguinis, 2013).

Third, the mismatch penalty effect suggests that reducing perceptual gaps between teachers and students may be a low-burden avenue for supporting well-being. Structured feedback in which teachers reflect on student perceptions -particularly in the domains where alignment was most consequential, including support for struggling students, patience, stress regulation, and pacing -could help teachers calibrate their practice to how students actually experience the classroom. This is consistent with the prosocial classroom model (Jennings & Greenberg, 2009), which positions teachers' social-emotional awareness as foundational to building the shared understanding that supports effective classroom relationships.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that teachers and students frequently perceive classroom dynamics differently, that several SEI characteristics are associated with student well-being in curvilinear ways, and that perceptual alignment between teachers and students carries incremental associative value for well-being outcomes. These findings highlight the value of combining multiple informant perspectives and allowing for nonlinear effects when studying teaching quality. Rather than assuming more is always better, attending to whether teacher behavior is perceived as appropriately calibrated -from the student's perspective -may better support the relational and instructional conditions associated with student well-being.
