How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard), also Internal Review Board - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term); not IEC followed by a number, which is a technical standard - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee/Commission, in the singular or plural - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee, Bioethics Committee/Commission, Ethikkommission - Ethical Review Authority/Unit/Office/Agency - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Human Research Protection Office/Program, Office for Human Subjects Protection - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee(s) on/for (Medical and Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt - Ethics review that was not needed: Ethics review was not required, did not require further ethical review, exempt from formal human research ethics review, no new institutional ethics review

A template line the authors have not filled in (“[Add ethics approval … here.]”) is not a statement.

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

When a statement is expected

The module sorts each paper by where its data came from (the internal .data_source(); the signs and how the rule was tested are described in R/data-source.R): - own: the authors collected data from people or animals, for this paper, in an earlier study of their own, or (a protocol) will collect them; - existing_records: records about individual people that the authors obtained for this study, such as hospital records or a cohort; - existing_unclear: existing data about people from a source the module could not identify; - open_resource: an open resource any researcher can use, such as UK Biobank or NHANES; - none: no data about people or animals (a review, simulation, commentary, theory, tutorial, coding of published papers, or figures about groups); - uncertain: the signs were missing or pointed both ways; - no_text: the paper has no text.

A statement is expected for own, existing_records and existing_unclear. A paper without a statement in these classes, or in uncertain, is flagged. The module reads the text, and the results of power, stat_p_exact, stat_check, repo_check, data_check and prereg_check when they ran before it (run it after them); without stat_p_exact, it finds the p-values in the text.

On 199 PsyArXiv and medRxiv preprints labelled after the rule was fixed, the module decided right whether a statement is needed for 86.9% (the earlier live-data search: 83.4%). Its main errors: other people’s studies described in the words of a new study, records and specimens described only as counts, and open resources the paper does not call open.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 685 tests with 34 instances of inconsistent reporting, Statcheck correctly detected 34 of them, and incorrectly identified 26. Therefore 0% of true instances were missed, and 43% of detections were false positives. See https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/ for more details of the validation.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on 226 Psychological Science papers with 5,019 hand-coded p-values, 438 of them imprecise (scienceverse/validation, branch stat_p_exact). Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). Of the misses, 13 are starred table notes (“*p < .05”) that the module leaves out on purpose and 11 are p-values it did not find; of the wrong reports, 20 are p-values the ground truth does not contain (such as “ps < .05”) and 6 use ≤ or ≥. These figures measure agreement with the hand-coding, which has known errors in both directions (scienceverse/validation#48).

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Of 1,783 non-significant p-values in the ground truth, version 0.4.4.3 found 1,719 (3.6% missed), and 94.8% of its 1,814 detections were in the ground truth. How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Introduction

One of the largest bodies of data on how individual people respond to color under ambiguity sits outside vision science. It was gathered by clinical psychologists and psychiatrists. Since 1921, examiners have shown the same ten inkblots to very large numbers of people and coded, response by response, whether each person used color and how.

The motive was a theory of emotion. Hermann Rorschach believed that chromatic color stirs feeling, and that a person who is carried away by the color of a blot is a person who is carried away by affect. That claim shaped clinical practice for decades. It also produced a long experimental argument about whether it was true.

Vision scientists have understandable reasons for having overlooked all this. Projective tests acquired a poor scientific reputation in the late twentieth century. The stimuli are uncalibrated.

The key terms are clinical. The literature is scattered across journals that color scientists do not read.

As a blanket verdict, that reputation is outdated. Modern Rorschach research uses standardized administration and coding with documented interrater reliability (Viglione et al., 2012; Viglione & Taylor, 2003) and international reference norms (Viglione & Giromini, 2016), and the validity of the test is now judged score by score through meta-analysis (Mihura et al., 2013; Viglione, 1999; Viglione & Hilsenroth, 2001; Viglione et al., 2022). The test's most persistent critics have accepted the principal meta-analysis as an unbiased summary of the published literature. They have withdrawn their call for a moratorium as far as the cognitive scores are concerned, while still disputing the scores meant to measure emotion and personality (Wood et al., 2015).

Researchers in the field now describe the Rorschach as a performance-based task and no longer as a projective test (Meyer & Kurtz, 2006; Meyer et al., 2014). I keep the older word in this paper because it is the one most readers outside the field will recognize.

Yet the two fields have been asking neighboring questions. Modern color-emotion research, led by groups such as those of Domicele Jonauskaite and Christine Mohr in Lausanne, Karen Schloss in Madison, and Daniel Oberfeld in Mainz, asks which colors go with which emotions, why, and whether colors change how people feel. A parallel line of work in social and personality psychology, led by Andrew Elliot and Markus Maier, asks how a color such as red affects motivation and behavior in particular contexts (Elliot & Maier, 2014). The projective tradition asked something different: what does a person's manner of using color say about that person? The first question is about shared meaning. The second is about individual differences.

The separation is explicit. Jonauskaite and Mohr's (2025) systematic review of 128 years of research on color and emotion set as an eligibility criterion that it would "exclude clinical samples" and studies "using clinical tests (e.g., Rorschach test, Lüscher test)." That was a reasonable choice for a review of healthy populations. It also means that the main modern synthesis of color-emotion research does not touch the projective literature. Traffic in the other direction has only just begun. De Villemor-Amaral and Yazigi (2022) traced the history of the color-affect idea in psychological assessment, from Rorschach through the Lüscher and Color Pyramid tests, and ended by citing the cross-cultural association studies of Jonauskaite and colleagues. Their essay accepts the link between color and affect and does not take up the experiments that tested it or the validity evidence. Piotrowski (2023) applied modern research on the color red to the red areas of Cards II and III, and Di Girolamo et al. (2026) applied the concept of color diagnosticity to Rorschach coding. The separation has been noticed before. In a doctoral dissertation, Hartz (2019) observed that "the Rorschach is almost never mentioned in the color psychology literature" (p. 40) and suggested that Rorschach research could lead color psychologists to study individual differences.

One experiment is an exception to the separation. Kron et al. (2009), cognitive psychologists writing in a personality assessment journal, showed that the balance of color against form on the Rorschach predicts how much color affects the naming of ordinary objects. Vision science did not take it up. The two literatures remain largely disconnected. I found no review that sets the experimental and validity evidence beside modern color science, or that addresses vision scientists. Figure 1 sets the two histories on one timeline.

Projective Testing in Plain Language

The Stimuli

There are ten cards, the same ten since 1921 (Table 1). They differ in color in a way that matters for everything that follows. The plates themselves are not reproduced here, out of respect for test security.

The ink is mottled, so every card also varies in lightness and texture. The colored cards differ from the gray ones in shape, complexity, and order as well as in color. No card was ever specified colorimetrically. Any comparison of "chromatic" with "achromatic" cards is therefore confounded from the start.

How Color Is Scored

Each response is coded for whether color helped produce it, and whether color or form led (Table 2).

Scoring Systems, Old and New

Readers who open this literature will meet several scoring systems, and results from one do not always transfer to another. Three periods matter (Table 3).

Rorschach died in 1922, a year after publishing the test, and left no finished system. In the United States five separate systems grew up between the 1930s and the 1950s, associated with Beck (1944), Klopfer and Kelley (1942), Hertz, Piotrowski (1957), and Rapaport et al. (1945 Rapaport et al. ( -1946)). All five kept Rorschach's three-way coding of form and color but differed in administration, added categories, and interpretation (Exner, 1969). Most of the experiments on color shock described below were run under these systems.

Exner's Comprehensive System (Exner, 1974 (Exner, , 2003) ) combined what he judged the bestsupported elements of the five into one method with fixed administration, coding rules, and norms. It dominated practice and research from the 1970s into the 2000s, and its color scores are the ones most validity studies have used. It drew sustained criticism over its norms and the evidence for some scores, remains in use, and was revised in 2022 (Exner et al., 2022).

The Rorschach Performance Assessment System (R-PAS; Meyer et al., 2011), developed by Meyer, Viglione, Mihura, Erard, and Erdberg, keeps the same cards and much of the same coding. It retains variables according to the meta-analytic evidence, controls the number of responses a person gives (Dean et al., 2007), and uses international reference norms. The two systems are now used side by side, and the older one can be used with international norms (Viglione & Giromini, 2016). The Color-Affect Hypothesis and Why It Was Believed Rorschach's claim was that the way a person handles color in a blot mirrors the way that person handles emotion. He held that chromatic color evokes emotional excitability and that color responses reflect a person's overall affective responsiveness (Rorschach, 1921 (Rorschach, /1942)). His evidence was clinical observation of patients, and he knew its limits. The link, he wrote, "has been found empirically," but "the basis for this deduction is, however, quite insufficient to satisfy the demands of scientific logic" (Rorschach, as quoted in de Villemor-Amaral & Yazigi, 2022, p. 71). He did try one experiment. He showed people pictures of animals in which shape and color disagreed, such as a cat colored like a frog, and noted that some people named the animal by its color and others by its shape (de Villemor-Amaral & Yazigi, 2022). That is the design that Siipola in 1950 and Kron and colleagues in 2009 would later use. Note what the claim is not. It says nothing about which color means which emotion. Red and blue are treated alike. The claim concerns a process: whether a salient feature is absorbed into a structured percept or allowed to take it over.

Color Shock

Rorschach also described "color shock." Some people falter when the first colored card appears.

They take longer, give poorer answers, or refuse the card. He read this as a sign of neurotic suppression of feeling. This became the tradition's most testable prediction.

The Perceptual Passivity Argument

The most interesting defense of the hypothesis came from Schachtel (1943) and Shapiro (1956 Shapiro ( , 1960)), and it is a claim about perception. They argued that color and form are taken in differently. Color arrives at once and without effort. It is something that happens to the observer.

Form must be actively traced, organized, and held.

Emotion, they said, shares the structure of color. An affect is also undergone, not constructed. A person whose perception is easily captured by color is therefore a person whose behavior is easily captured by feeling. A pure color response is perceptual impulsivity. A form-led color response is color brought under control.

A vision scientist will hear something familiar here. Color is a feature that is available early and can be registered with little attention. Shape recognition takes organization. The passivity argument is, in effect, an early statement that features differ in the effort needed to use them, joined to a hypothesis about individual differences in that effort. It was never tested with psychophysical methods.

Five Rival Explanations

In a congress lecture, Malmgren (1999) set out five ways the colored cards might disturb a response. They are worth keeping apart because modern color science can separate them. I give each with the classic source.

1. Color directly evokes an affective response (Rorschach, 1921 (Rorschach, /1942)).

2. Color perception and affect share a passive structure (Schachtel, 1943; Shapiro, 1956).

3. Colored regions call up emotionally loaded content, such as red as blood (Wallen, 1948).

4. Color conflicts with form, as in a blue region shaped like a spider (Siipola, 1950; Wolpin & Hamlin, 1959).

5. Color plus form is an informational overload, that is, simply a harder task.

What the Experiments Showed

Color shock was never convincingly shown to be caused by color. The most direct design was simple and will appeal to any experimentalist: reproduce the colored cards in gray and see whether the disturbance goes away. Lazarus (1949) showed 100 high-school seniors the standard cards and an achromatic version six weeks apart, in counterbalanced order, using group administration with slides. He concluded that color did not affect the scoring categories of the test or the frequency of the standard signs of color shock. The clearest change was a drop in poor-form answers when color was removed, which he attributed to the direct use of color in responses and not to disorganization. Later studies refined the picture (Table 4). (Holtzman et al., 1954), and a blue printing of Card I drew the most responses among four printings (Exner, 1959). These effects were specific to particular cards and hues. Baughman (1958) found no general effect of color on latency or on the summary scores.

Set against the five explanations, the pattern in these small and varied studies favors content associations (explanation 3) and color-form conflict (explanation 4). It gives little support to direct emotional arousal by color (explanation 1). The passivity account (explanation 2) was left largely untested.

A fair summary must also record the defense. Malmgren (1999) argued that the experiments were weak tests. Lazarus's group format could not record reaction times or spoken comments about the colors. His participants were normal adolescents, in whom little color shock would be expected. The test-retest design introduced practice effects. Later studies were small and examined one sign at a time. On this view the hypothesis is unproven, not disproven.

Experimental work on it has been sparse for decades, so the matter was abandoned more than settled.

For vision science, the lasting result is Siipola's. The strongest disturbance came from a mismatch between color and form. In modern terms it anticipates questions about color-form congruency and object-color knowledge. Tanaka and Presnell (1999) later showed that color speeds recognition mainly for objects that have a typical color, such as a banana, and that the wrong color slows it. Knowledge of an object's typical color can even change how its color looks. A banana shown in gray appears faintly yellow, and observers must shift it toward blue before it looks gray (Hansen et al., 2006; Witzel et al., 2011).

Second, the purest test of "color equals raw emotion" failed. Pure C, color with no form, showed no support in the available data. Neither did the experience type that Rorschach himself considered central.

Two cautions apply. Mihura and Meyer are co-developers of R-PAS and state that they share copyright and ownership in the manual, the scoring program, and the company that supports them (Mihura et al., 2013). And the color dominance score is not coded very reliably: in one study of 112 protocols its interrater reliability was only fair (intraclass correlation = .57), against .83 for the weighted color sum and .88 for achromatic color (Pignolo et al., 2017).

Deciding whether color or form led a response leaves room for judgment. Reliability can be much higher under research conditions: with two trained judges scoring every protocol, Kron et al. (2009) reported intraclass correlations of .93 for form-led and .91 for color-led responses. A recent study of color diagnosticity in R-PAS coding found that some objects are strongly tied to particular colors and others only ambiguously, and called for more standardized guidance (Di Girolamo et al., 2026). That is a question vision scientists know how to study.

Resources, Stress, and Overload

The color scores were never read alone. Rorschach systems combine them with other scores into indices of how much a person has to work with and how much that person is carrying. Three kinds of response enter these indices. A human movement response sees a person doing something ("two people lifting a basket"). An inanimate movement response sees an object in motion ("a spinning top"). A shading response uses the light and dark gradations of the ink ("it looks shadowy"). The first, added to the weighted color sum, gives a score for psychological resources. The second and third are read as signs of stress and distress. The difference between resources and demands, called the D Score, was meant to show whether a person is coping or overloaded.

Several of these indices did as well as the color scores or better in the same meta-analyses (Table 5). The typologies and single signs, such as the experience type and the use of white space as a sign of anger, did not. For this paper the lesson is that pure color read alone did poorly, and color did better when it was weighted by form or set in a balance: against form in the Form-Color Ratio, and against demands in the D Score.

The Critics Wood et al. (2015) accepted four groups of scores, which they called the cognitive quartet: productivity, complexity and synthesis, form quality, and deviant verbalization. They placed human movement, the resources score, and the blends ratio in this cognitive group, as measures of complexity and not of emotion.

They challenged the rest. They reanalyzed the four non-cognitive scores with the highest validity, adding unpublished dissertations. Two of those scores appear in Table 5. For the weighted color sum they obtained a validity of .41 across seven studies and 569 participants, higher than the original estimate. They still concluded that the score "probably does not measure its intended target." Their reasons were specific. Patients with borderline personality disorder, a condition marked by emotional dysregulation, scored close to the nonpatient average. And in one dissertation the score was unrelated to ratings of the person's emotionality by friends and family (r = -.08) or by the person (r = -.02). They called its psychological meaning "presently a mystery" (p. 241). For the shading total the result was worse. With ten further studies added, most of them dissertations, its validity fell from .37 to .09. Wood et al. did not reanalyze the Form-Color Ratio or the D Score. Their overall conclusion was that the evidence does not yet justify using the system to measure emotionality or negative affect. Mihura et al. (2015) replied that the reanalysis contained methodological errors, data errors, and omitted studies, and that its negative conclusions did not follow from its own numbers. Wood et al. nonetheless called the original estimates "an unbiased and trustworthy summary of the published literature" (p. 243). The clearest common ground between the two sides is the stronger evidence for the test's perceptual and cognitive scores, and its developers now present it as a performance task (Meyer et al., 2014). The color scores that survive are the ones that can be read that way.

How the Newer System Reframed Color

R-PAS kept the surviving color variables and changed how they are described. The share of responses to the colored cards, formerly the Affective Ratio, is now presented as a general responsiveness to compelling stimuli, and the color dominance score as a measure of cognitive control and modulation in reaction to the environment (Andò et al., 2019). The outward and inward experience types gave way to a simple proportion. The move is away from emotion and toward stimulus salience and control, which are concepts vision science shares.

The older reading has not been abandoned. Malone et al. (2013) studied 88 patients referred for psychological testing at a hospital clinic, scored with the Comprehensive System. They noted that the link between color and affect had "a long history of theoretical support but minimal empirical evidence" (p. 71). Color-led responses (CF) went with self-reported anxiety, depression, suicidal ideation (r = .35), and neuroticism (r = .35). Form-led color responses (FC) were unrelated to self-reported symptoms and to IQ, but went with better scores on tests of attention and memory (r = .28 to .36).

Patients reporting more suicidal ideation and more psychotic experience were more likely to show a balance tipped toward color-led responses. The authors called this initial support for the affect-modulation reading. The study has limits. The sample was small, the color scores were not coded by a second rater, and the correlations were exploratory, with only the significant ones reported. Two of its results stand out here. The relationships were with self-report, where the meta-analysis found almost none. And the formled response went with attention and memory, which fits a cognitive reading as well as an emotional one.

Cognitive Tempo

One study links color dominance to a standard cognitive variable. Katz and Ziffo (1975) classified people as impulsive or reflective on the Matching Familiar Figures Test and showed them the chromatic cards.

Impulsive responders gave more color-dominated (CF and C) responses. This fits one measurable version of the passivity argument: a fast, less analytic style of perceptual decision goes with letting color lead.

A Direct Test with an Object-Recognition Task

One study has tested the perceptual reading directly, and it deserves to be better known. Kron et al. (2009) asked whether the color scores reflect how much a person's visual system uses color when recognizing ordinary objects. They photographed ten fruits and vegetables and showed each in its typical color, in gray, and in the typical color of another item, such as a red banana. In a first experiment with 15 undergraduates, naming was fastest for typical colors and slowest for wrong colors. The difference between the two, the congruency effect, measures how much color enters into recognizing the object.

In the second experiment, 59 ninth-grade students took the Rorschach, scored with the Comprehensive System, and then the naming task. Students whose color responses were mostly color-led (n = 24) showed a congruency effect of 236 ms, against 114 ms for those whose color responses were mostly form-led (n = 17). Scored as a continuous balance of form-led against color-led responses, the Rorschach measure correlated with the congruency effect at r = -.25.

The weighted color sum and the Affective Ratio did not (r = .08 and .10). Only the balance of color against form predicted how much color affected object naming. The amount of color in the record did not.

The authors raised the conclusion this paper argues for. Their results, they wrote, "might imply that the response process of the [Rorschach] is another form of an object-recognition task," in which the color scores "are the relative contribution of colors and form to this recognition" (p. 141). They also named the alternative. A wrongly colored object sets up a conflict, and people with color-led records may simply be worse at resolving conflict, as in the Stroop task.

That would make the score a measure of cognitive control, which is close to how R-PAS now describes it. The study could not separate the two readings.

The study has limits. It is a single experiment with adolescents, the effect is modest, and the colors were not calibrated. I found no replication among the papers that cite it. A group in Japan followed up the conflict reading with Stroop-type tasks and brain potentials, in work published mainly in Japanese. A reanalysis of that work found only limited associations between chromatic color responses and performance on a Stroop-type task of attentional control (Matsuda et al., 2025), which does not favor the conflict reading. In a small sample of community adults, a balance tipped toward color-led responses went with greater thickness of the middle frontal cortex, which the authors took as support for the score as a measure of control over emotional responses (Steinert et al., 2021). Neither result settles the matter. But Kron et al. showed that the question can be answered in the laboratory, and that the answer so far favors a perceptual or cognitive account of the color-form balance.

Parts and Wholes

Color is not the only feature the Rorschach asks a person to integrate. Every response is also coded for location: whether the person used the whole blot (W), a common detail (D), or an unusual detail (Dd). Further scores record how well the parts were organized into a whole, such as synthesized responses (DQ+) and the frequency of organizational activity (Zf). A vision scientist will recognize the question of global and local processing, familiar from the hierarchical-figure work that began with Navon (1977).

Factor analysts have looked at these scores for decades (Meyer, 1992, reviews the history). In a clinical sample of 152 adolescents, Wood et al. (2003) found three clearly defined factors in

Comprehensive System scores: one of synthesized complexity, defined partly by the proportion of form-only responses, one of productivity, and one of form quality. R-PAS makes a related composite, Complexity, its first interpretive step. Viglione (1999) introduced the term for the productivity, precision, differentiation, and integration shown across a person's responses. The score combines features of location and object quality, content, and determinants. Responses of higher Complexity were accompanied by greater activity in the dorsal attention network (Vitolo et al., 2021).

Three points follow for the present argument. First, the structure that factor analysis recovers from the test is a structure of perceptual and cognitive integration. That fits the view that the Rorschach is best read as a perceptual task. Second, color use is not independent of that structure. Determinants, color among them, enter the Complexity score by construction, and form-only responding helped define the complexity factor of Wood et al. (2003). A person who gives many rich, synthesized responses will tend to produce more color responses of every kind.

Third, the color dominance score is a proportion, so it is partly protected from this, but any claim that it reflects something specific must show that it separates from complexity and productivity.

Whether letting color lead over form goes with seeing wholes or with seeing parts is an open and answerable question. The answer would say whether color dominance belongs to a general style of integration or stands apart from it.

The Neuroimaging Evidence

One imaging study compared the card types directly. Ishibashi et al. (2016) scanned 40 adults and found greater activity in left V4 and left orbitofrontal cortex for chromatic cards than for achromatic cards. The orbitofrontal result is sometimes taken as support for the color-affect hypothesis. It should not be. The card types differ in many ways besides color, and orbitofrontal cortex does much besides emotion. Giromini, Viglione, and colleagues have examined neural activity during Rorschach responding and mirroring activity linked to movement responses (Giromini et al., 2010 (Giromini et al., , 2017;; Porcelli et al., 2013; Vitolo et al., 2021). In a preliminary study, R-PAS stress and distress scores, which include achromatic color, predicted electrodermal reactivity to a later laboratory stressor (Giromini et al., 2016). None of this work isolates chromatic color, and that gap is an opening for vision science.

The Modern Science of Color and Emotion

Modern research finds that people link colors to emotions with striking agreement. Evidence that colors change how people feel is much thinner and less consistent, though it is not absent. Both halves of that sentence bear on the projective tradition.

Shared Associations: Jonauskaite and Colleagues

Jonauskaite, Abu-Akel, et al. ( 2020) asked 4,598 people from 30 nations, speaking 22 languages, to associate 12 color terms with 20 emotion concepts. National patterns correlated on average r = .88 with the global pattern. Agreement was closer between nations that are linguistically or geographically near. A classifier could still identify a participant's nation at 34.4% against a chance rate of 9.7%, so culture leaves a mark on a largely shared pattern. Among the prominent associations, red alone was linked to both a positive emotion, love, and a negative one, anger.

Four further results matter here.

• Words work nearly as well as patches. Emotion associations for color terms and for color patches were highly similar, r = .82 (Jonauskaite, Parraga, et al., 2020).

• Seeing the color is not required. Sixty-four men with congenital red-green color blindness showed associations similar to those of 66 controls, and severity of the deficiency made no difference (Jonauskaite et al., 2021). The associations are carried largely by concepts. The opposite split, seeing a color without having its concept, was noticed long ago. Darwin wrote that his young children "seemed quite incapable of affixing the right names to the colours in coloured engravings" (Darwin, 1877).

• Associations are not feelings. A review of 132 articles and 42,266 participants concluded: "for now, we do not know if we feel colours, but we know that colours convey emotions" (Jonauskaite & Mohr, 2025, Conclusions section). Of 31 studies of color and felt affect, only eight immersed participants in colored environments and accounted for lightness or chroma as well as hue, and their results were inconsistent (Jonauskaite et al., 2026).

• The body and the report can disagree. Sixty people spent six minutes in red or blue virtual-reality rooms that varied in lightness and saturation. Heart rate rose more in dark rooms than in medium-light ones, and skin conductance rose more in red rooms than in blue ones, on some lightness and saturation combinations. Self-reported valence and arousal showed no effect of hue, lightness, or saturation (Weijs et al., 2023).

Physiological arousal and felt emotion are separate outcomes.

Five linked presentations at the Progress in Colour Studies meeting in Wrocław, held on 10 to 12 September 2026, extend this picture. They are conference reports, not yet peer-reviewed publications, and the figures below are from the printed abstracts.

• Shared structure across 35 countries. In a sample of 6,995 people from 35 countries and 38 languages, association patterns were highly similar between countries (average r = .92). Two dimensions organized them: valence with lightness, and arousal with warmth (Ouznadji et al., 2026).

• No vision required. People with congenital blindness (n = 25), acquired blindness (n = 18), and intact vision (n = 60) rated 12 color terms against 20 emotion concepts.

Association patterns correlated between .865 and .900 across the three groups. The authors conclude that visual experience is not necessary for conceptual color-emotion associations (Jonauskaite, Gierlinger, et al., 2026).

• Colored rooms and felt emotion. Eighty people spent one minute in each of 18 virtualreality rooms that varied in hue and lightness. In the printed abstract, hue and lightness had no significant effect on which emotions people reported. Most reported feeling relaxed, calm, bored, or neutral (Adamczyk et al., 2026).

• Imagined against experienced. In a companion poster, 77 people both imagined colored rooms and were immersed in them. The two patterns of color-emotion correspondence agreed only weakly (r = .37). Red diverged most: 36% expected to feel tense in a red room, but 6% reported feeling tense after being in one, and the most common report was calm (Droz et al., 2026).

• Meaning behind the associations. A fifth paper coded the free associations people give to colors. When 135 people gave associations to their favorite and least favorite colors, the central prediction of ecological valence theory held: favorite colors drew mostly positive, object-based associations. Purple, whose emotional associations vary most across studies, carried the most abstract and diverse meanings. The authors suggest that the more abstract a color's meaning, the less people converge on the same emotions (Epicoco et al., 2026).

The conclusion is the same as before, and firmer: color carries affective meaning more reliably than it has been shown to produce the affect. Two of these results bear directly on the Rorschach.

The last suggests a conjecture. If abstract meanings produce divergent emotions, then concrete ones should produce convergent responses, and the red of Cards II and III has about as concrete a meaning as a color can have. The red-room result shows the distance between belief and experience. What people believe red will do to them is close to what Rorschach believed it did.

What red did in the room was much less.

The 2025 review also found that lightness and saturation carry much of the effect. Light colors go with positive emotions and dark colors with negative ones. Saturated colors go with positive, high-arousal emotions. Gray and black were each linked to sadness in 75% of the studies that examined the pairing.

Why the Associations Exist: Schloss and Colleagues

Karen Schloss's work supplies a mechanism. Ecological valence theory holds that people like a color to the degree that they like the things that carry it (Palmer & Schloss, 2010). In the original study, liking for associated objects explained 80% of the variance in average color preference.

Later tests in British, Saudi, and Japanese samples found less, from 66% down to 37% (Al-Rasheed et al., 2022; Taylor & Franklin, 2012; Yokosawa et al., 2016).

The theory makes preference movable. After people viewed liked objects of one color and disliked objects of another, preference for the color of the liked objects rose reliably (Strauss et al., 2013). Schloss et al. (2020) then showed that the familiar idea of happy yellow and sad blue depends on lightness and chroma. With those controlled, "colors with blue hue were no sadder than colors with yellow hue, and in some cases, colors with blue hue were actually happier" (p. 813).

Jonauskaite and Mohr (2022) questioned the color representations and the use of a single happysad scale in that study, and noted that dark yellows may be seen as brown.

More broadly, Schloss (2024) treats a color's meaning as something the observer infers in context from a network of learned color-concept associations. Meaning is inferred. It is not fixed.

Does Color Change Feeling and Behavior? Oberfeld, and Elliot and Maier

Two further lines of work ask whether color does something to the person, which is the question closest to Rorschach's own. Daniel Oberfeld's group in Mainz has tested the question with careful stimulus control. Wilms and Oberfeld (2018) noted that many earlier studies "failed to control for saturation and brightness when presenting stimuli with different hues" (p. 896). They varied hue, saturation, and brightness independently on an LED display. Sixty-two people viewed each of 30 colors for 30 seconds and then rated how they felt. Saturated and bright colors raised reported arousal and valence and produced stronger skin conductance responses, and arousal rose from blue and green to red. Saturation had the largest effect on arousal. The authors concluded that the effect of a color on emotion is determined "by the combination of hue, saturation, and brightness" (p. 910), not by hue alone. A second study from the same laboratory found no effect in a more ordinary setting. When 170 students took intelligence and memory tests in cabins painted red, pink, blue, or white, neither their performance nor their reported emotional state differed by color (von Castell et al., 2018). Brief, saturated, full-field color can shift reported feeling. The color of a room during a task may not.

Andrew Elliot and Markus Maier work in social and personality psychology. Their review begins with the older idea that long-wavelength colors such as red arouse and short-wavelength colors such as blue calm. Of the many studies testing it they write that "they have primarily produced null results; this is particularly the case for experiments utilizing rigorous methods" (Elliot & Maier, 2014, p. 98). That is the same verdict the Rorschach experiments reached on direct arousal. Their own color-in-context theory replaces arousal with meaning. A color carries meanings, some learned and some based on inherited tendencies. The inherited part has a long history. Darwin (1872) described the face reddening in rage, paling in fear, and blushing in shame, and observed that monkeys also redden when enraged. Red as a sign of anger may be read from faces before it is learned from culture. The meanings depend on the setting, and the color is said to shift motivation and behavior without the person's awareness (Elliot & Maier, 2012 , 2014). Red is the main case. In a test setting it is said to mean failure and to lower performance, and in courtship to increase attraction. The early findings drew wide attention, and they have weakened. A meta-analysis of 67 effect sizes found that the effect of red on test performance was much larger in the first studies than in later ones and concluded that, after correction for publication bias, there was no evidential value for it (Gnambs, 2020). A metaanalysis that included Elliot as an author found a small effect of red on men's ratings of women's attractiveness (d = 0.26) and a very small one for women rating men (d = 0.13), with signs that the effects had declined over time and were inflated by bias (Lehmann et al., 2018). Elliot and Maier (2014) themselves cautioned that the research "remains at a nascent stage of development" (p. 111).

The two halves of the theory have fared differently, and the pattern is familiar.

The claim that the same red means different things in different settings agrees with Schloss's account and with what Wallen and Siipola found in inkblots. The claim that red acts on behavior automatically is the closest modern counterpart to Rorschach's belief that color acts on the person. What This Does to the Projective Tradition

Second, high agreement leaves little for a diagnostic test to find. If nearly everyone links black with sadness, then linking black with sadness says little about a person. This is one reason tests that assign fixed personal meanings to colors, such as the Lüscher, face a basic validity problem.

Third, the Rorschach finding that survives is not reducible to shared meaning. It concerns how a person combines color with form, although meaning and the typical colors of objects may contribute to that and should be measured (Di Girolamo et al., 2026). Shared meaning and individual style are different things, and the two fields have each studied one of them.

A Constructivist Reading: Color Influences Emotion, Emotion Influences Color

Both literatures make more sense inside a model in which color and emotion determine each other. The constructivist account of color (Peterzell, 2026) supplies one. This section states the model briefly and then sorts the evidence by direction of influence.

The Model in Brief

The account distinguishes three levels.

• Level 1 is the physical stimulus: light of a given spectral composition reaching the eye.

• Level 2 is the color percept: the hue, saturation, and brightness a person consciously sees.

• Level 3 is the whole event the stimulus sets off. It has four families of processes: perceptual, physiological, cognitive-semantic, and affective-social-behavioral.

The percept is the visible core of the event, not the end of the process. The model's central claim is reciprocal determinism, a term borrowed from Bandura (1978). The Level 3 systems do not merely respond to the color. They help fix what is seen, while the percept shapes them in turn. 3 event in four families, each reciprocally related to the percept. Direction One: Color Influences Emotion

This is the direction both fields have mostly studied, and the evidence is uneven (Figure 5). That color calls up emotion concepts is well established: association patterns correlated r = .88 across 30 nations. That color calls up affectively loaded content has moderate support, in the blood seen in the red of Cards II and III and the love and anger words given to red Holtzman blots. That color raises bodily arousal has some support, inconsistent across measures. Heart rate rose in dark virtual rooms and skin conductance in red ones, without matching changes in reported feeling (Weijs et al., 2023). That color directly produces a felt emotion is not established as a general mechanism. Color shock was not shown to be caused by color, although saturation, brightness, and hue have shifted reported feeling under controlled conditions (Wilms & Oberfeld, 2018).

The pattern suggests that color reaches emotion mainly through the cognitive-semantic family and not by a direct line. Rorschach's first explanation was not established by the experiments that tested it, and Wallen's and Schloss's is better supported. In the model's terms, the path runs from the percept to meaning to affect.

Direction Two: Emotion Influences Color

This is the direction modern color-emotion science has largely set aside, and it is where the projective tradition has the most to offer. The oldest observation of emotion changing color is Darwin's (1872): rage reddens the face, fear pales it, and shame brings a blush. That is emotion changing the stimulus itself, at Level 1, for another observer. Within the observer, three kinds of influence need to be kept apart, because they sit at different levels.

Emotion shapes which colors a person chooses. After a sadness induction, healthy participants chose less chromatic and more often achromatic colors to represent their mood than after joy, relaxation, or fear, and darker colors than after joy or relaxation (Jonauskaite et al., 2019). Asked for a color to represent their mood, most anxious and depressed patients chose one, and two grays were their most frequent choices, whereas the healthy participants who chose a color most often picked a yellow (Carruthers et al., 2010). Use of black and gray in inkblot responses relates to independent markers of negative emotion (r = .24). This is the best supported of the three influences, and for color choice the evidence is experimental.

Emotion may shape how color is combined with form. Color dominance relates to several externally assessed clinical and behavioral criteria (r = .32) and to an impulsive cognitive tempo (Katz & Ziffo, 1975). On this reading, an aroused or poorly regulated state loosens the hold of form and lets the most salient feature lead. The direction of cause is not established, since the data are correlational. But the reading is the reverse of Rorschach's. It is the state of the person that alters the handling of color.

Emotion alters the percept itself. This is the strong claim, and the evidence is thin. People with depression often say the world looks gray. Bubl et al. (2010) reported sharply reduced retinal contrast gain in 40 patients with depression, measured with the pattern electroretinogram. The same laboratory has since reported related findings (Bubl et al., 2012 (Bubl et al., , 2015)), but the one independent test found normal electroretinograms alongside reduced psychophysical contrast sensitivity (Fam et al., 2013). These are results about luminance contrast, not about color. A report that sadness impairs blue-yellow discrimination was retracted by its authors within months (Thorstenson et al., 2015). Knowledge can reach the percept: the memory color effect shows that what a person knows about an object's color alters its appearance (Hansen et al., 2006; Witzel et al., 2011). That is a Level 3 process changing Level 2, but it concerns object knowledge, not emotion. Robinson et al. (2015) review the evidence on affect and color perception. And Firestone and Scholl (2014) showed that some reported effects of mental states on perceived brightness behave like effects on judgment, not on perception. The question is open.

Here the model's warning applies. Inkblot data show that emotion is tied to how people use and report color. They do not show that emotion changes how color looks. The first is a Level 3 behavior. The second would be a Level 2 effect. Reciprocal determinism predicts the second, and the projective literature cannot confirm it. That is a prediction to test.

Where Personality Enters

Bandura's formulation had three parts: the person, the person's behavior, and the environment, each shaping the others. Personality, in the sense of stable individual differences, is the first part.

The projective tradition's lasting contribution may be its insistence that observers differ in a stable way in how they handle color. Many Rorschach scores have been reported to hold steady over retest intervals of years (Exner et al., 1978). Classical group-average methods in vision science often treated such differences as noise. Individual-differences research in vision treats them as signal: the way measures vary together across observers reveals the mechanisms behind them (Mollon et al., 2017; Peterzell, 2016). The constructivist account takes the same view of the chromatic event. Jonauskaite and Mohr (2025) make a related point from within color-emotion science: not everybody shares the same correspondences, and individual differences that have not yet been studied probably explain much of the variation.

Two features of the Rorschach data fit this view. Agreement on what colors mean is high, so meaning is unlikely to be the main place where people differ. And the color scores show little relation to self-description in the few studies available, so the differences may not be available to introspection. What varies between people appears to be a way of processing: the weight given to color against form when a percept must be constructed. That is a perceptual individual difference with affective correlates, which is the kind of variable a reciprocal model expects to find.

What Vision Science Can Learn

The projective literature offers vision science several things, none of which requires accepting projective testing as a clinical tool. It offers a neglected individual difference, the balance of color against form. It offers an early result on color-form conflict and a testable hypothesis that color is taken in with less effort than form. It offers a cautionary record of what happens when uncalibrated stimuli are interpreted for decades. And it offers the reverse question: whether the state of the person shows in how color is used. In return, vision science can offer calibrated stimuli, more reliable measurement, the distinction between associating a color with an emotion and feeling it, and a mechanism, learned color-concept associations, for the content the cards evoke.

Studies That Would Close the Gap

Five studies would connect the two fields. Kron et al. (2009) made a start on the fourth, and two older studies bear on the second. Otherwise I found no published study that combines these elements.

1. Calibrated inkblots. Build blots in an approximately uniform color space, measure the displayed colors, and vary hue, lightness, and chroma independently on the same forms.

Code responses with R-PAS and record reaction time, skin conductance, and heart rate.

This is Lazarus's design with modern color science, and it provides a controlled basis for testing the five explanations. Figure 6 shows the logic.

2. Color-deficient observers.

Give the standard cards to people with congenital red-green deficiency and to matched controls. People with these deficiencies still see color, so measure each observer's color discrimination and do not rely on group labels. If color-led responses and blood content depend mainly on knowing a region is red, they will vary little with discrimination. If they depend on seeing it, they will vary more. The finding that blind and color-deficient people share the usual color-emotion associations makes the first outcome the likelier one. Two older studies give different answers. Among young men, 43 with color vision deficits differed from 43 controls only in giving fewer pure color responses (Corsino, 1985). Among 42 older adults, the 10 with color vision deficits showed more affective constriction on the Rorschach than the 32 with normal color vision, after controls for intelligence and number of responses (Hayslip et al., 1992). The first sample probably had lifelong deficits and the second deficits acquired with age, so the two are not strictly comparable. CIELAB is only approximately uniform, so a real study would measure the displayed colors and report their tristimulus values and viewing conditions. Displayed and printed colors will differ. A full design would add shading as a fourth factor.

Conclusion

The Rorschach tells us almost nothing about how color is seen and something modest about how color is used. Its founding belief, that color stirs emotion directly, was not established by the experiments that tested it. Narrower findings fared better. Letting color lead over form is modestly associated with several independently assessed outcomes. Some indices that set color in balance with other responses, such as the one for coping versus overload, are also supported, although the critics dispute several of them. These results show that the scores track something real. They do not establish what the scores measure. One experiment has shown that the balance of color against form predicts how strongly color affects the naming of ordinary objects (Kron et al., 2009). It has not been replicated, and it shows that the question belongs to vision science.

Modern color-emotion science explains what the projective tradition could not. Colors carry widely shared emotional meanings learned from experience with colored things. That explains the blood on Card II and helps explain the weakness of tests built on fixed color meanings. Neither field has the whole picture. One has studied what colors mean to nearly everyone. The other has studied how individuals differ in handling color. A model in which color and emotion determine each other reciprocally has room for both, and it points to the question both have left open: whether a person's emotional state changes not only what they do with color but what they see.
