Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

Metacheck does not yet analyze automatically whether sentences which include non-significant p-values are correct, but we recommend manually checking the sentences below for possible misinterpreted non-significant p values.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on 226 Psychological Science papers with 5,019 hand-coded p-values, 438 of them imprecise (scienceverse/validation, branch stat_p_exact). Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). Of the misses, 13 are starred table notes (“*p < .05”) that the module leaves out on purpose and 11 are p-values it did not find; of the wrong reports, 20 are p-values the ground truth does not contain (such as “ps < .05”) and 6 use ≤ or ≥. How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Abstract

Religious engagement has been associated with meaning in life, yet research has primarily examined how much people engage in religious behaviors rather than how valuable they perceive those behaviors to be. Drawing on values theory and situated expectancy-value theory, we examined whether religious behavior duration and perceived behavioral value show distinct associations with daily meaning in life. Participants completed daily surveys over 50 days, reporting time spent across 16 religious activities, perceived value of activities performed, and meaning in life. The analytic sample included 90 adults. Using Bayesian residual dynamic structural equation modeling (RDSEM), we separated withinperson fluctuations from between-person differences and accounted for temporal dependencies and systematic weekend effects. In an exploratory joint model, both longer-than-usual religious behavior duration (β = .11, 95% credible interval [.056, .16]) and greater-than-usual perceived value (β = .19, 95% credible interval [.15, .23]) were independently associated with greater same-day meaning in life.

Between individuals, average perceived value was associated with greater meaning in life (β = .53), whereas average duration was not. Preregistered analyses found no credible association between consistency of religious engagement and meaning, no average reciprocal next-day associations between duration and value, and no evidence that perceived value moderated the duration-meaning association.

Additional exploratory analyses revealed no consistent next-day associations with meaning in life.

Findings suggest that time spent engaging in religion and the value attributed to those experiences are distinct correlates of daily meaning, highlighting the importance of assessing perceived behavioral value alongside duration.

Introduction

Studying outcomes of behaviors is a common, and important part of most research programs within the social sciences. This can be done by focusing on the outcomes associated with how much, or how long people may engage with a particular behavior, but this ignores the effect of how individuals subjectively experience or value those behaviors. Through two theoretical perspectives, we suggest that the perceived value of a behavior may also influence its outcomes. Individuals may perceive a behavior as valuable for several reasons. Behaviors may be perceived as valuable because they are enjoyable, instantiate abstract values, help attain valued goals, or feel important to one's identity (Eccles & Wigfield, 2024; Hanel et al., 2018; Maio, 2010). Valued behaviors should be more motivating and more likely to be repeated, though whether that process is adaptive intuitively depends on the behavior itself. Engagement in valued behaviors may be especially important to developing meaning in life by connecting behavior with valued principles and goals (Caldaroni et al., 2026). Religion provides a relevant developmental context for examining these relationships, given the established associations between religious behavior and meaning in life (Prinzing et al., 2023). Accordingly, the present study uses intensive longitudinal data and residual dynamic structural equation modeling (RDSEM) to examine how both the duration and perceived value of religious behaviors relate to daily meaning in life.

Value of a Behavior

There are multiple theories we can draw on when making claims about the importance of studying perceived behavioral value, particularly for domain-specific activities such as religious behaviors. One line of reasoning builds upon values literature while the other follows the situated expectancy-value model literature. Both theories may predict adaptive outcomes, such as meaning in life, depending on the behavior of study.

Regarding values theory, values are abstract guiding principles that shape what individuals consider desirable and may motivate corresponding behaviors (Maio, 2010; Rokeach, 1973; Schwartz, 1992). Values are distinct from more proximal and concrete predictors of behaviors such as attitudes, intentions, and goals (Ajzen & Fishbein, 2005). Maio (2010) conceptualized three levels to values theory: systems (relationships between values), values (such as obedience), and value instantiations-the behaviors expressing those values. Because values are abstract, the same behavior may instantiate different abstract and possibly related values, which can make a behavior feel valuable (Hanel et al., 2018; Maio, 2010). For example, prayer may express conservation, self-enhancement, openness to change, self-transcendence, or some combination of these four higher-order value domains identified by Schwartz (1992). One reason individuals may perceive a behavior as valuable is that it instantiates personally important abstract values. Thus, value instantiation provides one theoretical rationale for studying perceived behavioral value alongside behavioral engagement. Furthermore, values may motivate corresponding behaviors, while repeated engagement may reinforce or alter certain values (Bardi et al., 2009; Maio, 2010), suggesting potential bidirectional influences between behavioral engagement and perceived behavioral value. Thus, beyond duration, the perceived value of religious behaviors may reflect the extent to which individuals experience them as expressions of personally important values, making them valued behaviors.

A second explanation justifying the importance of studying valued behaviors extends beyond values to the subjective experience of the activity itself. Situated expectancy-value theory conceptualizes this as subjective task value, comprising intrinsic value (enjoyment), utility value (usefulness for achieving goals), attainment value (personal importance), and perceived cost (Eccles & Wigfield, 2024). Therefore, a single behavior being seen as valuable may involve multiple components. For example, attending a worship service may be enjoyable, help fulfill a religious goal, satisfy a need for belonging, and may do this with a relatively low perceived cost.

Together, these two theoretical perspectives suggest that religious behaviors may be perceived as valuable for multiple reasons: they may instantiate abstract values, fulfill social expectations, reflect prior reinforcement, provide intrinsic enjoyment, help achieve desired outcomes, or hold personal importance. Thus, perceived behavioral value may capture dimensions of meaningful behavior engagement beyond what behavioral duration can.

In addition to justifying perceived behavioral value as a construct of interest, value instantiation and subjective task value offer another contribution to the present study. They both present complementary explanations for how perceived behavioral value may motivate engagement. This demonstrates a theoretical link between perceived behavioral value and later behavioral engagement. Behaviors expressing abstract values may encourage value-consistent action (Hanel et al., 2018; Maio, 2010), while those perceived as enjoyable, useful, or personally important may motivate repeated engagement (Eccles & Wigfield, 2024). In other words, engaging in valued behaviors may lead to repeating those behaviors. However, whether such engagement promotes adaptive outcomes, however, likely depends on the nature of the valued behavior. But in general, it appears that engaging in valued behaviors may provide experiences through which individuals develop a stronger sense of meaning in life (Caldaroni et al., 2026).

Therefore, examining both the duration of religious behaviors and their perceived value may capture complementary dimensions of behavioral engagement predicting meaning in life and help explain why the same behaviors have different outcomes across individuals.

Meaning in Life

One potential outcome of engaging in valued behaviors is a greater sense of meaning in life. Before examining this relationship, it is important to distinguish between two related constructs. Fuhrer et al., (2026) differentiate between believing that life has inherent meaning (meaning of life) and experiencing one's own life as meaningful (meaning in life). Interestingly, Fuhrer et al. (2026) identify that religious behaviors are more related to meaning of life, yet the present study measures meaning in life according to the above conceptualization. Additionally, Fuhrer et al. (2026) note that for much of prior quantitative research, the phenomenon that has been studied has actually been meaning in life, even if the claim has been meaning of life.

Therefore, although meaning of life appears more theoretically tied to religious and metaphysical beliefs, meaning in life is the construct that has historically been linked to well-being, health, and longevity (Fuhrer et al., 2026; Routledge & FioRito, 2021). Accordingly, we build on this literature by examining whether religious engagement predicts the extent to which individuals experience their own lives as meaningful.

Understanding how meaning in life develops is particularly important given its relevance to human development and well-being. Experiencing meaning in life may be an evolutionarily adaptive outcome that contributes to self-actualization (Upenieks, 2022). Meaning in life has also been associated with longer, healthier lives and has been found to uniquely predict health-related behaviors, such as physical activity, beyond other measures of well-being (Routledge & FioRito, 2021). These findings support the notion that meaning in life is an essential human need that deserves greater attention to how it can be predicted both within and between people. Knowing that meaning in life is an important developmental outcome, common places meaning in life can develop is via systems and routines. Individuals find meaning in life through diverse systems, including family, relationships, employment, education, recreational activities, and religious faith ("Where Americans Find Meaning in Life," 2018). Beyond systems, routines of behaviors may also contribute to the development of meaning in life. Behavioral routines are best conceptualized as recurring patterns of behavior, and have shown to influence meaning in life by providing structure and coherence to everyday experiences (Heintzelman & King, 2019; Mohideen & Heintzelman, 2023).

Religion and Meaning in Life

Religion provides a relevant developmental context for studying meaning in life, particularly in the United States, where more than half of adults identify with a religious tradition (Smith et al., 2025). In general, research shows that religion may help fulfill fundamental needs by shaping worldviews (Greenberg et al., 2014), fostering belonging (Baumeister & Leary, 1995), and supporting well-being (Hinkel et al., 2021; Koçak, 2022). Particularly relevant to the present study, religion may also predict meaning in life (Prinzing et al., 2023), which has been found to mediate the association between religiousness and well-being (Steger & Frazier, 2005).

Religious behaviors, broadly defined as activities undertaken for religious purposes, may provide one pathway through which individuals develop meaning in life. Research has generally found positive associations between both public and private religious behaviors and meaning in life (Fuhrer et al., 2026; Prinzing et al., 2023), with consistent or increasing religious behavioral engagement also associated with greater meaning in life (Upenieks, 2022).

Beyond these associations between individuals, research has also examined whether people experience greater meaning in life on days when they engage in more religious activity than usual. In a 14-day daily-diary study, Steger & Frazier (2005) found that individuals reported greater meaning in life on days with more religious activity relative to their typical levels.

Similarly, experience-sampling studies found heightened momentary meaning during religious and spiritual practices, including prayer and worship (Choi et al., 2017; Kucinskas et al., 2018). Prinzing et al. (2023) further identified within-and between-person associations, with positive relationships between religiousness and meaning in life across nightly assessments over 11 weeks and quarterly assessments over 18 months. However, their nightly measure assessed religious experience ("I felt God's presence"), while their quarterly measure combined behavioral duration with religious importance. Thus, although these findings suggest that religious engagement is associated with meaning in life across timescales in general, they do not consider valued behaviors in their assessments.

The Present Study

The present study extends existing research on religious engagement and meaning in life by examining not only the duration of religious behaviors but also their perceived value.

Meaning in life is an important developmental outcome that individuals may cultivate through within various developmental contexts, including religion. Previous research has established positive associations between religious engagement and meaning in life (Upenieks, 2022), although studies have historically only examined individual behaviors, their duration, or broader measures of religiousness (Prinzing et al., 2023). Beyond the amount of engagement, research on behavioral routines suggests that consistent engagement may also contribute to meaning in life (Mohideen & Heintzelman, 2023). However, behavioral duration alone is likely not the only path towards meaning in life. Drawing from values theory and situated expectancy-value theory, behaviors that are perceived as valuable may also contribute to meaning in life development and maintenance (Eccles & Wigfield, 2024; Hanel et al., 2018). Furthermore, perceived value may motivate repeated engagement, while engagement may reinforce perceived value, suggesting potentially bidirectional longitudinal relationships (Bardi et al., 2009). Finally, because behaviors may be valued for different reasons, their perceived value may also influence the association between behavioral duration and meaning in life. By examining these dimensions separately and together, the present study seeks to clarify how religious behavioral engagement relates to meaning in life.

Although intensive longitudinal studies have found within-person associations between religiousness and meaning in life, modeling these dynamic processes presents methodological challenges. Combining multilevel and time-series approaches can introduce Lüdtke's bias, arising from measurement error in observed person means, and Nickell's bias, arising when lagged outcomes are associated with stable person-level effects, violating exogeneity assumptions (Gistelinck et al., 2021). Dynamic structural equation modeling (DSEM) helps address these concerns by integrating multilevel, time-series, and structural equation modeling.

Specifically, DSEM uses latent decomposition to distinguish stable between-person differences from within-person fluctuations while accounting for temporal dependencies. Furthermore, residual DSEM (RDSEM) allows researchers to account for systematic weekend effects common to many Judeo-Christian religious practices, reducing potential violations of stationarity and bias in temporal estimates. This approach allows simultaneous examination of individual differences, within-person associations, and autoregressive effects, making it well suited to the present study.

The study addressed the following preregistered hypotheses: 1.

On days when individuals engage in more religious behavior than usual, they will report greater meaning in life.

2. On days when individuals perceive their religious behaviors as more valuable than usual, they will report greater meaning in life.

3. Individuals with less day-to-day variability in religious behavioral duration will report greater meaning in life. 4. Religious behavioral duration and perceived value will positively predict one another across consecutive days.

5. Perceived behavioral value will positively moderate the within-person association between religious behavioral duration and meaning in life, such that the association is stronger on days when behaviors are perceived as more valuable.

Measures

Duration of Religious Behaviors. Daily religious behavior duration (or simply duration) was assessed using 16 items corresponding to common religious and spiritual activities, including worship attendance, scripture study, prayer, religious discussion, spiritual reflection, religious music, and participation in religious ordinances (see Table S1 in the online supplements for the full measure). For each activity, participants reported the approximate number of minutes spent engaging in that activity that day. Minutes were summed across available activity responses and converted to hours by dividing by 60. Missing activity-level responses were excluded from the daily sum; daily duration was coded as missing when all 16 activity responses were missing.

Perceived Religious Behavioral Value. The perceived value of religious behaviors engaged in during daily life (or simply value) was assessed using 16 items corresponding to common religious and spiritual activities, including worship attendance, scripture study, prayer, religious discussion, spiritual reflection, religious music, and participation in religious ordinances (see Table S2 in the online supplements for the full measure). For each activity completed that day, participants rated the average value, significance, or impact of their time spent in that activity using a scale from 0 (not at all valuable) to 10 (extremely valuable). Ratings were averaged across available activities to indicate the average value of religious behaviors for that day. Daily value was coded as missing when no value ratings were available.

Meaning in Life.

Daily meaning in life was assessed using three items capturing adolescents' sense of meaning, purpose, and significance in life that day (see Table S3 in the online supplements for the full measure). Participants responded to each item on a scale from 1 (not at all) to 10 (extremely).

Time and weekend.

A day variable was created from each participant's survey start date and indexed the sequence of daily assessments across the study period. A weekend dummy variable was then created from the day variable to account for recurring calendar effects.

Including weekend allowed the DSEM to account for systematic weekday-weekend differences while estimating dynamic relations among the residual processes.

Participants

Participants were 139 individuals (82% women; 80% European American, 9% African American, 7% Latino, 2% Asian American, and 2% other) ages 18 -69 (M age 25.11, SD 10.77). Most participants were college students, with 44% enrolled in public universities, 43% in private universities, and 13% not currently enrolled at a university. By religious affiliation, 24% were Catholic, 22% were Protestant, 37% were Latter-day Saint (LDS or Mormon), 6% were identified with other affiliations, and 11% were not affiliated with any religion. Regarding marital status, 28% were married. In terms of education level, 6% had already graduated from a 4-year college or university, while 9% had some education beyond a 4-year degree.

Demographics information is summarized in Table S4 located within the online supplements.

Procedures

Participants were recruited from four U.S. universities: two private institutions in the West and Midwest and two public institutions in the East and South. Institutional review board approval was obtained at each university. Recruitment occurred through psychology department subject pools and posted flyers. Interested individuals completed online consent and provided their contact information before receiving an emailed baseline survey through Qualtrics, which assessed demographics and trait-level characteristics. Several days later, participants began receiving daily surveys for 50 consecutive days. This period was selected to capture a substantial portion of the academic semester and provide sufficient observations for longitudinal analyses.

Identical survey links were emailed each evening and remained active for 24 hours. Participants could respond that evening or the following morning or afternoon, but all questions referred to experiences on the day the survey was distributed. Participants enrolled in psychology courses received extra credit for completing the baseline survey. Daily survey compensation increased with participation: $1 per survey for the first 20 completed, $2 for the next 20, and $3 for the final 10, allowing participants to earn up to $90. Additionally, ten $100 Amazon gift cards were awarded through a raffle, with participants receiving one entry for every ten completed daily surveys. The initial sample included 142 participants. Thirty-one person-day observations containing no responses to variables of interest were excluded. Of the remaining 8,822 possible person-day observations, 5,497 were completed (62.3%) and 3,325 were missed (37.7%).

Although compliance was below the ideal 70%-85% reported in reviews of intensive longitudinal studies, missingness is common in such designs (Jones et al., 2019; May et al., 2018; Wen et al., 2017). An unabridged participants section is found in the online supplements.

Analysis Plan

All variables were computed, and descriptive and preliminary analyses were conducted in Stata version 19.5. Primary statistical analyses were conducted in Mplus version 9. We first conducted descriptive statistics. Descriptive statistics include frequencies, summary statistics (n, mean, SD, min max), standardized covariance matrices, confirmatory factor analyses (CFA) to determine dimensionality of our items, and reliability tests integrated within the DSEM analysis using the MEAR model (Schuurman & Hamaker, 2019). We then conducted our primary analyses using Dynamic Structural Equation Modeling (DSEM).

We assigned each hypothesis independent models to reduce complexity and achieve convergence. To address hypothesis 1, we modeled momentary duration of religiousness predicting momentary meaning in life, including weekend effects and carry over effects (Figure 1). To address hypothesis 2, we modeled momentary value of enacted religious behaviors predicting momentary meaning in life, including carryover effects (Figure 2). To address hypothesis 3, we modeled greater consistency (less volatility) in religious duration predicting greater meaning in life, including weekend effects, between-person variability in random error variance, viewing both within-and between-person models (Figure 3). To address hypothesis 4, we modeled duration and value of enacted religious behaviors reciprocally relating over time, including weekend effects, carryover effects with both within-and between-person models (Figure 4). To address hypothesis 5, we modeled the value of enacted religious behaviors as a moderator for the relationship between duration and meaning in life (Figure 5; Speyer et al., 2024). Preliminary Statistics

Preliminary analyses were conducted to examine the structure and distribution of the data at the between-person level in Stata V19.5. We ran two separate analyses, one for the variable means, variances, carryover effects and influence of the weekend effect on the variable, and one for the multilevel descriptive statistics. For our between-person descriptives, we did two analyses to determine the effect cleaning our data had (Table S5 and Table S6). In the between-person descriptives, we inspected variable distributions using frequencies and summary statistics, including sample size, means, standard deviations, minimums, and maximums. We also examined standardized covariance matrices, conducted confirmatory factor analyses to evaluate item dimensionality and Cronbach's alpha. After all between-person descriptives, we estimated within-person reliability within the DSEM framework using the MEAR model in Mplus V9.

Multilevel descriptive statistics, within-and between-person associations, weekend effects, and carryover parameters are reported in Table S7, located in the online supplements.

Missingness and Person-Day Observations.

Participants with fewer than five valid observations on the focal variables were excluded because of insufficient repeated observations for estimating within-person dynamic processes, resulting in a final analytic sample of 90 participants (36.62% excluded). Within this sample, religious behavioral duration and value were observed for 80.7% of person-day observations, whereas meaning in life was observed for 95.0%. Daily religious behavior duration was coded as missing when all 16 activity responses were missing, whereas perceived behavioral value was coded as missing when no value ratings were available. Missingness was predominantly intermittent rather than attributable to dropout (see Figures S1-S3). Sensitivity analyses using the full sample (N = 142) failed to converge to an admissible solution because the between-person posterior variance-covariance matrix was not positive definite. Consequently, primary RDSEM analyses used the 90-participant sample.

Additional missingness diagnostics and sensitivity analyses are reported in the online supplements.

Weekend Effect. Preliminary analyses indicated that several series exhibited systematic temporal trends that violated the assumption of stationarity, partly due to increased weekend religious activity. Following recommended practice, we addressed this issue using residual DSEM (RDSEM), including a weekend dummy variable to account for recurring calendar effects before estimating the dynamic processes of interest (Figures 1, 3, 4, & 5). Thus, the autoregressive and cross-lagged relations were estimated from the detrended residual series rather than the raw observed scores. Some person-day observations showed unusually high levels of religious behavior duration, often occurring on weekends but varying across weekends. This pattern likely reflected overlap between the study period and general conference among CJCLDS participants, during which members engage in extended religious instruction that temporarily increases religious behavior engagement. These calendar-related effects were accounted for in the primary analyses.

Dimensionality and Reliability.

Between-person dimensionality was assessed using confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) of person-level item averages. Religious behavior duration did not exhibit a unidimensional reflective structure, supporting its treatment as a cumulative behavioral index. In contrast, perceived behavioral value and meaning in life exhibited strong standardized factor loadings (.67-.88 and .95-.99, respectively), supporting their composite scores. Between-person reliability, assessed using Cronbach's alpha, was acceptable for duration (α = .78) and excellent for value (α = .94) and meaning in life (α = .98). At the within-person level, we decomposed daily scores into latent within-person fluctuations and stable betweenperson differences. Within-person reliability was assessed using a measurement error autoregressive (MEAR) model (Schuurman & Hamaker, 2019). Reliability was low for value (ρ wp = .45) and meaning in life (ρ wp = .39), suggesting substantial measurement error in daily fluctuations which is discussed in our limitations section. The duration model did not converge, preventing estimation of its within-person reliability. Additional details are provided in the online supplements.

Primary Analysis

All models were estimated using Bayesian Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) methods, beginning with 1,500 iterations and doubling the number of iterations to assess convergence. Convergence was evaluated using the potential scale reduction (PSR) statistic, which compares between-chain and within-chain variability. Values approaching 1.00 (typically ≤ 1.05) were considered indicative of adequate convergence. We also examined whether PSR values increased with additional iterations. Unabridged results are reported in the online supplements.

Greater Religious Behavior Duration Was Associated With Greater Meaning in Life

Our first hypothesis examined whether individuals reported greater meaning in life on days when they engaged in more religious behavior than usual (Figure 1). Results supported this hypothesis (Table 1). Greater daily religious behavior duration was positively associated with daily meaning in life (β = .163, 95% CrI [.094, .219]), explaining approximately 13.1% of within-person variance. This association varied across individuals. In a supplementary betweenperson analysis, average religious behavior duration was not credibly associated with average meaning in life (β = .153, 95% CrI [-.073, .370]). Thus, the hypothesized association was supported within individuals across days, but not between individuals.

Greater Religious Behavior Value Was Associated With Greater Meaning in Life

Our second hypothesis examined whether individuals reported greater meaning in life on days when they valued their religious behaviors more than usual (Figure 2). Results supported this hypothesis (Table 2). Greater daily religious behavior value was positively associated with daily meaning in life (β = .227, 95% CrI [.182, .271]), explaining 12.5% of within-person variance. At the between-person level, average religious behavior value was also positively associated with average meaning in life (β = .516, 95% CrI [.329, .664]), explaining 26.6% of between-person variance. Thus, perceived value was associated with meaning in life both within and between individuals.

Religious Behavior Variability Was Not Associated With Meaning in Life

To test our third hypothesis, we examined whether variability in religious behavior duration predicted average meaning in life using three complementary approaches. First, RDSEM indicated no credible association between residual variability in daily religious duration and average meaning in life (Table 3; Figure 3). This measure captured fluctuations unexplained by weekend effects and previous-day duration. Second, multiple regression indicated that weekly duration-pattern inconsistency, measured using seven-day root mean square successive differences (RMSSD), was not significantly associated with average meaning in life, b = -0.159, p = .519, R² = .011. Third, within-person standard deviations in daily religious duration were also unrelated to average meaning in life, b = 0.263, p = .351, R² = .019. Both regressions were done in Stata version 19.5 and controlled for average religious duration. Thus, none of the three approaches provided evidence that variability in religious behavior duration was associated with average meaning in life.

No Reciprocal Lagged Associations Between Duration and Value

To test our fourth hypothesis, we examined whether duration and value of religious behaviors were reciprocally related over time (Figure 4). The results did not support our hypothesis. Specifically, when modeling variability in both religious duration and value as cross-lagged predictors of each other, we found no significant association (Table 4). We still found weekend effects for duration, carryover effects for both duration and value with between-person differences in the within-person averages for the both cross-lagged relationships and carryover effects. In other words, there may be so much heterogeneity in how duration and value influence each other, that there is no population average in how they influence each other.

Perceived Behavioral Value Does Not Moderate the Duration-Meaning Association

To test our fifth hypothesis, we used a two-stage RDSEM to examine whether daily perceived value moderated the contemporaneous within-person association between religious behavior duration and meaning in life (Figure 5). Contrary to our hypothesis, the interaction was not credibly different from zero (Table 5). However, both duration and value independently predicted greater daily meaning, and the duration-meaning association varied across participants.

Thus, perceived value did not reliably strengthen the association between duration and meaning in life.

Duration and Value Independently Predict Daily Meaning

Following our preregistered analyses, we conducted an exploratory joint RDSEM to examine whether religious behavior duration and perceived value independently predicted daily meaning in life. Results indicated that both duration and value were positively associated with each other, as well as daily meaning at the within-person level, even after accounting for one another (Table 6). At the between-person level, only value was positively associated with meaning. Thus, duration and value showed distinct within-person associations with daily meaning, whereas only value was associated with differences in average meaning across individuals.

Lagged Associations Show No Consistent Average Effects

We then explored whether religious behavior duration and perceived value predicted next-day meaning in life and whether value moderated the lagged duration-meaning association.

Neither duration nor value showed a consistent average lagged association with meaning (Tables S8-S9). Similarly, value did not credibly moderate the lagged duration-meaning association (Table S10). Thus, these exploratory analyses provided no evidence of average next-day associations.

Discussion

The present study examined how religious behavior duration and perceived value relate to meaning in life, whether consistency in engagement matters, whether duration and value predict one another across days, and whether perceived value moderates or independently contributes to the duration-meaning relationship. Overall, both duration and perceived value were independently associated with greater daily meaning in life, although only perceived value was associated with meaning across individuals. We found no evidence that behavioral consistency was associated with meaning, that duration and value predicted one another across days, or that perceived value moderated the duration-meaning relationship.

Finding #1: Religious Behavior Duration Associates with Meaning in Life.

Our first analysis found that religious behavior duration was positively associated with meaning in life within individuals across days, but not between individuals. Specifically, participants reported greater meaning in life on days when they engaged in religious behaviors longer than usual, whereas individuals who spent more time engaging in religion on average did not report reliably greater meaning in life (Figure 1, Table 1). The within-person finding aligns with previous daily-diary and experience-sampling research linking religious activities to greater daily or momentary meaning (Choi et al., 2017; Steger & Frazier, 2005). However, the absence of a credible between-person association differs from previous research identifying positive associations between religiousness and meaning in life across individuals (Prinzing et al., 2023).

Importantly, previous studies primarily assessed whether individuals engaged in select religious behaviors, such as prayer and worship attendance (Choi et al., 2017; Kucinskas et al., 2018; Prinzing et al., 2023; Steger & Frazier, 2005), rather than how long they spent engaging in a broader range of religious activities. Thus, our findings extend previous research by suggesting that the duration of religious engagement may be more consistently associated with daily fluctuations in meaning than with stable differences in meaning across individuals.

Finding #2: Perceived Behavioral Value Associates with Meaning in Life.

Our second analysis suggests that perceiving religious behaviors as more valuable associates with meaning in life. This is true for both individuals across days, and across people.

Specifically, when a person feels they have greater value in their religious engagement, they may also report feeling greater meaning in life that same day, but also that on average people who engage in valued religious behaviors more may also report greater meaning in life (Figure 2, Table 2). Consistent with values theory and situated expectancy-value theory, these findings provide preliminary evidence that engaging in valued behaviors may be relevant to the development and maintenance of meaning in life, potentially extending beyond religious contexts (Eccles & Wigfield, 2024; Hanel et al., 2018).

Finding #3: Religious Behavior Variability Was Not Associated With Meaning in Life.

Our third analysis found no evidence that consistency in religious behavior duration was associated with meaning in life. Specifically, individuals who spent more consistent amounts of time engaging in religious behaviors across days or weeks did not report reliably greater meaning in life (Figure 3, Table 3). This finding contrasts with previous research linking behavioral routines to meaning in life (Mohideen & Heintzelman, 2023). However, whereas previous research examined routines as recurring patterns of specific activities, the present study assessed consistency in the total duration of religious engagement. Thus, consistency in time spent engaging in religion may not capture the structured behavioral routines previously associated with meaning in life.

Finding #4: No Reciprocal Lagged Associations Between Duration and Value.

Our fourth analysis found no credible average cross-lagged associations between religious behavior duration and perceived value. Specifically, spending more time engaging in religion than usual does not appear to predict greater perceived value the following day, nor do perceiving religious behaviors as more valuable predict greater next-day engagement (Figure 4, Table 4). This was unexpected given values theory and situated expectancy-value theory, which suggest that engaging in valued behaviors may motivate subsequent engagement (Eccles & Wigfield, 2024; Hanel et al., 2018). Additionally, previous tests of this mechanism using intensive longitudinal data have found that the importance of valued activities can predict subsequent behavioral engagement (Villanueva et al., 2020; Wieland et al., 2021). However, these studies examined prospective, goal-directed behaviors rather than whether the perceived value of completed behaviors predicts subsequent engagement. Additioanlly, Villanueva et al., examined the relationship over a shorter time-scale. In contrast, the present study assessed the perceived value of religious behaviors already performed, averaged across activities, and examined its association with total religious engagement the following day. Thus, associations between perceived value and subsequent engagement may be behavior-specific or unfold over shorter intervals than those captured by daily assessments (Kuppens et al., 2022).

Finding #5: Perceived Behavioral Value Does Not Moderate the Duration-Meaning Association.

Our fifth analysis found no credible evidence that perceived behavioral value moderated the within-person association between religious behavior duration and meaning in life (Figure 5, Table 5). This finding suggests that perceiving religious behaviors as more valuable does not reliably influence the relationship between how much time is spent engaging in religion and daily meaning in life. This finding suggests that the duration of behavioral engagement and the perceived value of engaging in those behaviors may contribute independently to meaning in life rather than necessarily interacting.

Finding #6: Duration and Value Independently Predict Daily Meaning.

Our sixth analysis was perhaps the most informative, revealing two distinct associations with meaning in life. Although religious behavior duration and perceived value represent related dimensions of religious engagement, each appear to be independently associated with greater daily meaning, even after accounting for the other. However, only perceived behavioral value was associated with meaning across individuals. These findings extend prior research linking religiousness to meaning in life (Prinzing et al., 2023; Steger & Frazier, 2005) by demonstrating that time spent engaging in religious behaviors remains positively associated with daily meaning, even after accounting for their perceived value. Furthermore, these findings extend the study of valued behaviors (Eccles & Wigfield, 2024; Maio, 2010) by demonstrating that perceived behavioral value was associated with meaning both within and between individuals, even after accounting for time spent engaging in those behaviors. Together, these findings provide preliminary evidence that the time individuals spend engaging in religious behaviors and the value they place on those experiences may represent complementary but distinct pathways toward the development and maintenance of meaning in life.

Limitations

The present study had several notable limitations. First, the study had substantial missing data and excluded participants with insufficient repeated observations, potentially introducing selection bias. However, missingness was primarily intermittent rather than due to dropout, and the remaining data made it possible to study repeated observations across a 50-day study period.

Second, low within-person reliability for perceived behavioral value and meaning in life may have influenced the associations and reduced the precision of our estimates. Nevertheless, credible associations still emerged despite this which provides preliminary evidence for the relationships of interest. Third, our primary findings were contemporaneous, preventing conclusions about temporal ordering or causality. However, the intensive longitudinal design and RDSEM allowed us to still distinguish daily within-person associations from stable betweenperson differences while accounting for temporal dependencies. Fourth, our sample consisted primarily of college students and was disproportionately female, White, and Latter-day Saint, potentially limiting generalizability to other populations. However, participants were recruited across four universities and represented multiple religious affiliations, providing an initial foundation for replication in more diverse populations.

Future Research

The present study identifies several promising directions for future research. First, future studies should use more frequent assessments to examine whether associations between behavioral engagement, perceived behavioral value, and meaning in life unfold over shorter intervals, such as hours rather than days. Second, future studies should investigate whether the perceived value of a specific behavior predicts subsequent engagement in that same behavior, rather than engagement across multiple behaviors. Third, researchers should examine whether the independent associations of behavioral duration and perceived value with meaning in life extend to other developmental contexts in which individuals find meaning, such as relationships, education, or employment. Finally, future studies should examine whether consistency in specific religious daily routines, rather than consistency in total time spent on religious behaviors day-to-day and week-to-week, predicts subsequent meaning in life.

Conclusions

The present study examined two potential pathways toward meaning in life: time spent engaging in religious behaviors and the perceived value of those behaviors. Using intensive longitudinal data and residual dynamic structural equation modeling, we found that both duration and perceived value were independently associated with greater daily meaning in life. These findings have implications for theory, research, and practice. Theoretically, they suggest that simply perceiving a behavior as valuable, without necessarily identifying the underlying values or goals, may be relevant to meaning in life beyond behavioral engagement itself. For research, these findings highlight the importance of considering not only how much individuals engage in behaviors, but also how much they value those experiences when studying the development and maintenance of meaning in life. Finally, for practitioners, including clinicians and religious leaders, these findings suggest that encouraging religious engagement may be only part of the picture. Helping individuals reflect on which religious experiences have been most valuable to them, and why, may offer another avenue for cultivating meaning in life. Ultimately, the findings suggest that meaning in life may be associated not only with how much religion individuals practice, but also with how much those experiences matter to them.
